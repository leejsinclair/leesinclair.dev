### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> August 2026 revision, six facts in the authors' terms: (1) no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement from AI; (2) employment of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed occupations is 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with less-exposed peers, with no comparable gap for experienced workers; (3) the divergence has widened steadily since August 2025; (4) it operates primarily through reduced hiring rather than increased separations; (5) declines are concentrated where AI usage substitutes for human tasks, while complementary usage goes with flat or rising employment, especially for experienced workers; (6) adjustment is through employment rather than base compensation. New mechanism material: young-worker declines are in codified-knowledge occupations, experienced-worker gains in tacit-knowledge occupations; women are more exposed on average. The six facts are NOT the same list as in 2025. The 2025 versions' six facts were: declines for early-career workers in exposed occupations such as software development and customer support; overall employment still growing but stagnant for the young; declines in automating not augmenting uses; a 13% (later 16%) relative decline after firm-time effects; adjustment in employment more than pay; robustness to remote work and sample choices. The hiring-versus-separations fact and the explicit 'no economy-wide displacement' fact are new in 2026. The 2026 tone on causality is more guarded than 2025's 'consistent with the hypothesis that generative AI has begun to significantly affect entry-level employment'.

- **Sample and method:** 
> Observational, descriptive. Anonymised administrative payroll records from ADP (which serves firms employing over 26 million US workers). Analysis sample is a balanced panel of firms observed monthly from January 2021 to June 2026, 3.5 to 5 million employees a month; an extended panel runs from January 2018. Full-time workers under 70 with positive earnings. Job titles mapped to 2010 SOC codes; titles are missing for roughly 30% of the sample and are imputed from the worker's own history in the 2026 version. Occupations sorted into AI exposure quintiles using Eloundou et al. (2024) GPT-4 beta scores and the Anthropic Economic Index (automative versus augmentative Claude usage), with five alternative measures as checks. Outcomes compared by age band (22-25, 26-30, 31-34, 35-40, 41-49, 50+) and exposure quintile, normalised to November 2022 (October 2022 in the 2025 versions). Supplementary: occupation-level long-difference regressions with controls, within-firm Poisson event studies with firm-time fixed effects, hiring and separation flows, CPS and ACS benchmarks.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> Headline 19%: a raw comparison of headcount indices, no model. 'Reduced hiring rather than separations' rests on Online Appendix Figure B.7 and Appendix K: a hire is a worker at a firm in month t but not t-1, a separation the reverse; both are cumulated over twelve months and divided by the cell's headcount a year earlier, measured January over January on the panel balanced from January 2018, comparing only the most exposed (Q5) and least exposed (Q1) quintiles by age group, shown as change since 2019. Finding: since 2022 both rates fell everywhere ('low hire, low fire'); separation rates for young workers fell at least as much in Q5 as in Q1; the hiring-rate gap between Q5 and Q1 young workers opened after 2022. Ageing out of the band and switching between quintiles were checked and ruled out (footnote 17). No numeric hiring or separation rates are given in the text, only the chart. A 'separation' is leaving the firm for any reason, so quits, layoffs and moves to non-ADP firms are not distinguished. Case for AI: gap kept widening after rates peaked; relative position was back at pre-pandemic level by November 2022; declines load on automating uses with an age gradient; sharpest in states with highest Claude usage (about -19% for the most exposed quintile in leading-adoption states); Census QWI data (Tucker 2026) show the same sign.
- **Data vintage:** 
> Three vintages. 26 August 2025: data through July 2025, headline 13% (regression). 13 November 2025: data through September 2025, headline 16% (regression). 'August 2026' revision released 12 August 2026 (PDF created 12 August 2026): data through June 2026, headline 19% (descriptive kept-pace), with 15% given as the same measure at the July 2025 vintage. The 2026 version also changed the data pipeline (new occupation crosswalk, imputed occupation codes) and the baseline month (November 2022 instead of October 2022). The essay should cite the August 2026 revision and say so.
- **Figure provenance:** 
> 13%: August 2025 abstract and conclusion; a regression estimate (12 log points) on July 2025 data. 16%: November 2025 abstract; same regression (15 log points) on September 2025 data. 15% and 19%: both from the August 2026 revision; a different, simpler measure. The trap: 13 to 16 to 19 is not one series. The like-for-like pairs are 13% to 16% (regression, firm controls) and 15% to 19% (descriptive, no controls). Writing 'the gap widened from 13% to 19%' mixes measures; the authors' own like-for-like statement is 15% to 19%. The regression measure behind 13% and 16% did not grow in the new pipeline: Appendix L puts the most-exposed-quintile within-firm estimate at -6.8 log points as of June 2026, or -5.3 (p = 0.26) under the original filters. Press coverage of the 2025 paper widely repeated '13%' and 'nearly 20% for software developers'; a web search summary of the 2026 release presents 13, 16, 19 as one sequence, which is the conflation to avoid. 'Nearly 20%' for young software developers is a 2025 figure (July and September 2025 data, peak-to-date, raw index) and has no stated successor in the 2026 text.
- **Population and transfer:** 
> Working employees, not students: full-time US workers at ADP client firms, with 'young' meaning ages 22 to 25 (age, not tenure or job level). Covers all occupations; software developers are one case study among many and results hold when computer occupations (SOC 15-1) and tech firms are excluded. Transfers directly to the claim that fewer junior engineers are being hired in the US, with the limits that the sample over-represents larger firms, manufacturing and wholesale, and high-exposure occupations, and conditions on firm survival. It says nothing about skill formation or learning.
- **Outcome measured:** 
> Employment (headcount of worker-firm matches) by age and occupational AI exposure; hiring and separation rates; annual base compensation. Not job postings, not productivity, not skills.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Authors' own, mostly quoted. Causality: 'this work does not estimate a causal impact of AI'. Education: estimates 'attenuate when controlling for occupational education levels, which may reflect either an alternative explanation or the very channel through which AI operates'. Pre-trends: 'more- and less-exposed occupations show some divergent trends that predate ChatGPT, particularly around the COVID-19 pandemic'; 'these patterns cannot be read as causal effects of AI'. External validity: 'the magnitude of the divergence appears specific to the ADP sample, but its direction is consistent' and the magnitudes 'urge caution against extrapolating them to the economy as a whole; a particular concern is that changes in the ADP sample might correspond to reallocation to firms outside the sample in equilibrium'. Selection: 'there may also be selection in which firms use ADP'; balanced panel conditions on firm survival; few firms under 10 employees. Hiring versus separations: pandemic-era differences 'indicate pretrends that warrant caution', and the flows 'are determined in equilibrium'. Pipeline: 'the firm fixed-effects estimates are more sensitive than the raw descriptive results'. Pay: base salary 'excludes bonuses, overtime pay, commissions, equity, and tips'. Forecasting: 'We do not make predictions about future impacts or claim that the trends we document will continue indefinitely.' Firm-level adoption cannot be studied because firm identifiers are anonymised.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Inside the paper: the ACS shows a much smaller and statistically insignificant gap for 2022 to 2024 (-0.022 versus -0.132 in ADP), though the two agree for professional, information and financial services; the CPS is too noisy to say (23 to 48 young software developers sampled a month); the education control halves or more the estimate; the within-firm estimate for the most exposed quintile is no longer significant under the 2025 specification. Outside work the authors cite as finding different results: Lambert and Schindler (2026) find that accounting for work-from-home significantly attenuates the AI-exposure correlation (the authors' replication on ADP data disagrees and attributes the difference to data sets); Frank et al. (2026) document deterioration in AI-exposed jobs predating ChatGPT; Iscenko and Curto Millet (2026) and others raise interest rates; Humlum and Vestergaard (2025), Gimbel et al. (2025) and Eckhardt and Goldschlag (2025) are cited among studies 'finding a range of results'. These outside papers were not opened for this note.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> Named by the authors: interest-rate rises from March 2022; unwinding of pandemic-era over-hiring in tech and knowledge work; the 2022 change to Section 174 R&D expensing, which raised the cost of software development; remote work and outsourcing; COVID-19 disruption to education and skill formation; shocks to college-educated labour markets generally (the education control); a general 'low hire, low fire' market that hits exposed young workers harder because their entry depends most on hiring and they enter at 22 or later (footnote 18); reallocation of young workers to firms outside the ADP sample; composition of ADP clients. The authors argue none is sufficient alone, and list 'AI, with COVID-19 education disruptions and work-from-home as other plausible contributors'.
- **Verification status:** 
> Checked against the primary source opened (August 2026 PDF and the 12 August 2026 news post): 19% wording and its construction; 15% at July 2025 vintage; the sentence attributing 13% and 16% to July and September 2025 data; data through June 2026; panel size; the Fact 4 hiring versus separations text and Appendix K definitions; causality statements; ACS comparison; education control numbers; Appendix L within-firm numbers; absence of any numeric software-developer figure in the 2026 text (searched the full extracted text). Checked against Wayback snapshots of the 2025 PDFs: 13 percent abstract wording (26 August 2025), 16% abstract wording (13 November 2025), data through July 2025 and September 2025, 'nearly 20%' for software developers in both, 6% decline, 12 and 15 log points. Not checked: values in Figure B.7 and Figure B.3 (charts not read), the Canaries Dashboard, and any of the cited third-party papers (Tucker 2026, Lambert and Schindler 2026, Frank et al. 2026).

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> The strongest current evidence for 'fewer junior hires', and it supports the specific framing that the entry point is narrowing rather than existing juniors being laid off. It also supplies the mechanism the essay needs: AI substitutes for codified knowledge taught in formal education while experienced workers' tacit knowledge gains value, so the rung where tacit knowledge used to be acquired is the one being removed. It is silent on learning with AI and on training.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> None directly; the paper does not study training. Indirect only: the codified versus tacit finding (tacit knowledge 'acquired through practice, mentorship, and repeated exposure to real situations', per the news post) suggests in-house training for the juniors a firm does hire should target experience-based judgement rather than the documented, checkable knowledge AI already reproduces. The earlier November 2025 version also noted 'inefficient incentives to train entry-level workers who may move firms' as a reason firms cut entry-level hiring. Both points are the authors' suggestive framing, not tested findings.
- **Open questions:** 
> How far young software developer employment has fallen as of June 2026 (no number in the 2026 text; would need reading Fig. B.3 or the dashboard). Actual hiring and separation rates behind Fact 4 (chart only). How much of the gap is caused by AI. Whether the pattern holds outside ADP clients given the ACS discrepancy. Whether the missing hires are postponed, moved to non-ADP firms or gone. What the monthly Canaries Dashboard shows after June 2026.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- A (Stanford authors' reply). Two claims. (1) Interest rates are not a good explanation for the disproportionate decline: using Zens et al. (2020) estimates, more AI-exposed occupations are on average less interest-rate sensitive, and the most AI-exposed occupations show larger early-career declines within both the below-median and above-median interest-rate-exposure groups. (2) A concession on timing: with firm-time fixed effects, the relative decline for the most-exposed 22 to 25 year olds 'starts becoming notable in 2024 (not late 2022 or 2023)', so the earlier part of the raw decline is at least partly due to other factors. They restate that the result is not causal and that AI is not the sole determinant, naming young software developers as a case with 'various factors'.
- B (Humlum and Vestergaard, Denmark). Among 11 AI-exposed occupations, including software developers, chatbot adoption is widespread but has produced no detectable change in earnings or recorded hours for workers or workplaces. The revised paper adds a direct test of the Canaries pattern: Denmark shows the same aggregate fall in early-career (age 22 to 25) employment in exposed occupations, but workplaces that encourage chatbot use show no different change in their early-career employment share from those that do not. Their reading is that firms' actual adoption is not what is driving the fall. What does change is task content and occupational mobility.
- C (Yale Budget Lab). C1: the economy-wide occupational mix has not shifted faster since ChatGPT than in earlier technology transitions in a way that can be attributed to AI, and the share of workers in high-exposure occupations is stable. On young graduates it reports a slight recent rise in dissimilarity that 'could be consistent with' Canaries or with a slowing labour market. C2 (May 2026): a synthetic difference-in-differences comparison of exposed and unexposed occupations finds 'no statistically or economically significant effects as of yet' on employment share or wages. It explicitly says CPS data are underpowered for the narrow early-career group.
- D1 (Iscenko and Curto Millet, Google, published by EIG). The Canaries pattern is better explained by the 2022 monetary tightening than by AI. Job postings in the most AI-exposed quintile peaked in March to April 2022, before ChatGPT; a hiring freeze mechanically shrinks a narrow 22 to 25 age band because that band is restocked only by new hires; AI exposure and interest-rate sensitivity are correlated through sector mix; junior and senior postings fell roughly in parallel.
- D2 (Eckhardt and Goldschlag, EIG). Across CPS-based measures (unemployment, labour-force exit, occupation switching, industry employment) and five AI-exposure measures, no meaningful AI effect is visible. Unemployment rose more for the least exposed workers than the most exposed, and unemployment has risen for young workers and recent graduates whether AI-exposed or not.
- How these qualify the Canaries result, taken together: nobody disputes that early-career employment in AI-exposed occupations has fallen (Humlum and Vestergaard replicate it in Denmark). The dispute is attribution. The Stanford authors themselves now date the part that survives firm-level controls to 2024 onward and say the earlier decline is partly other factors; critics offer interest rates, pre-existing trends and remote work; and none of the null studies has the power or the data to rule out an effect confined to 22 to 25 year olds in the US.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- A: 'we can't be sure of the causality from this sort of analysis'; 'the available interest rate data do not have as much occupational granularity as the AI exposure data'; 'the Zens et al. (2020) estimates do not condition on age, and young workers may potentially have different occupational interest rate exposure than the workforce as a whole'; 'Since we don't have an experiment where we can compare a world with AI to one without, this will remain an active area'.
- B2: occupation-level 'Confidence bands are again wide for these occupation-specific estimates and should therefore be interpreted with caution'; the paper frames nulls as early ('the absence of measurable labor market effects is not evidence that nothing is happening'); time savings are self-reported; B1 notes 'We do not have sufficient statistical power to detect comparable pass-through effects in our difference-in-differences' design and asks that pass-through rates be read 'with some caution'. Survey respondents are, by construction, employed in the occupation at survey time.
- C1: 'our analysis is not predictive of the future'; results on recent graduates 'should be interpreted with caution particularly given small sample sizes'; OpenAI exposure 'is not based on actual usage'; 'data from Claude usage alone is not representative'; 'Better data is needed'.
- C2: exposure metrics 'are ultimately informed guesses'; averaging across an exposed group 'could actually hide effects'; the design cannot separate AI from 'other events, like the rise in remote work, that are closely correlated with exposure to AI'; CPS is 'somewhat underpowered for subgroup analysis, like the 22–27 year old recent college graduates'; 'LLMs are constantly improving, such that the economic impacts from early models seem likely to be smaller than those of recent models'.
- D1: 'absence of evidence is not the same as evidence of absence'; 'We are still in the early innings of the AI transformation'; 'many of the key intellectual questions here are still to be settled conclusively'. The essay states no limitations of its own postings data or of the interest-rate argument.
- D2: 'The lack of causal evidence is a problem ... for all of our analyses presented here'; the exposure measure 'is agnostic as to whether AI is a complement or substitute to workers'; new entrants 'are a small portion of the labor force' and 'may not show up in this industry-level analysis'.
- **Counter evidence:** 
- Against the null findings: the Stanford note reports the fixed-effects decline for exposed 22 to 25 year olds is 'growing' and has 'not shown a reversal' as of October 2025, i.e. it survives controls for firm-wide shocks, which a pure interest-rate story acting at firm level would not predict.
- Against D1's interest-rate mechanism: the Stanford note finds AI exposure and interest-rate sensitivity negatively correlated using the same Zens et al. (2020) source D1 cites, and the Budget Lab (C2) finds, on pre-pandemic data, that AI-exposed occupations are 'generally less sensitive to the business cycle than unexposed occupations'. C2 adds that this did not hold in the post-2022 period.
- Against the Canaries AI reading: Humlum and Vestergaard find the same early-career fall in Denmark but no difference between adopting and non-adopting workplaces; D1 shows postings in exposed occupations peaked in spring 2022; B3 cites Frank et al. (2026, 'AI-Exposed Jobs Deteriorated before ChatGPT') and Lambert and Schindler (2026, remote work) for pre-existing trends. Those two papers were not opened.
- Against using Humlum and Vestergaard as a rebuttal of Canaries: their own text allows 'anticipatory hiring freezes unrelated to actual adoption' as a driver. A firm that stops hiring juniors because it expects AI to do the work, without yet encouraging chatbot use, would show up as a null in their design. Their data also end in December 2024, the year the Stanford authors say the controlled effect begins.
- Against D2 as a rebuttal: it predates Canaries, uses unemployment rather than employment counts, and on the two LLM-based exposure measures it does find slightly higher relative unemployment for exposed workers.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Monetary tightening from March 2022 hitting rate-sensitive sectors where AI-exposed jobs cluster (D1). Disputed by the Stanford note.
- Mechanical cohort ageing: any broad hiring freeze shrinks a narrow 22 to 25 band faster than older bands, with no layoffs and no targeting of juniors (D1). This is an argument about the measure, and it applies to any cause of reduced hiring, including AI.
- Pre-existing decline: postings in exposed occupations peaked March to April 2022 and occupational-mix shifts were 'well on their way during 2021' (D1, C1).
- Post-pandemic over-hiring and correction in tech and information sectors (raised in D2 for publishing and data processing).
- Remote work, which is correlated with AI exposure (C2 citing Kolko 2026; B3 citing Lambert and Schindler 2026).
- Young workers always bear downturns: the job ladder is procyclical, more so for the young (D1 citing Haltiwanger, Hyatt and McEntarfer 2018).
- Anticipation: firms freezing junior hiring in expectation of AI, ahead of real adoption (Humlum and Vestergaard, section 3.4). This would still be an AI effect on entry-level hiring, but one based on belief, not on demonstrated substitution.
- Offsetting effects inside the 'exposed' group: some exposed occupations expand and some contract, averaging to zero (C2).

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This item bounds the first of the essay's two findings. The safe statement is that employment of 22 to 25 year olds in AI-exposed jobs, software developers included, has fallen in US payroll data and in Danish register data, and that economists disagree on how much of that is AI. The Stanford authors call their own result suggestive and not causal, and now place the part that survives firm-level controls in 2024 and after. An essay that says 'AI is why juniors are not being hired' overstates the evidence. An essay that says 'fewer juniors are being hired into these roles, whatever the mix of causes' is supported, and the leadership question (who will be senior in five years) does not depend on settling the cause. It has no bearing on whether learning with AI weakens comprehension.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> None directly: no source here tests any training format, frequency or feedback method. Three indirect points. First, Humlum and Vestergaard find employer initiatives matter: in the May 2025 version, 30% of employees had received training, and encouragement nearly doubled take-up (47% to 83%); the revised paper reports that use and reported benefits peak where encouragement is combined with enterprise tools and training. That is evidence that organised employer effort changes how AI is used, not that it improves skill. Second, they find AI is absorbed through task reorganisation (new tasks in content generation, AI oversight and AI integration) before pay or hours move, which suggests what juniors need to learn is shifting toward review and oversight. Third, the Google/EIG essay itself says on-the-job training will need rethinking 'especially should AI take over tasks which were previously seen as formative', which is the essay's premise stated by authors otherwise sceptical of the displacement story.
- **Open questions:** 
- How much of the early-career decline is AI, interest rates, remote work or post-pandemic correction. No source separates them, and the Stanford authors say no experiment exists.
- Whether firms are cutting junior hiring in anticipation of AI without having adopted it, which the Danish design cannot detect.
- Whether the Danish null still holds after December 2024, once coding agents became widespread.
- Whether the Canaries decline is specific to software developers once firm-time effects are included; the note gives no developer-specific number.
- What the Stanford authors' August 2026 update does to the timing and interest-rate arguments made in the February 2026 note [uncertain: not examined here].
- Whether the Budget Lab's later 2026 tracker updates changed its 'no clear footprint' reading [uncertain: only the 16 July 2026 takeaways were read].

- **Key claims:** 
- A: In Current Population Survey data, employment falls with AI exposure across occupations only for young workers (ages 20 to 24). Other age and exposure groups have risen or held roughly steady.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- A: 'this pattern may not be causal and could be driven by some other factor correlated with AI exposure, such as education.'
- A: Small samples. Partitioning by age and exposure 'can result in relatively few observations in a given age-by-AI-exposure group, leading to noisy estimates', hence the 12-month moving averages.
- A: CPS records an unemployed person's most recent occupation, not the one they are seeking, so AI-driven unemployment among would-be entrants 'may not be visible in unemployment rates by occupation'. The article's example is a computer science graduate who never finds a programming job.
- A: The section heading itself reads 'AI impact small and uncertain, but could grow'.
- B: The codified versus tacit explanation is introduced as Brynjolfsson and coauthors 'speculating'.
- B: 'considerable variation exists' in the relationship between experience premium and AI exposure.
- B: The median-premium estimate's 90 percent confidence interval 'clearly includes zero'; the high-premium effect is 'likely even a positive effect', not a firm finding.
- B: Chart 3 shows 'no relationship between an occupation's AI exposure and post-2022 wage growth' before the interaction term is added.
- B states no explicit limitations section and does not discuss causality directly.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Education or another trait correlated with AI exposure (named by Atkinson and Yamco).
- Cyclical weakness and the unwinding of the 2022 post-pandemic hiring surge. A says the entrant pattern is not typical of past cycles but shows this only descriptively.
- Interest rates and the end of cheap capital hitting technology and professional services hiring from 2022, which lines up in time with ChatGPT's release.
- Composition effects in B: if a sector sheds lower-paid junior staff, its average weekly wage rises mechanically even if no individual is paid more. That would produce 'employment down, wages up' without AI complementing anyone's tacit knowledge. Davis does not address this.
- The experience premium may proxy for things other than tacit knowledge: licensing, credentials, seniority pay scales, or general occupational skill level. High-premium occupations (lawyers, underwriters, analysts) are also high-wage, high-education occupations that saw strong demand for other reasons.
- Occupations with steep wage ladders may simply be the cognitive, text-heavy occupations that any language-model exposure index scores highly, so the premium and exposure correlation may be built in.
- Different exposure indices: A uses Eloundou et al., B uses Felten, Raj and Seamans. The two pieces are not measuring exposure the same way.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Strong support for the 'fewer junior hires' leg, with a useful refinement: the mechanism is fewer people being hired in, not juniors being laid off, and the aggregate effect so far is small. Davis supplies the 'who will be senior in five years' framing from an institutional source: experience is worth more, the entry-level route to getting it is becoming uneconomic for firms in the short run, and that is unsustainable in the long run. Neither piece says anything about learning with AI or about training.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> None directly. Neither source studies training, and Davis stops at saying firms must rethink 'how entry-level employees gain experience on the job'. By inference only: if what is scarce is tacit knowledge that used to accumulate as a by-product of doing codifiable entry-level tasks, then training has to create that accumulation on purpose, and exercises should target judgement in the firm's own systems (why this code is the way it is) more than textbook knowledge AI already supplies. That inference is the essay's, not the Dallas Fed's, and should be worded as such.
- **Open questions:** 
> Whether the pattern is causal. What the 16.4 and 15.5 percent shares are shares of. Whether the wage rise in computer systems design is a composition effect. Whether the experience premium measures tacit knowledge at all. Whether any of this holds for software engineering occupations specifically, since neither piece breaks them out. Whether data after September 2025 continue the trend. Whether tacit knowledge stays out of AI's reach, which Davis assumes and does not test.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Using AI assistance to complete a task that requires learning a new library reduced measured skill formation: the AI group scored lower on an immediate quiz (statistically significant).
- AI assistance did not deliver a statistically significant speed-up on this learning task. The paper: 'Our results do not conclusively find a speed up or slow down'.
- The largest score gap was on debugging questions and the smallest on code reading (exploratory, not pre-registered; magnitudes are shown only in a chart, Figure 8, not stated numerically).
- How the AI was used mattered. Six interaction patterns were identified among AI-group participants; three (heavy delegation or AI-led debugging) averaged under 40% on the quiz and three (which kept the participant cognitively engaged) averaged 65% or higher.
- The authors' proposed mechanism: the control group hit more errors, especially Trio-specific ones, and resolving them independently built debugging skill. This is offered as a hypothesis ('Our hypothesis is that...').
- The control group reported higher self-reported learning; the AI group found the task easier, and both found the quiz similarly hard.
- Authors expect the effect to be larger with agentic or autocomplete tools, calling the chat setup 'a lower bound for cognitive offloading'. This is an expectation, not something the study tested.

- **Limitations stated:**

- Task length (paper 7.1): 'Ideally, skill formation takes place over months to years. We measured skill formation for a specific Python library over a one-hour period.'
- Participant realism (paper 7.1): 'there was not the same incentive to learn the library as if it were required for their actual job.'
- Prompting skills (paper 7.1): 'We collect self-reported familiarity with AI coding tools, but we do not actually measure differences in prompting techniques.'
- Evaluation design (paper 7.1): skill formation measured by a single quiz; other studies could use a follow-on task.
- Human assistance (paper 7.1): 'We do not include the counterfactual of how skill formation would be impacted by receiving assistance from humans.'
- Interaction patterns are not causal (post): 'Our qualitative analysis does not draw a causal link between interaction patterns and learning outcomes'. Paper: 'each behavior pattern cluster is small'.
- The question-type breakdown, including the debugging gap, is 'exploratory data analysis (not pre-registered)'.
- Post describes the results as 'preliminary' and the study as 'only a first step'.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Time on task and exposure, not AI as such: the control group typed more code and met more Trio-specific errors, so the quiz may partly reward having hit those exact errors. The authors say Trio errors 'mapped directly to topics tested on the evaluation'.
- Selection into patterns: participants chose how to use the AI. More motivated or more able people may both ask conceptual questions and score well, so the pattern differences may reflect the person, not the method.
- Instruction effect: everyone was told to work as fast as possible for a flat fee, which pushes AI users toward delegation. Under a 'learn this' instruction the gap could be smaller.
- Short-horizon measure: an immediate quiz may capture familiarity from recent struggle rather than durable skill; the gap could shrink or grow with delay.
- Low power on speed: with n = 52 and a control group in which 4 people did not finish in time (time censored at 35 minutes), a real speed advantage could be missed or understated.
- Unexplained arithmetic: the 4.15-point raw gap on 27 points is 15.4%, not 17%, so the headline percentage depends on how scores were converted.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> The strongest randomised evidence for 'weaker learning with AI': a 17-point lower immediate quiz score with no significant speed gain, and the worst gap on debugging, the skill needed to supervise AI output. It also supports in-house training as a response, because the result turned on how people used the tool. It says nothing about hiring volumes. Do not call the participants junior engineers without qualification.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> Suggests exercises where the learner writes and debugs code without the AI producing the answer, since independent error resolution is the proposed mechanism. If AI is allowed, steer it toward conceptual questions and explanations and away from generating or fixing the code (the three high-scoring patterns). Conceptual Inquiry cost little time (22 min against 19.5 for full delegation). Debugging practice deserves particular weight. Speed pressure appears to push people toward delegation, so practice needs protected, low-stakes time. On frequency, spacing and delayed retention the study has nothing: it is one session with one immediate quiz. On using AI to generate exercises: none.

> Does the gap persist after days or weeks? Does it hold for genuinely junior engineers (only 4 participants had 1 to 3 years)? Is it larger with agentic tools, as the authors expect but did not test? How does AI help compare with help from a human? Why does 4.15/27 come out as 17%? Where is the 25th analysed AI participant in the six patterns? What are the numeric per-question-type gaps? When was the data collected?

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Prather et al. (2024): Generative AI does not remove the metacognitive difficulties novices already had. All five previously documented difficulties (Forming, Assumption, Dislodging, Location, Achievement) were still observed, and three new AI-specific ones appeared: Interruption (constant inline suggestions break the student's thinking), Mislead (the tool steers the student down a wrong path) and Progression (the student finishes without understanding the constructs used). Students with better grades and higher self-efficacy used the tools to accelerate; weaker students struggled yet believed they had done well. The authors say the tools 'could be widening the digital divide' and that task completion hides the problem.
- Bassner et al. (TUM, 2026): In a three-arm RCT, both a scaffolded hint-only tutor (Iris) and unrestricted ChatGPT raised exercise scores well above a no-AI control, but neither produced greater pre-to-post knowledge gains or better code comprehension. Both AI arms reported lower frustration and lower extraneous and germane cognitive load. Only the scaffolded tutor raised intrinsic motivation. Students preferred ChatGPT, which the authors call a 'comfort trap'. Note the direction: this is a null on learning, not a measured harm. The AI arms did not learn less than control; they learned the same while scoring higher.
- Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025): Unrestricted GPT-4 access during maths practice improved practice scores but lowered scores on a later unassisted exam relative to students who never had it. A guard-railed tutor (teacher-supplied solutions and common mistakes, hints instead of answers) improved practice scores more and left exam scores statistically indistinguishable from control. So guardrails removed the harm but produced no learning gain. Students did not notice: the GPT Base arm did not perceive learning less, and the GPT Tutor arm believed they did better than they did. The authors attribute the harm to students using the tool as a 'crutch' (asking for the answer and copying it), not to being misled by the model's errors.
- Kazemitabaar et al. (CHI 2023): For 10 to 17 year olds learning Python from scratch, access to a Codex code generator during training raised authoring performance and did not reduce performance on manual code-modification tasks. On post-tests without the tool, the Codex group did no worse one day later and scored somewhat higher one week later, though not significantly. Learners with higher prior (Scratch) competency benefited significantly on retention; low-prior learners were neither helped nor harmed. This is the main counter-example to a simple 'AI harms novice learning' claim, and the study design itself was scaffolded (see alternative_explanations).

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Prather: 'One limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size, with only 21 participants'. Only two tools were studied (Copilot and ChatGPT), and 'we were limited by the single site of the experiment'. In the conclusions: 'our sample size was too small to tell if prior programming experience, socio-economic background, race, or gender are factors in GenAI harming novice programmer experience'.
- Bassner (TUM): limitations section not read (full text inaccessible). The abstract itself hedges with 'In this setting'.
- Bastani: 'our study focuses on two generative AI tutors for a single topic (mathematics) deployed in a single high school in Turkey.' 'Our deployment was also carried out in Fall 2023, when generative AI was still very new; modes of interaction may have changed now that users are more familiar with effective uses of these tools and their shortcomings.' 'both closed and open-weight models have significantly improved in performance since then.' 'we focus on short-term outcomes due to limitations imposed by our partner school; studying long-term outcomes is a key direction for future research.'
- Kazemitabaar: correctness scores depend on the authors' own rubric; fixed topic order means effects of topic difficulty and of growing familiarity with the tool cannot be separated; non-significant differences may reflect sample size; post-tests 'did not leave the boundaries of what learners were trained on', so transfer was not tested; only novices outside formal classrooms were studied, and the authors call for studies at high school or university level; qualitative analysis of how learners actually used the generator was left for future work.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> The four studies do not agree on a harm. Kazemitabaar found no learning penalty and a non-significant advantage at one week, with a significant benefit for higher-prior learners. Bassner found no difference in learning between either AI arm and control: unrestricted ChatGPT did not measurably reduce learning in that single session. Only Bastani shows a statistically significant learning loss from unrestricted AI, and that is in school mathematics with a 2023 model that was wrong about half the time. Prather reports no learning outcome at all, and roughly half of his participants used the tools well. On guardrails, the evidence is weaker than commonly stated: in Bastani the tutor removed the harm but showed no gain over no AI, and in Bassner the hint-only tutor matched but did not beat both ChatGPT and control on learning. Neither study shows that tutor-style AI improves learning; they show it avoids a loss (Bastani) or improves motivation (Bassner).
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> Bastani: students had no reason to learn during the assisted phase if copying the answer earned the practice grade; the harm may reflect incentives and an answer-giving interface more than AI as such. GPT Base's 49% error rate could also have misled students, though the authors test and largely reject this. The exam followed immediately, so the result may be lost practice time, not durable harm. Bassner: one 90-minute session on one hard topic may be too short for any condition to produce differential learning, so a null is expected; the ChatGPT arm may have learned as much because they still had to read and integrate code. Kazemitabaar: the design was itself a guardrail. Each AI-assisted authoring task was followed by a manual modification task on the generated code, the tool was unavailable for modification, and tasks were small and sequenced. The absence of harm may be credit to that structure, not to Codex. The benefit for high-prior learners is consistent with expertise-dependent effects (stronger learners use generated code as a worked example). Prather: the gap between strong and weak students existed before AI (the original 2018-era study found the same five difficulties); without a no-AI comparison group the study cannot show AI widened it, only that it did not close it and added new failure modes. Participants also knew they were observed and were thinking aloud.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Supports the 'weaker learning with AI' strand, with qualifications. It gives independent support for the Anthropic trial's core pattern: AI raises output while understanding does not rise with it, and learners do not notice (Prather's 'illusion of competence', Bastani's over-optimistic self-reports, Bassner's 'comfort trap'). It also cautions against overstating it: only one of the four studies found an actual loss, one found no loss with a slight non-significant gain, and none studied working engineers. For the training proposal, the useful point is that structure around the AI matters more than the AI itself. Says nothing about hiring.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> 1) Completion is not evidence of understanding. All four studies show high task success with AI alongside flat or unknown learning, so a training programme needs an unassisted check (Bastani's closed-book exam, Kazemitabaar's no-AI post-tests) or it will measure the tool. 2) Do not rely on self-report. Learners rated themselves well when they had not learned (Prather, Bastani) and preferred the less educational tool (Bassner). 3) Follow generated code with a manual task on that same code. Kazemitabaar's generate-then-modify structure is the one design here with a retention result that was not negative, and the authors suggest gating use of generated code behind 'a Parsons problem that is automatically produced from the AI-generated code' or a multiple-choice question on its concepts. That is close to Lee's small-challenge idea and to codebase treasure hunts. 4) Hint-first AI is a reasonable default but should be sold modestly: it avoided the harm (Bastani) and helped motivation (Bassner) without producing more learning than no AI. Bastani's tutor also depended on human-written solutions and common mistakes, which bears on whether AI-generated exercises can go unchecked. 5) Expect weaker juniors to need more scaffolding. Prather's strugglers accepted more suggestions (34.1% vs 24.5%) and Kazemitabaar's gains went to those with stronger prior knowledge. 6) On frequency: Kazemitabaar's programme was many short tasks across ten sessions with a one-week retention check, which is the only evidence here that resembles 'small and regular'. Nothing in these four tests frequency or spacing directly.
- **Open questions:** 
> The TUM paper's effect sizes, per-arm numbers, exclusions and stated limitations (full text not obtained). Whether any of these effects last beyond a week: three of four have no delayed test. Whether guard-railed AI can improve learning over no AI, as opposed to merely not harming it. Whether findings from children, school maths and first-year students hold for employed junior engineers working in real codebases. Whether Bastani's result would replicate with current models and students who are now used to the tools. Whether Prather's 'widening gap' is caused by AI, given no comparison group.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Roediger and Karpicke 2006: taking a recall test on a prose passage produces better retention after two days and one week than spending the same time re-reading it, even with no feedback on the test. Re-reading wins only on a test five minutes later. The benefit is therefore not just re-exposure. Students who re-read were more confident they would remember, and remembered least.
- Cepeda et al. 2006 (meta-analysis): across the verbal recall literature, spacing two study episodes apart beats massing them at every retention interval examined, with study time held equal. The best gap between sessions grows as the time you need to remember grows. The authors say the literature cannot yet give a precise optimal gap.
- Cepeda et al. 2008: in a large randomised experiment with delays up to a year, final recall first rises and then slowly falls as the gap between two study sessions widens. The best gap gets longer as the test delay gets longer, but shrinks as a share of it (roughly 20 to 40 percent of a one-week delay, 5 to 10 percent of a one-year delay). Too short a gap costs much more than too long a gap.
- Bjork and Bjork: performance during training is an unreliable guide to learning. Conditions that make practice feel fluent (massing, blocking, re-reading, fixed conditions) often produce weaker long-term retention and transfer than conditions that feel harder (spacing, interleaving, variation, generating or retrieving answers). The difficulty is only 'desirable' if the learner has the background knowledge to respond to it successfully.
- Kalyuga et al. 2003: guidance that helps novices (worked examples, integrated explanations) can stop helping and then hurt as learners gain expertise, because it becomes redundant and takes up working memory. Novices learn more from worked examples than from solving problems unaided; with experience the advantage disappears and then reverses. Guidance should be faded gradually, not removed in one step.
- **Headline figures:** 
- Roediger and Karpicke 2006, Experiment 1 (n = 120, one re-study versus one test). Proportion of idea units recalled on the final test: after 5 minutes, re-study 81% versus test 75% (d = 0.52, favouring re-study); after 2 days, test 68% versus re-study 54% (d = 0.95); after 1 week, test 56% versus re-study 42% (d = 0.83). The one-week gap is 14 percentage points.

After 5 minutes: SSSS 83%, SSST 78%, STTT 71%. After 1 week: STTT 61%, SSST 56%, SSSS 40%. STTT versus SSSS at one week: d = 1.26, a gap of 21 percentage points. SSST versus SSSS at one week: d = 0.82. STTT versus SSST at one week was 'marginal', d = 0.31.
- Roediger and Karpicke 2006, Experiment 2: proportional forgetting over one week was 52% for SSSS, 28% for SSST and 14% for STTT. The SSSS group read the passage 14.2 times in total, the STTT group 3.4 times.
- Roediger and Karpicke 2006, Experiment 2: predicted memory in one week on a 7-point scale was 4.8 for SSSS, 4.2 for SSST and 4.0 for STTT. The group that predicted best performed worst.

> Roediger and Karpicke 2006: two randomised laboratory experiments with Washington University undergraduates aged 18 to 24, taking part for course credit. Bjork and Bjork: a short narrative overview of other people's experiments and their own, no new data. Kalyuga et al. 2003: narrative review of cognitive load experiments, many from the authors' own group, including work with mechanical trade apprentices and trainees.

> Roediger and Karpicke: a crossover interaction between learning condition and delay, replicated across two experiments, with large standardised effects at one week and the re-study control deliberately favoured by giving it full re-exposure to the text. Cepeda 2006: accuracy differences pooled over hundreds of comparisons, plus a separate look at the few multi-day studies after sorting those with a relearning confound from those without. Cepeda 2008: random assignment to 26 cells, a prior 150-person lab study with the same pattern, and a fitted retention surface (R squared = .98). Bjork and Bjork: a theory (storage strength versus retrieval strength) and a set of cited experiments on variation, spacing, interleaving and testing. Kalyuga: a series of experiments in which the same instructional comparison gave opposite results for novices and for more experienced learners, explained by working memory limits.

- **Figure provenance:** 
> Every Roediger and Karpicke, Cepeda 2006 and Cepeda 2008 figure above was read from the paper itself. Points where versions in circulation go wrong: (1) Roediger and Karpicke is often summarised as 'testing beats studying', dropping the fact that re-study won at 5 minutes. The result is a crossover, not a uniform advantage. (2) The popular '61% versus 40%' figure is Experiment 2, three tests versus four study periods, at one week. The single-test comparison in Experiment 1 is 56% versus 42%. - **Population and transfer:** 
> None of the five studied software engineers or programming. Roediger and Karpicke: undergraduates recalling short prose passages. Cepeda 2006: mostly laboratory verbal recall (word lists, paired associates). Cepeda 2008: adult internet volunteers memorising trivia facts. It is strongest for the factual and conceptual layer of the job (what a module does, why a decision was made, how a library behaves) and weakest for complex problem solving, where the testing effect is disputed (see counter_evidence). Kalyuga's result transfers in principle, since it is about the novice-to-experienced shift itself.

> Roediger and Karpicke: delayed retention (free recall of idea units at 5 minutes, 2 days, 1 week), plus predicted retention. Cepeda 2006: final-test recall accuracy at retention intervals from seconds to years. Cepeda 2008: delayed recall and recognition at 7 to 350 days. Bjork and Bjork: long-term retention and transfer, as reported in the studies they cite. Kalyuga: post-instruction test performance and rated mental load. None measured on-the-job performance.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Roediger and Karpicke: 'The boundary conditions for the testing effect are not yet known'. They expect strong effects when tests 'occur relatively soon after learning and permit relatively high levels of performance', and limit their educational claim with 'at least in fact-based courses'. Initial recall in their experiments was about 70%, so the tests were mostly passed.
- Cepeda 2006: 'it is unfortunate that we cannot say with certainty how long the ISI should be to optimize long-term retention.' Also: 'new studies are sorely needed to clarify the effects of interstudy and retention intervals that are educationally relevant, that is, on the order of weeks, months, or years.' They note the file drawer problem, that variance data were missing from most studies so accuracy differences were used in place of effect sizes, and that 'there is insufficient data to say whether' expanding intervals help.
- Cepeda 2006 scope: verbal recall tasks only. The paper notes an earlier review (Donovan and Radosevich 1999) found that for skill tasks longer lags produced smaller effects.
- Cepeda 2008: 'The optimally efficient gap between study sessions is not some absolute quantity that can be recommended, but rather depends dramatically on the RI'. On the fitted function: 'we do not contend that this function offers a uniquely accurate characterization of the surface'. Data were collected online, with more dropout at longer delays; the appendix argues this did not bias results.
- Bjork and Bjork: difficulties become 'undesirable' when 'the learner does not have the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully'. They also say 'The effects of distributed practice on learning are complex.' Asked about real-world impact: 'Overall, the impact has been slight.'
- Kalyuga: a review that argues for its own theory; the conclusion states the effect has 'a plausible theoretical explanation within a cognitive load framework'. I found no explicit limitations section in the parts I read.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> Re-exposure: the simplest reading of a testing effect is that a test is a second look at the material. Roediger and Karpicke designed against this (the re-study group saw 100% of the text again, the test group only what it could recall) and the effect survived. Time on task: both spacing papers equate study time, so extra time is not the explanation there. Motivation and incentives: in classrooms, regular quizzes may work partly because people study more when they expect to be tested. Roediger and Karpicke say this themselves ('they will study more and will space their studying'), and Yang et al. list motivation among three supported accounts. That matters for a workplace scheme, where the effect may come partly from attention and expectation, not retrieval alone. Transfer-appropriate processing: a final recall test resembles a practice recall test, so some of the benefit may be practice at the test format. This predicts weaker gains when the real task (debugging, design) looks unlike the quiz. Selection in Cepeda 2008: dropout rose with delay; the authors report completers and non-completers did not differ on initial knowledge or demographics.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the evidence base for the proposed response (continuous in-house training through small, regular challenges). It supports the mechanism: being made to retrieve or work something out beats re-reading it, spread-out practice beats a block, and the benefit shows up later, not on the day. It also explains the Anthropic trial's pattern in older terms. Bjork and Bjork's line about looking up an answer you could have generated is close to a description of asking an AI for the code. What it does not supply is direct evidence about engineers, codebases or workplace outcomes. The essay can say the proposal is consistent with well-replicated learning science. It cannot say that science has tested it.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> Format: make the engineer produce an answer before seeing one (predict what this function returns, find where this value is set, explain why this test fails). Recall and generation formats beat recognition, so prefer open questions over multiple choice (Rowland abstract; Roediger and Karpicke used free recall). A treasure hunt through the codebase fits this if the hunter has to find and state the answer unaided. Frequency: little and often, not an onboarding week. Cepeda 2006 puts the floor at a day or more between sessions. Cepeda 2008 says the right gap depends on how long the knowledge must last: about three to four weeks between visits to the same material if the aim is to keep it for a year, and erring long costs less than erring short. So weekly challenges are sound, provided topics come back a few weeks later instead of being covered once. Difficulty: pitch it so most attempts succeed. Roediger and Karpicke's tests ran at about 70% success, and they expected strong effects when tests 'permit relatively high levels of performance'. Bjork and Bjork say a difficulty the learner lacks the background to meet is not desirable. Kalyuga adds that true novices learn more from a worked example than from an unaided problem, and that support should be faded gradually. For a new junior that means walkthroughs first, then partly completed tasks, then open challenges. Feedback: give the right answer afterwards. The classroom meta-analysis lists corrective feedback as a moderator, and Cepeda 2008's review sessions were test-then-answer. Stakes and measurement: keep it low-stakes, and judge the scheme by a delayed check, not by how the session felt. Both Roediger and Karpicke and Bjork and Bjork show learners rate the weaker method as the better one. Expect people to say the challenges feel less productive than reading the docs. Use of AI: none of these papers addresses it. My inference only: AI generating the questions is compatible with all of the above, while AI supplying the answer at the moment of difficulty removes the retrieval or generation step that the effect depends on.
- **Open questions:** 
> Whether retrieval practice helps with complex, interacting material such as real code, given the unresolved van Gog and Sweller versus Karpicke and Aue dispute. Whether any of this has been tested on working engineers (nothing found in these sources). Whether spacing results from fact recall hold for skills; Cepeda 2006 covers verbal recall only and notes skill tasks behaved differently in an earlier review. The exact pooled effect sizes in Rowland 2014 and Adesope et al. 2017. The original wording of Bjork 1994. The correct publication details for the Bjork and Bjork chapter.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Lehtinen, Seppälä, Korhonen (2023): students can submit a working program and still fail simple, automatically generated multiple-choice questions about that same program. On a second programming course (students had already passed a Python CS1), 27% failed at least one of two or three such questions about their own rainfall solution. The failure rate was about the same as earlier studies found in the first weeks of a first course, which the authors call surprising. Students who got the execution question (Q2) or the purpose question (Q3) wrong had lower median course points; the variable-naming question (Q1) showed no such relation.
- Lehtinen, Lukkarinen, Haaranen (2021): in an open online introductory JavaScript course aimed at working adults, about one third of students could not correctly explain, in free text, the structure and execution of a small program they had just submitted. For the first exercise, answering the questions correctly was associated with later course points and retention more strongly than passing the functional tests alone. The association was not significant for the second exercise.
- Lehtinen, Koutcheme, Hellas (2024): this paper tests LLMs as ANSWERERS of comprehension questions, not as generators. GPT-3.5 wrote 60 programs; the deterministic, template-based QLCpy library generated 399 questions about them; GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 then answered. Neither model answered everything correctly even though the code was LLM-written. GPT-4 beat GPT-3.5 on every question type. Errors resembled novice errors (bad tracing, misreading the question, a misconception about a built-in) plus some LLM-specific ones (miscounting line numbers, hallucinating a justification for a wrong answer).
- Lehtinen (2024 thesis abstract): pulling the studies together, 'as many as 20%' of students who write a correct program may answer incorrectly on concepts critical to reasoning about it, and more than half fail to mentally trace their program's execution. Students who answer more QLCs wrong tinker more and do less well on the course. Current AI answers QLCs better than the average novice but still makes human-like reasoning errors about its own code.
- Goodfellow et al. (2025 ITiCSE and UKICER papers): AutoMCQ uses GPT-4o mini to generate personalised multiple-choice comprehension questions from each student's CodeRunner submission. The 2025 papers describe the tool and report positive student feedback but give no accuracy figures. They state that correctness cannot be relied on, add an escape option ('This question doesn't seem right'), and restrict use to formative assessment.
- Goodfellow et al. (2026 journal article): in a pilot, the authors checked all 72 generated questions and found 1 incorrect. In a full deployment, 4,704 questions were generated for 134 students; students flagged 57 (1.2%), and the authors judged only 1 of the flagged questions 'completely incorrect'. The authors do not claim a measured error rate for the full set: they did not check every question, and say incorrect questions 'were not always identified by students'. Class test averages rose 3% and 4% on the previous year, which the authors call negligible.
- **Headline figures:** 
- Lehtinen 2023: 'Out of the 291 students in the study 79 students (27%) failed at least one of the QLCs generated for their program.' Spring 2021, Aalto, second programming course for non-CS engineering students. Each student got 2 or 3 multiple-choice questions and one attempt.
- Lehtinen 2023, per-question success rates (Table 1): Q1 'Which of the following are variable names in the program?' 249 of 291 correct (86%). Q2 'From which line can program execution jump to line X?' (an except block) 207 of 243 correct (85%). Q3 'Which of the following best describes the purpose of line X?' 281 of 291 correct (the text says 96%, Section 5.1.1 says 'Only 5% of students failed Q3'; 281/291 = 96.6%, i.e. 3.4% failed).
- Lehtinen 2023: 324 students consented (94% of those who started the course); 33 were excluded for not writing the program or not answering the QLCs, leaving 291 (90%). Of the 291, 13 had a final program that still failed at least one functional test, so 278 fully passed.
- Lehtinen 2023, course points (Table 2, Mann-Whitney U, Bonferroni threshold p < .017): Q2 correct median 5366 vs incorrect 4227.5, n = 207 vs 36, p = .011, common-language effect size .63. Q3 correct median 5348 vs incorrect 3941, n = 281 vs 10, p = .006, CLES .76. Q1 correct 5272 vs incorrect 5603.5, n = 249 vs 42, p = .556 (no difference; the incorrect group's median was slightly higher).
- Lehtinen 2021: 'One third of the students struggled to explain their own program code.' 125 consenting students of 146 enrolled (86%) in an online introductory JavaScript course, fall 2020. Exercise E1 (functions): 'approximately one third of the students failed to answer at least one of the three questions correctly'. Exercise E2 (loops): 'Little less than one third'. Exercise E3 (event-driven programming): 'Approximately half'. Group sizes used in the tests: E1, 27 passed tests but answered incorrectly vs 45 passed and answered correctly (27 of 72 = 37.5%, my arithmetic); E2, 20 vs 46 (20 of 66 = 30%, my arithmetic).
- Lehtinen 2021, later success after E1: median share of later points 0.50 for those who passed tests but answered incorrectly vs 0.98 for those who answered correctly (Mann-Whitney U = 299, n = 27 and 45, P < .001). Retention: 0.33 vs 0.75 of later exercises attempted (P = .008). For E2 the differences were not significant (P = .483 and P = .514).
- Lehtinen thesis 2024 (abstract): 'as many as 20%' of students with a correct program may answer incorrectly on critical concepts; 'More than half of the students fail to mentally trace the execution of their program.'
- Prior studies as summarised in Lehtinen 2023 (not opened): Jask (Java, CS1) found 'above 80% success rates' on static questions and 'below 50%' on dynamic (execution) questions; the JavaScript study reported success rates 'from 33% up to 97%'.
- Lehtinen, Koutcheme, Hellas 2024: 60 GPT-3.5-generated Python programs (10 per task, 6 tasks), 399 QLCs, 798 answers. Overall success: GPT-3.5 69%, GPT-4 88%. 'We identified 122 answers from GPT-3.5 that were incorrect and 47 from GPT-4 that were incorrect (61% less than GPT-3.5).'
- Lehtinen, Koutcheme, Hellas 2024, by question type (Table 3, GPT-3.5 / GPT-4): ParameterNames 95% / 100%; VariableNames 73% / 98%; LoopEnd 40% / 72%; VariableDeclaration 63% / 93%; VariableRole 83% / 91%; LinePurpose 88% / 100% (only 16 questions); LoopCount 68% / 86%; VariableTrace 55% / 70%.
- Lehtinen, Koutcheme, Hellas 2024, error types (Table 5): hallucinating a justification for a wrong answer, GPT-4 14 vs GPT-3.5 3; illogical execution steps, 31 (GPT-3.5) vs 10 (GPT-4); line number counted incorrectly, 30 vs 13. Inter-rater agreement on error coding was only moderate (Cohen's kappa 0.501 on 54 answers).
- Goodfellow 2026, pilot: 6 volunteers, 3 programming questions each, 4 generated questions per programming question = 72 questions; 'only 1 question out of the generated 72 questions was incorrect' (1.4%, my arithmetic). Model: GPT-4o mini, default settings.
- Goodfellow 2026, full study: cohort of 239 in an introductory Java class; 4,704 questions generated by 134 students; flagged by students 57 times (1.2%); 'Out of these responses only 1 was completely incorrect.' Total cost $0.50; about $0.0002 per 4 questions.
- Goodfellow 2026, survey (n = 16): 13/16 (81%) agreed the questions helped them understand their code; 15/16 (94%) thought the questions adequately tested understanding; 14/16 (88%) agreed questions were mostly coherent; all said questions were relevant. The paper also prints '11/16 or 63%' for identifying misunderstandings; 11/16 is 69%, so one of the two numbers is a typo.
- Goodfellow 2026: class test averages rose 3% (first test) and 4% (second test) on the previous year, described as 'negligible'. Engagement with the AI-question quizzes was 51-63% of the class for the first three and 36-41% for the last three.
- **Sample and method:** 
> Lehtinen 2023: observational, single course, Spring 2021 at Aalto. 291 non-CS engineering students on a 12-week second programming course (Python CS1 as prerequisite), taught online during COVID. Week-1 recap exercise: a rainfall-problem variant, auto-graded with four unit tests and up to 10 submissions. After submitting, students answered 2 or 3 template-generated multiple-choice QLCs (static program analysis, qlcpy) once, worth 5 of 100 exercise points. Correct and incorrect answerers were compared on total course points with Mann-Whitney U tests and Bonferroni correction. Students were not interviewed. Lehtinen 2021: observational, one online open JavaScript course for working adults, fall 2020, 125 consenting students. Manually prepared open-text QLCs at three exercises; answers coded by inductive content analysis (86% inter-rater agreement on an 18% sample). Lehtinen et al. 2024: a computational experiment with no human participants. Six CodeCheck Python exercises; GPT-3.5 prompted until 10 accepted, distinct, test-passing solutions per exercise; QLCpy generated one question of each supported type; gpt-3.5-turbo and gpt-4 answered with default parameters and an 'answer and explain' prompt; two researchers marked correctness and coded the decisive error in each wrong answer. Goodfellow 2026: tool deployment with surveys, no control group. Pilot with 6 second and third-year volunteers (all 72 questions author-checked). Full deployment in an introductory Java class of 239; students had to opt in because code was sent to OpenAI; 134 generated questions over five weekly practicals; 16 completed the end survey. Accuracy in the full study rests on student flags, a manual look at frequently-missed questions, and an unreported cross-check 'via other LLMs'.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> Lehtinen 2023: counts of correct and incorrect answers per question (Table 1), distractor-level breakdown, and non-parametric comparisons of course points (Table 2, Figure 2). Lehtinen 2021: coded free-text answers and Mann-Whitney comparisons of later points and retention across three groups (failed tests; passed tests but answered wrongly; passed and answered correctly). Lehtinen et al. 2024: per-type and per-task success rates for two models and a qualitative coding of 169 wrong answers into ten error types, with worked examples. Data and code are stated to be available. Goodfellow 2026: author inspection of 72 pilot questions, the count of student flags in deployment, Likert survey results, a small thematic analysis, and year-on-year class test averages. The thesis abstract summarises the underlying papers; its 20% and 'more than half' figures were not traced to a specific table.
- **Figure provenance:** 
> The '27%' is from Lehtinen 2023 (abstract and Section 4): 79 of 291. The abstract's 'solved by 90% of the students' is loose. The 90% is 291 of 324 consenting students who wrote the program AND answered the questions; 13 of those 291 still failed a functional test. So '27% of students who solved it' is close but not exact: the paper does not report the rate among the 278 who passed every test. Section 5.1.1 says 'Only 5% of students failed Q3' while Table 1 gives 281 of 291 correct (3.4% failed). The 'one third of 125 students' figure that Goodfellow et al. repeat comes from Lehtinen 2021, where 125 is the number who consented, not the number answering each question, and 'one third' is the authors' rounding (E1 works out at 27 of 72 who passed the tests). That 2021 figure is for open-text explanations in a course for working adults, not multiple choice. The '69%' and '88%' in Lehtinen et al. 2024 are accuracy of LLM ANSWERS to template-generated questions; they are not the accuracy of LLM-generated questions, and the research note's framing ('how accurate LLM-generated questions and answers were') merges two different things. That paper has small internal inconsistencies: Section 4.1 says 47 GPT-4 errors, Section 4.2 says 49 while giving the same total of 169, and the error-code table sums to 121 and 46. The only question-accuracy figures are in Goodfellow 2026, not in either 2025 paper. '1 of 72' is a full author check on the pilot. '1 completely incorrect' in the full study is 1 of the 57 FLAGGED questions, not 1 of 4,704; quoting it as an error rate of 1 in 4,704 would overstate the source. Pilot size differs between versions: the UKICER abstract says 'eight former students', the journal article says 6 volunteers. The journal article also says both '22 students participated, with 16 completing the survey' and 'Despite 134 participants beginning the survey, only 16 completed it'. The thesis's 'as many as 20%' is lower than the 27% and one-third figures because it refers to 'concepts that are critical', a narrower measure; the abstract does not say which study it comes from.
- **Population and transfer:** 
> Mostly university novices. Lehtinen 2023: first-year (some second or third-year) non-CS engineering students who had passed one Python course. Goodfellow: first-year Java students with no required prior experience. The exception is Lehtinen 2021, whose course was for 'people in the workforce aiming to expand their skills', some with 'years of industry experience'; it is the closest to adult learners in work, though still an introductory course. Lehtinen et al. 2024 studied models, not people. Transfer to junior engineers is by analogy: the programs were tiny (a rainfall function, a countdown loop) and the questions elementary. The transferable idea is the mechanism, that passing tests does not show the author understands the code, which is likely to matter more when the code came from an AI tool. No study here tested that on professional code.
- **Outcome measured:** 
> Immediate comprehension-question score on the learner's own just-submitted code (Lehtinen 2021, 2023), and its association with later course points and retention within the same course. LLM answer accuracy on comprehension questions (Lehtinen et al. 2024). Question correctness, student flags, student perceptions, cost and year-on-year class test averages (Goodfellow 2026). None measures delayed retention, whether answering the questions causes learning, or on-the-job performance.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Lehtinen 2023: 'As students volunteered for the study there is a risk of selection bias.' '33 students did not complete the studied task. This likely excluded lower achieving students'. 'there are reasons other than poor understanding, such as carelessness which can make the student select an incorrect option.' 'we did not interview the students to discover why they answered as they did.' 'the population who answered incorrectly to Q3 is small and recommend validation using a larger population.' Some students may have had outside help answering, and plagiarised or copied code is a possible cause of wrong answers.
- Lehtinen 2021: E3 'had significant self-selection bias'. 'This early research aims to report potential and correlations of QLCs and not to validate instruments or causalities.' Results depend on question wording, alignment with teaching and exercise design. Open-text answers also test the ability to describe concepts, not only comprehension.
- Lehtinen et al. 2024: two models, six problems, one language (Python); 'it is possible that the performance of other (and upcoming) LLMs would differ from our results.' One generic prompt, not optimised: 'the overall average model performance would likely have been somewhat better had we resorted to building programming problems and QLC-specific prompts.' Default sampling parameters. Only the first decisive error in each wrong answer was coded, and explanations attached to correct answers were not checked.
- Goodfellow 2026: 'Due to the nature of GenAI, not all generated questions will be useful or internally coherent. Some questions are occasionally nonsensical or relate to unimportant aspects of the problem'. 'only 16 completed it. This means that our data is limited and that our results are possibly overly positive due to selection bias.' Reliance on the flag option 'relies on students being able to understand and identify incorrect questions. As shown in our results only 1 student out of 57 correctly identified an incorrect question.' 'It is also likely that students did not identify incorrect questions and believed some were correct, doubting their own understanding.' Not tested on larger code segments, where the authors expect more hallucinations.
- Goodfellow 2025 (ITiCSE): 'In a survey, the majority of students claimed it helped them better comprehend their code. However, a larger study would be needed to formally prove this.'
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Within the Lehtinen studies the link between question failure and later performance is partial. In 2023 the variable-name question (the most-failed one, 14% wrong) had no relation to course points, and the significant Q3 result rests on 10 students. In 2021 the association held for one exercise and not the next. Most students did answer correctly: 73% got every question right in 2023, and success on the individual questions was 85-97%. So the finding is 'a sizeable minority', not 'most'. Wrong answers were often terminology slips (calling print or sum a variable) that need not mean the student cannot reason about execution; the 2023 authors themselves say execution and purpose errors mattered more than terminology. On the AI side, Lehtinen et al. 2024 cuts against using an LLM unchecked as the answer key: GPT-4 was wrong on 12% of simple questions about short LLM-written programs and tended to defend wrong answers. Against that, those are 2023-era models with a deliberately plain prompt, and Goodfellow's later GPT-4o mini deployment found very few wrong questions among those checked. Goodfellow's own evidence of benefit is weak: no control group, a 'negligible' rise in test averages, and a 16-person self-selected survey.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> For students failing questions on their own code: carelessness on a low-stakes question worth 5 points with one attempt; unfamiliar terminology; ambiguous wording (33 of 36 wrong Q2 answers picked the try line, 24 of them the line directly before the correct one, which may be a defensible mental model of where the jump starts); copied or shared code; or, in 2021, difficulty writing an explanation in prose. The correlation with course points may reflect general diligence or prior experience, not comprehension specifically: in the 2021 study, answering correctly correlated with prior programming courses (r = .43) and prior hours (r = .37). The 27% is also a function of how many questions were asked; with three questions each answered correctly about 85-97% of the time, a quarter failing at least one is partly arithmetic. For the AI results: GPT errors on line-number questions reflect how models handle numbered text, not comprehension. Goodfellow's low flag rate may show that students cannot tell when a question is wrong, not that questions are right; the authors say as much. Their test-score rise could be cohort variation.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Bears on 'weaker learning with AI' and on the training response; says nothing about hiring. It supplies pre-AI baseline evidence that working code is a poor proxy for understanding: about a quarter to a third of learners failed simple questions about code they had just submitted, in data from 2020 and 2021, before ChatGPT. That supports the argument that AI-assisted output will hide even more, but the studies do not show it, since none compared AI-assisted with unassisted code. It also gives the nearest thing to a tested precedent for Lee's small-challenge idea: short questions generated from a person's own code, delivered right after they finish.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> 1) Format: two to four short questions about code the person has just written or merged is a workable unit. It was run at scale in both Aalto and Strathclyde courses, takes minutes, and surfaced gaps that passing tests did not. 2) Question type matters. Execution and purpose questions ('from which line can execution reach this handler?', 'what is this line for?') tracked later performance; naming and terminology questions did not. Tracing questions are the hardest and the most revealing. For a team, that points to questions about control flow, failure paths and intent, not vocabulary. 3) Using AI to generate the exercises looks feasible and cheap (GPT-4o mini, $0.50 for 4,704 questions), and the checked sample was almost all correct (71 of 72), but it has only been validated on small beginner Java programs. The authors expect more hallucination on larger code, which is the case that matters for a real codebase. 4) Do not let the model be the unchecked answer key, and do not rely on learners to spot bad questions: students flagged 57 questions and were right once, and Lehtinen et al. show a model will defend a wrong answer with a plausible explanation. A reviewer, a second-model check or a deterministic generator (QLCpy-style templates have known-correct answers by construction) is needed. 5) Keep it formative and low-stakes, as both groups do. Goodfellow notes students 'bristle' at code interviews and treat wrong answers as embarrassment; private, ungraded questions avoid that. A wrong or debatable question can be turned into a discussion. 6) Frequency: both deployments were weekly or per-exercise, embedded in the normal work, not a separate course. Engagement in the Strathclyde class fell over the term (from 51-63% to 36-41%), a warning that voluntary challenges decay. 7) None of this shows that answering the questions improves skill. The evidence is that the questions detect fragile understanding; whether they repair it is untested.
- **Open questions:** 
> Whether answering questions about your own code produces learning, as opposed to detecting its absence (no controlled study found here). The true error rate of LLM-generated questions at scale: Goodfellow's full analysis is deferred to 'a future paper'. How LLM-generated questions perform on larger, real-world or AI-written code. Whether people fail more questions about AI-generated code they accepted than about code they wrote; these studies predate or do not isolate AI assistance. Which underlying study the thesis's 20% and 'more than half' figures come from. Whether any of this holds for employed junior engineers. How current models would score on the 2024 QLC answering test.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Dave Thomas (code kata): developers practise on the job, which is why they make mistakes on the job. Practice should be separated from delivery. A kata is a short exercise of 30 to 60 minutes, repeated with small improvements, done without deadline pressure and with feedback each time. The solution is not the point. He offers the idea as an analogy with musicians, athletes and karate, plus his own experience, and says 'I don't know' how to make it happen in workplaces beyond taking the pressure off and teaching people to play with code.
- Tian Pan: AI lets a new engineer ship before reading the codebase, producing code that is 'locally coherent' but 'globally inconsistent'. He proposes five practices: a traced 'code archaeology' exercise before the first commit, with a written list of surprises reviewed by a senior; architecture decision records used as onboarding reading; a weekly 30-minute team code reading; pairing a junior with a senior for exploration (not implementation) before AI is used to implement; and team standards encoded as custom instructions for the AI tool. He does not propose AI-free periods.
- Garima Agarwal (LeadDev): after six months of mandatory AI coding tools, juniors could not explain a service they had shipped features against. Her team split onboarding work into 'output tasks' and 'learning tasks' (attempt without AI first, then compare with the AI's approach in a written paragraph), added a weekly 15-minute 'decision walk' through a merged pull request, a two-week AI-free 'codebase read' answering set questions, and a monthly debugging session on real anonymised incidents. This is the closest thing found to a treasure hunt through a codebase, and the only practitioner source that reports an outcome number.
- Giordano Scalzo (LeadDev): names three exercises for juniors (AI-off sessions, Explain the Machine, Break It challenges) aimed at what he calls the 'Confidence Gap'. How they work is behind a registration wall and was not read.
- Laurie Barth (LeadDev): a first-person account of a senior engineer changing teams. AI made early contribution faster but she reports understanding the system less deeply and closing the knowledge gap more slowly than she would have without it.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Dave Thomas admits he does not know how to bring practice into real workplaces: 'I don't know, but my gut tells me we need to do two main things.' He also notes practice needs 'no pressure', which he says 'is why it is hard to practice in a project environment'.
- Tian Pan concedes that encoding standards in the AI tool 'isn't a fix for the learning problem'. He states no limits on his other four practices and gives no data for them.
- Agarwal states the costs plainly ('The costs were real. The codebase read adds two weeks to onboarding. The decision walks add 30 minutes of senior engineer time each week. The debugging sessions require preparation.') but does not state the limits of her evidence: no team size, no comparison group, no account of what else changed over the six months.
- Anthropic's docs: 'An output style gives Claude instructions to follow. It doesn't guarantee that something always happens or never happens.' The styles also 'produce longer responses than Default by design, which increases output tokens'. Drew Bent, to Engadget: Anthropic doesn't 'have all the answers'.
- OpenAI [uncertain, from press coverage of the launch, not the primary page]: study mode runs on system instructions and so shows 'some inconsistent behavior and mistakes across conversations'; users can switch back to ordinary ChatGPT at any time.
- Barth: AI's 'accuracy can be subjective' and is hard to check without knowing the codebase.
- Rooksby et al. say of the coding dojo that 'the theoretical bases behind it have never been investigated', and their own paper looks at only two sessions.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> 1) The nearest controlled test of a gamified format found no learning gain: Cole (2022) reports higher motivation with CTF-style exercises but no significant difference in exam scores. That supports CTF and treasure-hunt formats as engaging, not as better for learning. A 2025 study of 60 undergraduates reportedly found CTF improved self-efficacy and outcomes, but it was seen only as a search snippet and its design is unclear. 2) Coding-dojo research is thin. Rooksby et al. is qualitative and covers two sessions. A Brazilian undergraduate case study (snippet only) reportedly found students' self-assessed competence rose more than their measured performance, which hints that dojos can inflate confidence. 3) The one study of a vendor learning mode is reported as mixed: a gain in microeconomics and none in neuroscience, run by the vendor with a measure it designed. 4) Barth's account cuts against any assumption that an explaining AI is enough: she used AI to learn a codebase and still reports shallower understanding. 5) Learning and Explanatory styles are opt-in, cost more tokens and are not enforced, so a junior under delivery pressure can simply leave them off. Nothing shows how often they are used. 6) Thomas's own condition for practice is the absence of pressure, which a scheduled, manager-run programme may not provide.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> Agarwal's drop from seven months to four could come from things other than the redesign: a different cohort of juniors, better AI tools over the same six months, more senior attention of any kind (a Hawthorne-style effect), ordinary variation in a small group, or a metric defined after the fact by the person who made the changes. Publication is also selective, since leaders write up redesigns that seemed to work. Cole's null result on exams may reflect an insensitive test or equal time on task rather than equal learning. The reported study mode gain in one subject and not the other may reflect differences in the subjects, the students or the exams. The popularity of katas for two decades shows that practitioners like them, not that they work.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the evidence base for the essay's proposed answer, and it is weak. Practitioners are converging on the same shape Lee proposes: short, regular, low-stakes exercises that make a junior read and explain the system. Agarwal's AI-free codebase read and Tian Pan's traced request are treasure hunts in all but name, and Thomas's kata is the small coding challenge. But the support is one team's before-and-after number, some anecdotes and a twenty-year-old analogy. The essay can say the proposal matches what experienced leaders are trying. It cannot say it has been shown to work.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> Frequency: the sources cluster on weekly and short. Tian Pan suggests 30 minutes a week of shared code reading, Agarwal a 15-minute weekly decision walk and a monthly incident session, Thomas 30 to 60 minutes per kata. Format: every AI-era design asks the junior to explain or trace real code rather than write new code, usually as a set question ('Where does a request enter the system, and what happens before it reaches the database?'), with the answer written down or spoken to a senior. Difficulty and AI use: Agarwal's sequence is attempt without AI, then use AI, then explain the difference, which keeps the tool but puts the effort first; Tian Pan puts exploration before AI implementation. Feedback: all rely on a senior engineer reviewing the answer, which is the real cost (30 minutes of senior time a week in Agarwal's account, two extra weeks of onboarding) and the part an AI-generated programme would have to replace or support. Use of AI to generate exercises: none of these sources proposes or tests it. The Claude Code Learning style is the nearest thing, an AI choosing which few lines the human writes, and it has no outcome data. Thomas's warning applies to delivery: practice needs to be free of deadline pressure. A sensible reading is that an in-house programme should be treated as an experiment and measured, for example by Agarwal's metric of time to first independently handled incident or by her no-laptop 'explain the service' question, since no one else has measured it.
- **Open questions:** 
> Whether any of these designs improves comprehension or performance among working engineers under controlled conditions. No such study was found. How many juniors sit behind Agarwal's figure and what her employer is. What Scalzo's three exercises involve. What OpenAI's study mode trial found in full, since its page could not be opened. Whether the Claude Code Learning or Explanatory styles change what users retain, and how many people use them. Whether a published, named codebase scavenger-hunt design exists; none turned up in search. Whether AI-generated exercises work without a senior engineer checking the answers.

- IBM: Fortune notes the layoffs announced a week after Krishna's October 2025 remarks, and that US headcount would be 'roughly flat'.
- AWS: Garman, asked how AWS measures success with AI in engineering, says in the Berman interview 'I won't pretend that we have kind of that magic measure today' (auto-captions, about 14:15).

- AWS: Garman accepts in Platformer that 'half of white-collar jobs may change', and Newton notes his optimism 'doesn't always seem to square with Amazon's own moves'.
- Beane: the ASQ abstract limits the finding to surgical trainees in robotic and open surgery. The generalisation to other industries in the TED talk rests on other researchers' data that he pooled, described only in outline.
- Beane: he says the minority who did learn got there by breaking rules, which he calls 'not OK' and 'not sustainable' (TED talk), so the study describes a failure, not a tested remedy.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> IBM's announcement sits beside its own cuts: Fortune reports thousands of jobs cut in late 2025 with US headcount roughly flat, so tripling a small undisclosed base may not mean many more juniors. Fortune also cites a Korn Ferry figure that 37% of organisations plan to replace early career roles with AI, and names Dario Amodei and Jim Farley as executives predicting entry-level losses. Amazon has cut roughly 30,000 corporate jobs since October 2025 (Platformer), its chief executive has written that AI will 'reduce our total corporate workforce', and Garman himself said in 2024 that most developers might not be coding within 24 months. Garman's claim that juniors are the most fluent with AI tools cuts against the essay's other evidence that novices who lean on AI understand less of what they build. On Beane: his own bomb-disposal example shows a technology that improved novice learning ('Trainees learn better than they did before robots'), so separation is a design outcome, not a law. His evidence is from robotics, where the novice is physically displaced; a junior engineer still has full access to the code and the tool. No critique of The Skill Code by another researcher was opened.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Employer branding. Both statements are cheap to make and flattering. Neither comes with a number that could be checked later, apart from Amazon's 11,000.
- Base effects. 'Tripling' tells you nothing without the starting point. If graduate hiring had been cut hard in 2023 to 2025, tripling could simply restore an earlier level.
- Definition. If IBM counts career changers and returners as entry-level, the tripling is not all new graduates.
- Cost. Garman's first argument is that juniors are the least expensive staff. Hiring them alongside cuts to middle management is consistent with cheapening the workforce as much as with protecting a pipeline.
- Selling the tool. Garman's remark is wrapped in a pitch for Kiro as a way to teach juniors good practice. A cloud vendor benefits if customers keep large engineering teams that consume its services.
- Different jobs, same title. IBM's juniors now spend more time with clients and marketing. That may build commercial skill while reducing the time spent learning to read and reason about code, which is the skill the essay is worried about.
- Beane's residents may have been failed by scheduling and supervision rules as much as by the robot. A hospital could have changed how console time was allocated.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the leadership-response strand. Two large employers say out loud that cutting juniors empties the pipeline, which supports the essay's question about who will be senior in five years. Neither says how the juniors will be trained, beyond rewritten roles (IBM) and an AI tool that guides them (AWS). That gap is where the essay's proposal sits. Beane supplies the mechanism for why hiring alone is not enough: a senior with a capable tool has no practical reason to bring the junior into the work, so the learning has to be designed in.
- **Implications for training design:** 
> Nothing here tests a training format, so there is no evidence on frequency or on katas and treasure hunts. Beane's framework gives design criteria that can be quoted as his view, not as proven results. Challenge: exercises should sit near the edge of what the junior can do and feel uncomfortable, which argues against AI doing the hard part. Complexity: juniors need exposure to the wider system, which is a reasonable argument for a guided tour or hunt through the codebase. Connection: the expert has to be near. His bomb-disposal example worked because the senior sat beside the junior and talked through live work, so a challenge delivered by AI with no senior engineer reviewing or discussing it would miss the third C. Garman's Kiro point and Beane's 2018 call to 'build AI to help: to coach learners as they struggle' both allow AI a role in generating or guiding exercises. IBM's redesign moves juniors toward clients and marketing, which is a caution: a training programme needs to protect time on code comprehension specifically.

> How many entry-level people IBM hired before and after, and whether the tripling is US-only and includes non-graduates. Whether LaMoreaux said 'double down' or 'doubled down' (no recording opened). The exact words Garman spoke (the audio was not listened to) and which 'leadership group' he meant. What The Skill Code says in its own words, and whether it discusses software engineers at all. Whether either company has any data showing its juniors are learning the craft under the new arrangements.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Who coined what. Neither Osmani nor Storey claims to have coined their term, and neither should be credited with it. 'Comprehension debt' in the AI-code sense was in print from Jason Gorman on 30 September 2025 ('what I've been calling "comprehension debt"'), about five and a half months before Osmani's post. Osmani's post does not mention Gorman. 'Cognitive debt' comes from the MIT Media Lab preprint 'Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt...' (Kosmyna et al., June 2025), which is about essay writing and EEG measures in individuals; both Storey and Osmani link to it. Storey calls it 'a term gaining traction recently' and adapts it to software teams. What Storey did originate is the software-team definition, the 'Triple Debt Model' and the term 'intent debt' as she uses it. [uncertain: I found no earlier use than Gorman's for 'comprehension debt', but did not search exhaustively, so 'earliest found' is safer than 'coined'.]
- Osmani's definition: comprehension debt is 'the growing gap between how much code exists in your system and how much of it any human being genuinely understands'. He treats 'comprehension debt' and 'cognitive debt' as two names for one thing.
- Osmani's argument: AI writes code faster than people can review it, so review stops spreading understanding. Tests and specs help but cannot replace comprehension. Standard measures (velocity, DORA, PR counts, coverage) do not show the loss, which makes it worse than technical debt because nobody chose it. Engineers with deep system knowledge become more valuable.
- Osmani on juniors: 'a junior engineer can now generate code faster than a senior engineer can critically audit it'. This is his only direct statement about junior engineers, and it is about review load, not about how juniors learn.
- Storey's blog definition (9 February): cognitive debt is debt that 'lives in the brains of the developers', as against technical debt, which lives in the code. On 18 February she tightened it to 'the accumulated gap between a system's evolving structure and a team's shared understanding of how and why that system works and can be changed over time'.
- Storey's paper definition: cognitive debt is 'a team-level, project-level property reflecting the erosion of shared understanding across a software system over time'. She separates this from the individual, neural sense used by Kosmyna et al. and from 'comprehension debt', which she attributes to Alakmeh et al. 2026 (a paper she co-authored), not to Osmani or Gorman.
- Triple Debt Model: technical debt lives in code, cognitive debt in people, intent debt in artifacts (specs, decision records, tests). The three reinforce each other. AI may reduce technical debt while speeding up the other two.
- Storey says cognitive debt 'is not new'. What is new is 'the rate at which this gap can accumulate, and the difficulty of detecting it'. She also allows that it can be taken on deliberately, as her students did.
- Storey's warning signs: reluctance to change the system, changes that give unexpected results, slow onboarding, losing track of who knows what, low bus factor.
- Storey's remedies: code review and pair programming for spreading understanding, system walkthroughs where developers explain code they did not write, retrospectives, deliberate communication during onboarding and offboarding, and reimplementation. She warns against using AI to produce documentation in place of the team doing the understanding.
- Naur 1985: programming is building a theory held by the programmers, not producing program text. The theory lets a programmer explain how the program maps to the world, justify why each part is as it is, and respond to requests for change. It cannot be fully written down. A program dies when the team holding its theory is dissolved, even if the code keeps running. New programmers acquire the theory only by working closely with those who have it, doing 'the relevant things under suitable supervision and guidance'. Reviving a program from documentation alone is 'strictly impossible'. Naur wrote nothing about debt or AI; the link to both is made by Storey and others.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Storey (blog) presents the idea as needing research, not as established: 'this phenomenon demands serious research attention. How do we measure cognitive debt? What practices are most effective at preventing or reducing it in AI-augmented development environments?'
- Storey (paper) says the phenomenon is old: 'Cognitive debt is not new; developers have long worked with incomplete and distributed understanding of complex systems.'
- Storey (paper) hedges throughout ('may', 'perhaps') and ends with 'Points for Debate', including: 'This paper frames cognitive debt as a risk to be managed ... Others argue this understanding is often not needed at all, similarly to how managers have always had to trust team members. With generative AI automating more development work, knowing how much debt is acceptable in a particular organization or project remains uncertain.'
- Storey (paper) on documentation: 'The debate about whether and how intent should be documented is far from settled.'
- Storey (paper) allows that taking on the debt can be rational: students 'were rightly focused on getting rapid feedback on their ideas'.
- Osmani states no limitations. His site carries the general line 'The views and opinions expressed on this site are my own.'
- Naur limits his own strongest claim: 'the need for revival of an entirely dead program probably will rarely arise' (HTML transcription only).
- **Counter evidence:** 
- Fowler (13 February 2026) reframes it: the cruft is 'ignorance', and the debt metaphor only says you either pay interest or make 'an explicit investment to gain knowledge'. Which is right 'depends on the relative costs'. On that reading some cognitive debt is worth carrying.
- Storey reports the objection from Michael Wuersch and others that 'cognitive debt reflects a failure of good engineering discipline', which is to say nothing new. She agrees 'in principle'.
- Storey's own 'Points for Debate' records the view that detailed understanding 'is often not needed at all', as with managers who trust their teams.
- The same tools may reduce the debt. Storey: 'Used deliberately, AI may help make cognitive work more visible rather than obscuring it.' Fowler's 9 February fragment suggests asking the LLM to explain complex code it wrote. Storey's paper counters that this risks 'the appearance of understanding'.
- Developers have always worked with code they did not fully understand (legacy systems, libraries, copied Stack Overflow answers), as Storey's paper notes, and software has still been built.
- Naur's 'strictly impossible' claim about reviving a program from documentation is absolute and untested, and teams do take over inherited code. Whether AI assistants make that takeover easier is an open empirical question that postdates him.
- The evidence borrowed from Anthropic is about one person learning a library in an hour. It does not show that teams lose their grasp of a codebase over months.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- The student team's wall may come from inexperience, a compressed semester, and no design discipline. Storey's blog never says how much of the code was AI-generated, and the team itself 'initially blamed technical debt'. The same story could be told about student projects before AI.
- 'Comprehension debt' may be ordinary legacy-code maintenance cost under a new name. Gorman says so himself: 'In that sense, this is nothing new. What is new is the scale'.
- The two terms name different things that are often merged. Kosmyna's cognitive debt is an individual's reduced engagement while writing essays. Storey's is a team's eroded shared understanding. Osmani's is a ratio of code to understood code. Osmani's opening sentence ('hidden cost to human intelligence and memory') leans on the first, his definition on the third.
- Motivated framing: Osmani and Gorman both sell books or training on disciplined AI-assisted development, and Storey's research area is human aspects of software engineering. None of that makes the idea wrong, but each has reason to see the problem.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This gives the essay its vocabulary for the 'weaker learning with AI' strand and the bridge from an individual's quiz score to a team's long-term health. Naur is the strongest support for Lee's training proposal: he says a new programmer gets the theory only by working with those who hold it and by doing the work under guidance, not from reading code and documents. Storey supplies the modern framing and names onboarding as a place where the debt shows. Neither term is evidence. They are names for a worry, backed by one anecdote and one borrowed trial, so the essay should use them as framing and credit them accurately (Gorman and the MIT group for the phrases, Storey for the team-level model, Osmani for popularising).
- **Implications for training design:** 
> Indirect, and argued, not tested. (1) Format: Naur's test of holding the theory is being able to explain how the program maps to the world, justify why each part is as it is, and respond to a change request. Codebase treasure hunts and challenges could be built around those three (find where X is handled, explain why it was done this way, make a small change safely). (2) Storey's named practice closest to Lee's idea is 'system walkthroughs where developers explain code they did not write for the purpose of knowledge sharing and theory building'. She also lists retrospectives, pair programming, review, and reimplementing a feature to rebuild understanding. (3) Frequency: Storey's blog asks for 'regular checkpoints where the team rebuilds shared understanding'; no interval is given. (4) Contact with people: Naur says reading is 'insufficient' and that the learner needs 'close contact with the programmers who already possess the theory'. A purely self-serve, AI-generated quiz would not meet that on his account, so exercises should be reviewed or debriefed by someone who knows the system. (5) AI-generated material: Storey's 'Resist the automation of understanding' is a direct caution. AI can lower the cost of producing exercises, but AI-written explanations consumed passively give 'the appearance of understanding'. The exercise has to make the engineer do the work. (6) Measurement: Storey suggests tracking onboarding time and knowledge concentration (bus factor), which could serve as outcome measures for a training programme. None of these sources reports outcome data for any such practice.
- **Open questions:** 
> Whether anyone has measured comprehension or cognitive debt in a working team (none found in these sources). Whether walkthroughs, checkpoints or codebase exercises reduce it. Who first used 'comprehension debt' (Gorman, September 2025, is the earliest found). Whether the phrase 'cognitive debt' predates the June 2025 MIT preprint, and whether the 2024 CHI workshop paper cited by Storey exists. What Alakmeh et al. (ICPC 2026) actually say about comprehension debt. Whether Storey's paper has been published in a peer-reviewed venue. How much AI the student team in Storey's anecdote used. The exact pagination of Naur's original article.
