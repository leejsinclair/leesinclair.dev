### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- The follow-up experiment (started August 2025) gives 'an unreliable signal of the current productivity effect of AI tools'. METR is changing the design.
- Primary stated reason: 'a significant increase in developers choosing not to participate in the study because they do not wish to work without AI', which METR says likely biases the estimated speedup downwards.
- Two named selection channels. (1) Recruitment and retention: 'An increased share of developers say they would not want to do 50% of their work without AI' (50% being the share of tasks randomised to no-AI), so the study misses developers most optimistic about AI. (2) Task selection: 'When surveyed, 30% to 50% of developers told us that they were choosing not to submit some tasks because they did not want to do them without AI', so the study misses tasks with high expected uplift.
- The 30% to 50% figure describes developers who were IN the study and held back SOME tasks. It is not a count of developers who refused to take part. METR gives no number, share or rate for developers who declined or dropped out; it says only 'a significant increase' and 'an increased share'.
- METR judges the reported estimate is likely 'a lower-bound on the true productivity effects of AI on these developers', and also says the selection 'seem[s] to affect a minority share of developers and of tasks, which limits the degree of bias'.
- METR believes, 'based on conversations with study participants', that developers are 'likely' more sped up in early 2026 than in early 2025, but says its data is 'only very weak evidence for the size of this increase'.
- The pay cut from $150/hour to $50/hour 'also likely contributed to the higher selection'. METR's judgement, from interviews and surveys, is that higher expected uplift is the primary cause. The post offers no quantitative split between the two.
- Four further problems, judged 'less severe': task types shifted toward AI's strengths; output quality differed between arms (code quality, documentation, tests); some developers were less likely to finish AI-disallowed tasks (one finished none of them); time reporting is hard with agents because developers work on other things while the agent runs.
- Six lines of future work are listed: more intensive (shorter, possibly better paid) experiments; observational data; questionnaires with time-use studies; fixed-task experiments; evaluations (task evals of autonomous agents); developer-level randomisation. None is announced as the chosen new design.
- METR expects the problem to worsen: 'As AI capabilities continue to increase and developers' expectations grow as well, these effects will only get more dramatic, further limiting the validity of this study design.'
- **Headline figures:** 
- Study size: 'data from 57 developers, across 143 repos, and 800+ tasks'. 10 developers from the original study plus 47 newly recruited. Median 10 years' experience.
- Dates: 'started a new experiment in August 2025'. No end date is given in the post. The post is dated 24 February 2026. (From the public data.csv, my own tabulation: issue randomisation timestamps run from 12 August 2025 to 23 February 2026.)
- Pay: $50/hour, down from $150/hour in the original study.
- Task withholding: 'When surveyed, 30% to 50% of developers told us that they were choosing not to submit some tasks because they did not want to do them without AI.' A range, with no explanation of why it is a range, no survey n and no question wording.
- Returning developers (n=10): 'we now estimate a speedup of -18% with a confidence interval between -38% and +9%'. Negative means tasks took LESS time with AI allowed. Interval crosses zero.
- Newly recruited developers (n=47): 'the estimated speedup is -4%, with a confidence interval between -15% and +9%'. Interval crosses zero.
- Early-2025 comparison as restated here: 'the use of AI causes tasks to take 19% longer, with a confidence interval between +2% and +39%'. The post's first sentence rounds the same result to 'a 20% slowdown'.
- Sign convention, settled from METR's own material: the figure is the percentage change in completion time when AI is allowed. The post uses +19% for 'take 19% longer', so -18% is 18% less time, i.e. faster with AI. The published regression.py confirms it: it regresses log(total implementation time) on an AI-allowed dummy and reports exp(beta) - 1 as the 'speedup', with a 95% interval. METR's label 'a speedup of -18%' is what causes the confusion, since a negative speedup reads like a slowdown.
- No pooled estimate for all 57 developers is given in the post.
- Aggregate statistic cited in passing: 'approximately 4% of GitHub commits are authored by Claude Code' (linked to a SemiAnalysis newsletter, not METR's own measurement).
- From the public dataset, my own tabulation and not stated by METR: 1,134 issue rows, 57 developer IDs, 10 panel developers; 611 AI-allowed and 523 AI-disallowed. Completed share: 460 of 611 AI-allowed (75%) versus 368 of 523 AI-disallowed (70%). Among the 10 returning developers: 71 of 87 AI-allowed complete (82%) versus 66 of 102 AI-disallowed (65%). Re-running the README's regression on complete issues gives point estimates of -18.1% (returning, 137 issues), -3.6% (new, 690 issues) and -5.8% (all 57 developers, 827 issues), which matches the post's -18% and -4%.
- **Sample and method:** 
> Randomised controlled trial at the task level, same method as the early-2025 study. 57 experienced open-source developers (10 returning from the 2025 study, 47 newly recruited from 'a more diverse set of repositories—including smaller, more greenfield, and less mature repositories'), median 10 years' experience, working on their own existing projects across 143 repos, 800+ tasks. Developers pre-specified each task and submitted it for randomisation to 'AI allowed' or 'AI disallowed', then self-recorded time to completion. Paid $50/hour. Started August 2025; no end date given (public data runs to 23 February 2026). Alongside the trial METR ran surveys and interviews with participants, which are the basis for the 30% to 50% figure and the quotes; the post gives no sample size, response rate, dates or question wording for those surveys.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> Three kinds. (1) Regression estimates from the trial data, with 95% intervals, backed by a public dataset and script that reproduce the point estimates. (2) A participant survey, reported only as a '30% to 50%' range with no n or wording. (3) Interviews and 'conversations with study participants', represented by three selected quotes. The central claims about why the design failed (developers declining to take part, task withholding, expectation of uplift as the main driver over pay) rest on (2) and (3) and on METR's judgement, not on a measured quantity. The claim that developers are 'likely' more sped up than in early 2025 is explicitly based on conversations, not on the estimates.
- **Data vintage:** 
> Published 24 February 2026. Data collection began August 2025; the public dataset's timestamps run 12 August 2025 to 23 February 2026. Tools in use were late-2025 and early-2026 agentic tools ('such as Claude Code and Codex'). Blog post only, no paper or arXiv version, no revision note on the page as read on 12 October 2026. The early-2025 comparison figures come from arXiv 2507.09089 (data February to June 2025).
- **Figure provenance:** 
> All trial figures (57 developers, 10 + 47, 143 repos, 800+ tasks, $50 and $150, -18% [-38%, +9%], -4% [-15%, +9%], 30% to 50%) originate in this post and nowhere earlier. The -18% and -4% point estimates are reproducible from METR's public data.csv using the README's log-linear regression (I reproduced -18.1% and -3.6%; intervals not recomputed). The 30% to 50% figure has no underlying data published. Conflicting versions in circulation: (a) the sign. Secondary write-ups split between reading -18% as an 18% speed-up and reading it as no speed-up or a slowdown; METR's text and code make it 18% less time with AI, with an interval that includes zero. (b) '19%' versus '20%': the post itself uses both for the 2025 result. (c) 30% to 50% is often restated as developers 'refusing to work without AI' or refusing to take part; METR's wording is that they chose 'not to submit some tasks'. (d) The '4% of GitHub commits' figure is SemiAnalysis's, cited by METR, and was not checked here.
- **Population and transfer:** 
> Experienced open-source contributors (median 10 years) working on repositories they already know, self-selected into a paid study, and by late 2025 heavy users of agentic tools. Transfer to engineers in ordinary companies is limited: these are volunteers choosing their own tasks, paid hourly by a research body, with no employer able to assign work or require a condition. The specific failure (people opting out of the no-AI arm) depends on participation being voluntary and on task choice being theirs. An employer could mandate a no-AI arm, though the quality, completion and time-tracking problems would still apply.
- **Outcome measured:** 
> Task speed: self-recorded time to complete a pre-specified task (initial implementation plus post-review time), compared across AI-allowed and AI-disallowed arms. Not quality, not value delivered, not employment. The post itself flags that time differences 'may not represent value-differences'.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Developer-level selection: 'a significant increase in developers choosing not to participate in the study because they do not wish to work without AI'; the study is 'systematically missing developers who have the most optimistic expectations about AI's value'.
- Task-level selection: 30% to 50% of surveyed developers withheld some tasks; 'we are systematically missing tasks which have high expected uplift from AI'.
- Pay confound: 'the second study also had a lower pay rate, $50/hour instead of $150/hour in the original study, and this also likely contributed to the higher selection'.
- Task-type substitution: tasks attempted 'were different with agentic AI, leaning on the strengths of AI', so in-study tasks differ from out-of-study tasks and 'the time-differences within the study may not represent value-differences'.
- Quality differences between arms: 'differences in subjective code quality, or the amount of documentation or tests they chose to create'.
- Differential completion: 'Some developers were less likely to complete tasks that they submitted if they were assigned to the AI-disallowed condition. One developer did not complete any of the tasks that were assigned to the AI-disallowed condition.'
- Time measurement: unreliable 'for the fraction of developers who use multiple AI agents concurrently'; developers 'would often work an unrelated task while waiting for the agent'.
- Overall: 'these issues make it challenging to interpret our central estimate, and we believe it is likely a bad proxy for the real productivity impact of AI tools on these developers.'
- What METR does not claim. It does not claim a measured speed-up: the data is 'only very weak evidence for the size of this increase' and both intervals include zero. It does not claim the bias is large: selection affects 'a minority share of developers and of tasks, which limits the degree of bias'. It does not endorse developers' own figures: 'Some developers self-report very high speedups, though as we documented in our earlier study those estimates can be quite unreliable.' It does not retract or revise the early-2025 result. It does not say which new design it has chosen.
- The GitHub README adds: 'Please interpret these results with a major grain of salt'. It also notes that 'invalid issues from cheating developers are filtered out of this dataset' and that some time fields have uncorrected 'fat-finger errors'.
- **Counter evidence:** 
- The post's own numbers cut against a strong reading in either direction. Both late-2025 intervals include zero, so the data alone does not show a speed-up; among the 47 new developers the estimate is -4% [-15%, +9%], close to nothing.
- Against 'the control group is impossible': METR says the selection affects 'a minority share of developers and of tasks', and it still collected 800+ randomised tasks from 57 developers, roughly half of them without AI. The no-AI arm was run; what failed was its representativeness.
- Against 'developers will not work without AI' as the sole cause: pay fell by two thirds between the studies, and METR concedes this 'likely contributed'. The original study at $150/hour did not report this problem. The two changes (better tools, lower pay) happened together and the post does not separate them with data.
- Against treating reluctance as proof of real uplift: the developers' expectation of uplift is what drives the withholding, and METR's 2025 study found such expectations were wrong in sign. The post repeats that self-reports 'can be quite unreliable'. The quoted '2 hours' versus '20 hours' is a developer's belief, not a measurement.
- The lower-bound claim is METR's inference, not a result. Differential non-completion and quality differences between arms could bias the estimate in either direction, and METR does not sign those effects. In the public data (my tabulation) AI-disallowed tasks were completed less often than AI-allowed ones (70% versus 75% overall; 65% versus 82% among returning developers), which means the completed no-AI tasks are themselves a selected set.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Price, not dependence: at one third of the previous rate, the study no longer compensates for the inconvenience of working without a preferred tool. Reluctance then reflects the pay, and a higher rate might restore participation. METR lists 'possibly pay higher rates' as a remedy, which implies it takes this seriously.
- Rational preference rather than inability: the quotes describe not wanting to ('I really like using AI!', 'I will feel so painful', walking versus an Uber), not being unable to. The post does not distinguish preference, expected time cost and skill loss, and offers no evidence of declining ability.
- Mistaken beliefs: developers may be withholding tasks where they expect large AI gains that would not materialise, as in 2025. In that case the withheld tasks are not truly high-uplift and the downward bias is smaller than METR supposes.
- Changed population: 47 of 57 developers are new and come from 'smaller, more greenfield, and less mature repositories', where AI help plausibly differs from large mature codebases. Differences from the 2025 result may partly reflect who and what was studied, not only better tools. The 10 returning developers are themselves a self-selected subset of the original study group (the post does not say how many were invited back).
- Changed work: developers reshaped tasks around what agents do well and worked on other things while agents ran, so 'time per task' no longer measures the same thing. The estimate may be unreliable because the unit of measurement broke, independent of anyone refusing a control condition.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the primary source for the thought. It supports a narrow claim: in one voluntary, task-randomised study, enough developers avoided the no-AI condition (by not joining, by withholding tasks, by not finishing them) that METR stopped trusting the estimate. It does not support 'developers refuse to work without AI' as a general finding, and it does not show a measured speed-up. The pay cut is a confound METR admits. The honest framing is that the no-AI arm was still run, but who and what ended up in it stopped being representative.
- **Implications for measurement:** 
> Voluntary task-level randomisation against a no-AI arm degrades as tools improve, because people select out of the comparison where the gap is expected to be largest; the bias then runs against finding an effect. METR's own list is the practical guide: shorter, better-paid experiments to raise compliance; fixed tasks set by the experimenter; developer-level randomisation (less task selection, more developer selection, lower power); observational data and transcripts; carefully worded surveys with time-use studies. Its later posts add two points leaders can use: separate speed on a task from value produced, since people shift toward tasks AI makes cheap, and treat self-reported gains as an upper-leaning signal. Time per task is itself weakening as a unit when agents run in parallel, so outcome measures (work delivered, quality, review and rework) matter more than stopwatch comparisons.
- **Open questions:** 
> How many developers declined or dropped out, and of how many approached. The survey n and wording behind 30% to 50%, and why it is a range. How much reluctance is pay and how much is expected uplift. Whether withheld tasks really had high uplift. When data collection ended. Which design METR has chosen, and whether a redesigned trial is running: as of 12 October 2026 nothing on metr.org reports one.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- In a randomised controlled trial, allowing early-2025 AI tools made experienced open-source developers take 19% longer on real issues in repositories they knew well.
- The same developers believed the opposite: they forecast a 24% reduction in completion time before the tasks and estimated a 20% reduction afterwards.
- Economics experts (n=34) and machine learning experts (n=54) forecast reductions of 39% and 38% respectively, so they were further off than the developers.
- Developer forecasts were still informative about relative task length (Pearson correlation 0.64 for AI-allowed and 0.59 for AI-disallowed forecasts against actual time), so the miscalibration is specific to the effect of AI.
- Of 21 candidate factors (the arXiv abstract and blog say 20; the v2 paper body says 21), the authors find evidence that 5 contribute to the slowdown, mixed or unclear evidence for 10, and evidence against 6. Those named under 'likely to contribute' include over-optimism about AI usefulness, high developer familiarity with repositories, large and complex repositories, low AI reliability and implicit repository context.
- The authors do not claim that AI fails to speed up most developers, and say the result is a snapshot of one setting.
- The paper's discussion concludes that the result 'underscores the importance of conducting field experiments with robust outcome measures, compared to relying solely on expert forecasts or developer surveys'.

- **Sample and method:** 
> Randomised controlled trial with randomisation at the task level, within developer. 16 experienced open-source developers, recruited from the authors' professional networks and outreach to active contributors of large GitHub repositories (51 filled in an interest survey, about 20 were selected, several dropped out, 16 remained). Each supplied a list of real issues from their own repository and forecast the time for each with and without AI. Issues were then assigned by a simulated fair coin flip to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed (25 early issues used a block randomisation scheme that was abandoned). In the AI-allowed arm developers could use any tool or none; METR provided Cursor Pro subscriptions to 13 developers and a 30-minute onboarding call. In the AI-disallowed arm no generative AI was permitted, although search engines and non-LLM autocomplete were. Outcome was self-reported total implementation time, including time after pull request review, cross-checked against screen recordings. Tasks were worked on between February and June 2025, most in February and March. 246 issues were completed. Supplementary data: exit surveys and interviews, Cursor analytics, expert forecasts, and labelled screen recordings.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> A pre-specified regression estimate on 246 randomised tasks, with tasks defined before assignment. Robustness checks reported in Appendix C.3: alternative estimators including a simple ratio of means, alternative outcome measures, exclusion of the 25 differently randomised issues (slowdown of 20%), dropout subsets (slowdowns of 21% to 42%), extreme imputation of missing post-review times (14% and 23%), and screen-recording time in place of self-reported time (25% against 24% on the same subset). Compliance checked from recordings: 3 cheating instances in 54 labelled AI-disallowed recordings. Mechanism evidence from 143 hours of labelled screen recordings and developer interviews, which the authors call speculative and suggestive.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Scope: 'We caution readers against overgeneralizing on the basis of our results.' (Section 4.1)
- Setting-specific: 'many of the factors we find evidence for contributing to slowdown are specific to the setting we study—these results do not imply that current AI systems are not useful in many realistic, economically relevant settings.' (Section 1)
- Not a forecast: 'these results do not imply that future models will not speed up developers in this exact setting' (Section 1)
- Elicitation: better prompting, scaffolding or fine-tuning 'could yield positive speedup in this setting' (Section 1). The blog adds that Cursor 'does not sample many tokens from LLMs'.
- Artefacts: 'While we can't completely rule out the impact of experimental artifacts, the slowdown effect appears broadly robust across a wide range of experimental design decisions.' (Section 1)
- Sample size and power: the blog FAQ says 246 issues give '(just enough) sufficient statistical power'. The paper says it is 'not powered for statistically significant multiple comparisons when subsetting our data' and calls the factor analysis 'speculative, suggestive evidence'.
- Selection into the study: 'it's possible that there is systematic bias in which developers agree to participate', specifically that heavy AI users may decline because they would lose AI on half their tasks (Appendix C.2.5). The blog FAQ: 'No developer reports thinking in this way, but we can't rule this (or other sampling biases) out.'
- Learning effects: no clear effect across the first 30 to 50 hours of Cursor use, but 'we can't rule out learning effects beyond 50 hours of Cursor usage' (blog FAQ). Only 44% had used Cursor before.
- Self-report: the outcome is self-reported time, and qualitative evidence was partly gathered with targeted questions, 'so we cannot rule out bias from this type of qualitative evidence' (Section 2.4).
- Non-random sample: 'our sampling of developers is non-random and treatment is assigned at the issue level' (Appendix D.2).
- Dropout: 13 assigned issues were not completed (7 AI-disallowed, 6 AI-allowed) and 24 were intentionally dropped; 3 developers were removed from the study and their issues excluded (Appendix C.3.3 and G).
- Screen-recording labels cover a non-random subset, which 'may bias estimates of time allocation' (Appendix G.8).
- The blog's Table 2 lists four things the study does not show: that AI does not speed up many or most developers, that AI does not help outside software, that near-future AI will not help in this setting, and that existing AI cannot be used more effectively in this setting.
- **Counter evidence:** 
- METR's own follow-up (February 24, 2026): in a continuation begun in August 2025 with 57 developers, 143 repositories and 800+ tasks at $50/hour, the estimate for the 10 returning developers was -18% (interval -38% to +9%) and for new recruits -4% (interval -15% to +9%), negative meaning less time with AI. Both intervals include zero. METR says 'we believe it is likely that developers are more sped up from AI tools now — in early 2026 — compared to our estimates from early 2025', but calls the data 'only very weak evidence for the size of this increase'.
- METR now labels the 2025 results 'out of date' on the original blog post.
- Within the paper: the one developer with more than 50 hours of Cursor experience showed positive speedup, though footnote 16 notes a second such developer was slowed, which moves that bucket to roughly 0%.
- Earlier studies cited in the paper found speedups on synthetic tasks: Peng et al. 56%, Paradis et al. 21%, and Weber et al. a 65% increase in requirements satisfied. These were read only as the paper describes them, not opened.
- The interval of +2% to +39% only just excludes zero, and the paper says a hierarchical bootstrap that resamples developers gives 'somewhat wider confidence intervals'.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Selection: developers who gain most from AI may have declined to join, which would bias towards slowdown. The authors list this as unclear in 2025; by 2026 they treat it as the main reason the design no longer works.
- Tool learning curve: most developers were new to Cursor, and benefits may appear only after far more hours than the study covered. The blog raises 'strong learning effects ... that only appear after several hundred hours of usage'.
- Experimentally driven overuse: developers may have used AI more than they otherwise would because the task was in the AI-allowed arm. Listed as unclear; developers reporting overuse saw similar slowdown.
- Scope creep: AI-allowed issues had 47% more lines of code per forecasted hour, so developers may have done more rather than worked slower. The authors rate this unclear, since developers who reported scope creep were slowed less.
- Trading speed for ease: developers may accept being slower because AI-assisted work feels less effortful. Reports were mixed.
- Task mix: issues were capped at about two hours and exclude non-programming work, so they may not match normal work.
- The blog's three reconciliations with benchmarks and anecdotes: the RCT underestimates capability, benchmarks and anecdotes overestimate it, or each measures a different part of the real task distribution.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Central. This is the best-known randomised no-AI control for experienced engineers, and its own authors report that the control arm stopped being runnable within a year. The 2025 paper flagged the risk in an appendix (one developer said they would not take part again because they did not want to lose AI on half their work). By February 2026 METR reported 'a significant increase in developers choosing not to participate in the study because they do not wish to work without AI' and that 30% to 50% of developers were withholding tasks they did not want to do without AI. It also supplies the reason leaders cannot fall back on asking people: developers who were 19% slower believed they were 20% faster.
- **Open questions:** 
- Whether the 19% slowdown held outside this population in early 2025; the study does not test ordinary company engineers.
- How large the effect is with current tools; METR says its newer data is too selected to say.
- Whether the perception gap persists with newer tools and more experienced users.
- The exact confidence level and standard-error method behind the +2% to +39% interval as quoted in the 2026 update.
- Whether the paper has since been peer-reviewed or published in a venue.
- Why the abstract says 20 factors and the v2 body 21.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Peng 2023: in a one-off lab-style task (write an HTTP server in JavaScript), freelancers given Copilot finished 55.8% faster than a control group without it. The authors call it, to their knowledge, the first controlled experiment on AI tool productivity in professional software development.
- Cui et al.: across three company-run field experiments with 4,867 developers, using Copilot raised weekly completed pull requests by 26.08% (SE 10.3%). Less experienced developers adopted more and gained more. Each experiment alone is noisy.
- Paradis 2024: 96 Google engineers on one enterprise-grade C++ task were about 21% faster with three AI features than with them switched off, but the regression estimate is not significant at the 5% level.
- METR Feb 2026: the task-randomised follow-up to its 2025 trial gives 'an unreliable signal' because developers decline to take part, hold back tasks, or fail to complete tasks rather than work without AI. METR is changing the design.
- Shen and Tamkin 2026: in a randomised learning experiment, the AI group scored 17% lower on a quiz about a new library, with no statistically significant speed gain in the main study.
- Murphy-Hill 2026: Microsoft's own early-2026 study of Claude Code and Copilot CLI does not randomise at all. It estimates a 24% rise in merged pull requests among adopters from a synthetic control built from engineers who never used either tool.
- **Headline figures:** 
- Peng: '55.8% reduction in completion time'. Treated mean 71.17 minutes against control mean 160.89 minutes, conditional on finishing. 95% CI [21%, 89%], p = 0.0017. Comparison group: no Copilot, internet search allowed. Data May to June 2022. My arithmetic check: (160.89 - 71.17) / 160.89 = 55.8%.
- Peng: 95 recruited, 45 treated and 50 control. Success rate 7 percentage points higher for treated, not significant (95% CI [-0.11, 0.25]). Participants in both groups guessed a 35% gain.
- Cui: '26.08% increase (SE: 10.3%) in completed tasks' (weekly pull requests), pooled precision-weighted IV estimate, 4,867 developers. By firm: Microsoft 27.38% (SE 12.88), Accenture 17.94% (SE 18.72), anonymous company 54.03% (SE 42.63). Only the Microsoft estimate is significant on its own.
- Cui: commits +13.55% (SE 10.0%), builds +38.38% (SE 12.55%). Including a second, abandoned Accenture experiment the pull-request figure falls to 21.34% (SE 9.92%).
- Cui, Microsoft adoption: 8.5% of the treated group signed up in the first two weeks; 42.5% after reminder emails in February 2023; by January 2024 adoption was 75.6% treated against 64.0% control.
- Paradis: AI group 96 minutes (N = 47, SE 9.3) against 114 minutes (N = 46, SE 8.1) for no-AI; t-test on log time p = .038. Regression estimate 'about 21%' (β = -0.24, 95% CI [-0.51, 0.03], p = 0.086); a second model gives 26%. Data June to July 2024.
- METR 2026 (sign convention is METR's, negative means less time): returning developers -18% (CI -38% to +9%), new developers -4% (CI -15% to +9%). 57 developers, 143 repos, 800+ tasks. Pay $50 an hour, down from $150. 30% to 50% of developers said they withheld some tasks.
- Shen and Tamkin: 17% lower quiz score, 'two grade points' (Cohen's d = 0.738, p = 0.010), main study n = 52.
- Murphy-Hill 2026 (observational): '+24.0% lift in PRs/engineer/day' [95% CI +14.5%, +33.7%], post-period 5 January to 29 April 2026.
- **Sample and method:** 
- PENG 2023. Unit of randomisation: individual developer, between subjects, one fixed task. No-AI control: yes (no Copilot, web search and Stack Overflow allowed). Data: 15 May to 20 June 2022, before Copilot's general release. Sample: 95 Upwork freelancers (45 treated, 50 control), mostly aged 25 to 34, mostly from India and Pakistan. Trouble keeping a control: none reported. The only compliance problem ran the other way: five treated participants never finished Copilot sign-up and started without it.
- CUI ET AL., MICROSOFT. Unit: mixed. 616 developers randomised individually, 1,130 by team (average team 6.2). No-AI control: yes, control had no Copilot access. Data: first week of September 2022 to 3 May 2023, with outcomes followed into 2024. Sample: 1,746 developers, 50.4% treated. Trouble keeping a control: yes. The experiment ended because control developers sought access; a few controls got access anyway; 0.5% of controls had adopted early on.
- CUI ET AL., ACCENTURE. Unit: individual developer. No-AI control: yes, until December 2023. Data: last week of July 2023 to December 2023, adoption tracked to April 2024. Sample: 320 developers in Southeast Asia, 61.3% treated. Trouble: control given access after about four months; a separate second Accenture experiment was abandoned after a layoff hit 42% of participants.
- CUI ET AL., ANONYMOUS FORTUNE 100 FIRM. Unit: team, with randomised access dates (staggered rollout). No lasting no-AI control: everyone got access within about six weeks, so the comparison is early against late. Data: September to October 2023 rollout. Sample: 3,054 developers. Trouble: the design never tried to hold a control group; the authors say differences in treatment status lasted 'only a short time'.
- CUI ET AL., METHOD. Company-run rollouts, analysed afterwards (the trial was post-registered). Because many treated developers never used the tool and controls later did, the 26% is an instrumental-variable estimate of the effect on those who used it, weighted towards weeks when treated and control adoption differed most.
- PARADIS 2024 (GOOGLE). Unit: individual developer, between subjects, one fixed task of up to about three hours. No-AI control: yes, by instruction. Control participants were told to disable three AI features that were already in production for all staff. Data: recruited June to July 2024. Sample: 96 full-time Google engineers, 48 per arm, 93 completed. Trouble keeping a control: none reported, and the paper does not say whether it checked that control participants kept the features off.
- METR FOLLOW-UP (reported Feb 2026). Unit: task, within developer. Developers nominate their own tasks, then each is assigned AI-allowed or AI-disallowed. Data: from August 2025. Sample: 57 open-source developers (10 returning, 47 new). Trouble keeping a control: yes, on three fronts. Developers declined to join, withheld tasks, and were less likely to finish AI-disallowed tasks.
- SHEN AND TAMKIN 2026. Unit: individual, between subjects, fixed tasks, about one hour. No-AI control: yes. Sample: 52 crowd-recruited programmers, paid a flat $150. Data-collection dates are not stated in the sections read. Trouble keeping a control: yes, in pilots. 35% of the no-AI group used AI on the first platform and about 25% still did after explicit warnings; the main study moved platforms and used screen recordings to verify compliance.
- MURPHY-HILL 2026 (MICROSOFT). Not randomised. Self-selected adopters compared with a synthetic control of never-users. Pre-period 1 October 2024 to 4 January 2026, post-period 5 January to 29 April 2026.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> Peng: timestamps from GitHub Classroom on a test-suite-graded task, one t-test. Cui: GitHub telemetry (pull requests, commits, builds) at developer-week level, two-stage least squares with assignment as the instrument. Paradis: IDE-logged time on task, t-test and linear regressions with self-reported covariates. METR: self-recorded task times under task-level random assignment, plus surveys and interviews with participants about what they held back. Shen and Tamkin: a pre-registered quiz and platform transcripts, with screen recordings. Murphy-Hill: Azure DevOps pull-request counts and a Bayesian structural time-series model.
- **Figure provenance:** 
- 55.8%: Peng et al. abstract and Results. It is a reduction in completion time, not a 55.8% rise in output. Later papers restate it loosely: Cui et al. write 'the 58% decrease Peng et al. (2023) find', Paradis et al. write 'a 56% speed increase', Shen and Tamkin write '55.5% faster'. Use 55.8% and call it a cut in time taken.
- Peng dates: the text says the experiment 'began on May 15, 2022' but the Figure 1 note says the Upwork job posting started 'May 25th 2022'. The paper does not reconcile these.
- Peng completers: 'Thirty-five developers from both the treated and control groups completed the task and survey' is ambiguous (35 in each group, or 35 in total). Do not cite a completion count.
- 26% / 26.08%: Cui et al., pooled weighted-IV estimate for pull requests. The standard error is 10.3%, so a rough 95% range is about 6% to 46%. 'Tasks' means pull requests. It is the effect on developers who took up the tool, not on everyone offered it. An alternative pooled figure of 21.34% appears when the abandoned second Accenture experiment is included.
- Cui abstract wording changed between versions: the February 2025 draft says 'Though each experiment is noisy'; the Management Science abstract says 'Although each experiment is noisy and results vary across experiments'.
- Cui internal date inconsistencies: Microsoft 'ended on May 3rd, 2023' in section 2.2 but 'the conclusion of the experiment in April 2023' in section 3; the anonymous firm 'started in October 2023' but invites went out 'between September 2023 and October 2023'.

The significant result is the unadjusted t-test (96 against 114 minutes, about 16% less time by my arithmetic). The paper's text gives mean log times that look swapped between groups (AI 4.6, no-AI 4.3), which reads as a typo.
- METR -18% and -4%: METR's post, where negative means tasks took less time. METR itself says the estimate is unreliable.
- **Population and transfer:** 
> Peng: low-income freelancers on a greenfield toy task; weak transfer to staff engineers in existing codebases, as Paradis et al. point out. Cui: ordinary employees doing their real jobs at three large firms, the best transfer in the set, but the tool is 2022 to 2023 autocomplete Copilot, not agents. Paradis: Google engineers on Google's internal tools and monorepo; realistic task, single company. METR: experienced open-source maintainers on their own repositories. Shen and Tamkin: crowd-recruited programmers learning an unfamiliar library in an hour. None of the randomised estimates covers agentic tools in ordinary company work.
- **Outcome measured:** 
> Task speed: Peng, Paradis, METR (time to complete). On-the-job output: Cui (weekly pull requests, commits, builds, build success rate), Murphy-Hill (merged pull requests). Immediate quiz score: Shen and Tamkin. None measures employment, postings or delayed retention.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Peng: 'This study examines a standardized programming task in an experiment to obtain a precise measure of productivity, instead of a task where developers collaborate on large projects'; and 'this study does not examine the effects of AI on code quality.'
- Cui: 'While standard errors are consistently large and the effect sizes differ across the three different companies'; low power from 'factors that reduce the take-up and duration of the three experiments'; implementation 'rather ad-hoc'; around 30% to 40% of engineers never tried the tool.
- Paradis: 'There may be external validity constraints on our estimates given how participants were all Google employees'; 'We cannot, however, fully eliminate the risk associated with us evaluating our own team's developer tools'; 'a "true estimate" of the contribution of AI tools to developer speed will be a moving target.'
- METR: 'our data is only very weak evidence for the size of this increase'; pay cut from $150 to $50 an hour 'also likely contributed to the higher selection'; self-reported time is unreliable for developers running several agents at once.
- Shen and Tamkin: one chat-based task over one hour; participants had 'not the same incentive to learn the library as if it were required for their actual job'.
- Murphy-Hill: merged PRs are 'an imperfect proxy for throughput'; adopters self-select; one company, one early-2026 window.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Against a blanket claim that the no-AI control cannot be run: Peng (2022) and Paradis (2024) report no difficulty at all, and Shen and Tamkin ran a clean no-AI arm in 2026 once they used screen recordings. All three are short, fixed-task sessions lasting hours. The trouble shows up where the control must last weeks or months inside real work (Cui's Microsoft arm in 2022 to 2023, METR in 2025 to 2026). Against reading METR's problem as new: Cui's Microsoft control group was already asking for access by spring 2023, so pressure on the control predates agentic tools. Against the headline gains themselves: Paradis's adjusted estimate is not significant, two of Cui's three firm-level estimates are not significant, and Murphy-Hill cites field experiments that found 'no significant change in merged pull requests'.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
- Cui's Microsoft control ended for business reasons as much as developer demand: the firms ran these trials 'to decide whether or how extensively to adopt these technologies', so ending the hold-out once the answer looked positive is ordinary management, not evidence of dependence. The paper says controls 'seek access', which shows interest, not inability to work without the tool.
- Paradis's lack of reported non-compliance may mean nobody checked. The control was an instruction to disable features, and the sections read describe no verification.
- Shen and Tamkin's pilot non-compliance is about paid crowd workers taking a shortcut on a timed task, a different thing from professional developers refusing unassisted work.
- METR's selection could be driven partly by the pay cut and by the task-level design, which lets developers see and avoid the no-AI draw. METR says so itself.
- Fixed-task trials avoid the problem partly because participants cannot choose which work enters the study, not because developers are happy to work without AI.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Direct. Of the randomised trials checked, the two that needed a no-AI control to hold for months inside developers' own work both report losing it: Microsoft's control group sought access and the trial ended (May 2023), and METR's developers avoided the no-AI condition (2025 to 2026). The short fixed-task trials (Peng, Google, Shen and Tamkin) kept theirs. So the accurate claim is narrower than 'the control group is gone': long, real-work controls are failing, and one-sitting lab controls still work but need policing. Microsoft's own 2026 study of agentic tools dropped randomisation for a synthetic control.
- **Implications for measurement:** 
- Short fixed-task experiments with a no-AI arm are still feasible, but compliance has to be verified (Shen and Tamkin needed screen recordings) and the result says little about real work.
- Staggered rollout is the design firms can actually run: everyone gets the tool, the order is random, and early is compared with late (Cui's anonymous firm). The window is short and the estimate noisy (54% with a standard error of 43).
- Expect imperfect compliance and plan the analysis for it. Cui's 26% is an effect on those who took the tool up, estimated with assignment as an instrument, because many treated developers did not use it and controls later did.
- Where randomisation is not possible, the fallback seen in 2026 is observational: never-users as a synthetic control, with a placebo test (Murphy-Hill). The authors state the self-selection risk plainly.
- A leader cannot cite a current randomised number for agentic tools in ordinary company work. The randomised evidence with a clean control is from 2022 to 2024 and from autocomplete-era tools.
- **Open questions:** 
> Whether the Management Science final text changed any design description from the February 2025 draft. Whether Google verified that control participants kept AI features off. How many completers Peng had in each arm. Whether any other randomised developer-productivity trial with a no-AI control reported results in 2026. Whether Microsoft's control group 'seeking access' reflected developer demand or a management decision; the paper gives one sentence and no counts.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> WHAT METR'S TEXT SAYS. (a) The new experiment gives 'an unreliable signal'. (b) The raw results 'show some evidence for speedup': returning developers -18% (CI -38% to +9%), new recruits -4% (CI -15% to +9%). (c) METR believes developers are likely more sped up in early 2026 than early 2025, on the basis of conversations with participants, and calls its own data 'only very weak evidence for the size of this increase'. (d) The estimate is likely a lower bound and 'a bad proxy'. (e) Selection affects 'a minority share of developers and of tasks'. (f) The pay cut 'also likely contributed'. THE SIGN. -18% is a speed-up: tasks took an estimated 18% less time with AI allowed. Three things in METR's own material settle it. The same paragraph gives the 2025 result as tasks taking '19% longer' with an interval of +2% to +39%, so positive means more time. The paragraph opens 'Our raw results show some evidence for speedup', and the next sentence says the true speedup 'could be much higher'. METR's published regression.py computes the figure as exp(beta) - 1, where beta is the coefficient on the AI-allowed dummy in a regression of log implementation time. A negative value means less time with AI. METR's phrase 'a speedup of -18%' is loose wording for a -18% change in time, and it is what caused the confusion. WHO READ IT AS A SPEED-UP (correct on sign): Eric Brown ('an 18% speedup for returning developers'), urandom.io ('flipped to an 18% speedup'), AgentMarketCap ('a speedup of 18%'), CoderCops ('roughly 18% faster'), and TFD, who re-ran METR's public data and got a 6% to 7% sample-wide speed-up, noting 'negative values indicate speedups'. Most of these add that METR flagged the number as unreliable. WHO READ IT AS A SLOWDOWN (wrong on sign): Particula Tech (Mondragon) says 'a negative speedup is a slowdown', that the 2025 figure was 'about -19%' and that the estimate was 'essentially flat'. This contradicts METR's +2% to +39% interval for 2025 and METR's 'some evidence for speedup'. braindetox reports, without naming them, that one Korean-language blog printed '18% slowdown, -9% to +38%' and another paired 'slowdown' with the original interval. WHO DECLINED TO READ A DIRECTION: Rob Bowley does not call -18% a slowdown. He says posts claiming 'an 18% productivity boost' misrepresent an article whose point is that the data is too compromised to use, and he accepts that 'METR believe GenAI coding productivity is improving'. braindetox says the sign is ambiguous because METR gave no formula (it did not check the public regression script). Birchtree quotes no number; it says early findings 'seem to indicate that the uptick in productivity is actually pretty significant now'. WHERE COMMENTATORS GO BEYOND METR. Bowley: 'Developers are now so reliant on AI that they won't work without it' and 'everyone uses it now'. METR's text is narrower: 30% to 50% withheld some tasks, an 'increased share' would not do half their work without AI, and selection affects a minority. METR never uses 'reliant' or 'dependent'; it attributes the behaviour to 'higher expectation of AI-driven uplift' plus lower pay. Bowley does not mention the pay cut. Bowley also says METR 'couldn't complete' the experiment; METR says the data is unreliable and that it is changing the design, and it published 800+ tasks of data. Birchtree: 'pretty significant' overstates 'only very weak evidence', and the claim that the new study used 'Claude Code with Opus 4.5' is not in METR's post, which names only 'agentic tools ... such as Claude Code and Codex'. Eric Brown: developers 'started refusing to participate in tasks where AI wasn't allowed' and 'the refusal is the data point' is his interpretation; he omits the pay cut. Particula: 'refused to run the no-AI arm' and 'refused to do the no-AI half of the experiment' misdescribe what METR reports, which is tasks not being submitted before randomisation.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> METR: 'unreliable signal'; 'only very weak evidence for the size of this increase'; 'likely a bad proxy for the real productivity impact'; self-reported speedups 'can be quite unreliable'; time measurements 'unreliable for the fraction of developers who use multiple AI agents concurrently'; selection 'primarily due to higher expectation of AI-driven uplift' but lower pay 'also likely contributed'. Repo README: 'interpret these results with a major grain of salt'. Bowley: such studies are 'a narrow window on software delivery'. Birchtree: 'they don't have conclusive results'. TFD: the heuristic 'can't truly "correct" for any selection' and 'None of these numbers should be taken as my personal best-guess'. Particula and Brown state no limits on their own readings.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Against the slowdown reading: METR's own sentence 'Our raw results show some evidence for speedup', the +2% to +39% interval attached to '19% longer', the exp(beta) - 1 estimand in regression.py, and TFD's independent rerun giving a speed-up. Against the confident speed-up reading: both intervals include zero (-38% to +9%, -15% to +9%), and METR calls the central estimate a bad proxy. Against 'developers won't work without AI': METR says selection affects 'a minority share of developers and of tasks', gives no count of developers who declined to take part, and names the cut from $150 to $50 an hour as a contributor. Against Bowley's 'I don't see how you can practically design an effective controlled experiment': METR lists six options, including shorter, better-paid experiments, fixed-task experiments and developer-level randomisation, while noting the last worsens developer-level selection and has lower power.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> The reluctance may be a pay effect: at a third of the earlier rate, fewer developers accept the inconvenience of a no-AI assignment, whatever their attachment to the tools. METR says it believes expectations are the main driver, on interviews and surveys, but gives no decomposition. Preferring AI may be a rational choice about expected time saved; the developer quotes ('AI can finish things in just 2 hours, but I have to spend 20 hours') read that way, and no commentator opened here separates preference from dependence. The move from +19% to -18% among returning developers may reflect ten self-selected people out of the original cohort, better tools, more practice, or noise within overlapping uncertainty. Developers' expectations of uplift were shown to be overoptimistic in the 2025 study, so tasks withheld for 'high expected uplift' need not have high actual uplift; METR asserts the bias runs downward but this is an inference, not a measurement.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Central as a caution. The update is the main evidence that the no-AI control is getting harder to run, and the commentary shows two opposite ways to get it wrong: reading -18% as a slowdown (Particula), and inflating '30% to 50% withheld some tasks' into 'developers won't work without AI' (Bowley, Brown). METR's own list is more specific: shorter, better-paid experiments; observational data; careful questionnaires with time-use studies; fixed-task experiments; developer-level randomisation with its stated costs. The sign muddle carries its own lesson: report an effect as time change or as speed-up, state which, and give the interval.

> Whether anyone at METR has publicly corrected the slowdown reading. How many developers declined to take part at all (METR gives no count). The survey question and n behind '30% to 50%'. How much of the selection is pay. Whether any economist or software-engineering academic has published a response; none was found. Which specific posts Bowley was rebutting (he names none). What pooled estimate METR itself would report (only TFD's 6% to 7% rerun exists).

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Supports the background claim that AI use is now the default: 68% to 73% daily use, under 1% of Stack Overflow's AI-question respondents reporting none. That makes a no-AI arm unrepresentative of normal work and harder to recruit for. It does not show that developers refuse to work without AI. No survey asks that, so METR's 30% to 50% remains the only figure on tasks actually withheld, and the thought should say so.
- **Implications for measurement:** 
> Surveys can tell leaders how much AI is used and how it is trusted, not what it does to output. Three usable points. First, with daily use this common, a clean without-AI comparison inside one team is closer to a deprivation test than a baseline, so before-and-after or between-team comparisons on delivery records are more realistic than a control group. Second, self-estimates (CoderPad's 54%, Sonar's 35% average productivity boost) should not stand in for measurement. Third, the surveys point at what is worth measuring instead: verification effort (Sonar: 38% say reviewing AI code takes more effort, 48% always check) and codebase understanding (53% worried), which delivery speed alone will miss.
- **Open questions:**
