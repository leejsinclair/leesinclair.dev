## 1. Simon Armitage, Gilgamesh: A New Verse Translation (2026)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Gilgamesh: A New Verse Translation

**Date**

> Liveright (W. W. Norton), New York, 14 April 2026, 224 pp., ISBN 9781631496684; Faber & Faber, London, UK edition 8 October 2026 (ISBN 9780571357246)

**Creator**

> Simon Armitage (UK Poet Laureate, professor of poetry at the University of Leeds), working with Oxford Assyriologist Jacob L. Dahl

**Form**

> Verse translation in loose four-beat lines, with short passages of justified prose for material outside the Standard Babylonian version, prose outlines heading each tablet, and about 40 pages of front matter (Introduction plus 'A Note on the Translation')

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Armitage does not read Akkadian. He worked from a translation of the cuneiform prepared by Jacob L. Dahl and read through a transliteration to get a feel for the sound (LARB, Nicolas Liney). He has said poetry should be left to poets and called Benjamin Foster's objection to non-Assyriologist translators 'disheartening and discouraging'. He first read the whole poem in Stephen Mitchell's 2004 version (Pittsburgh Review of Books interview).

**Scholarly adviser:** Jacob L. Dahl, Assyriologist, University of Oxford

**Non standard material**

> Marked typographically: material taken from tablets other than the Standard Version is set as brief blocks of justified prose rather than verse (Asymptote, Jordan Spector; LARB). This is the only distinct typographic device apart from the prose outlines at the start of each tablet.

**Gap policy**

> Bridged, not displayed. Armitage writes that it is 'absolutely right to recognize the poem's inherent incompleteness' but not improper 'to favor readability and a sense of poetic wholeness over the worship of its accidental breakages.' He frames the work as 'a poem in a permanent state of evolution' and says 'All translation is an illusion. All translation is a compromise.'

### Reading of the Arc

**Arc interpretation**

> Primarily grief and the fear of death: LARB (Liney) reads it with Rilke as 'das Epos der Todesfurcht', and Armitage's introduction says 'Thousands of years later death is no less bewildering to humankind.' Secondary strands: love between men (publisher copy; a Southern Bookseller Review blurb calls it 'proto-bromance', Asymptote reads the friendship through tragic-romance structure while noting Armitage treats a sexual reading as debatable), and a closing turn to community, since the final walls of Uruk loop back to the opening and place Gilgamesh within the city rather than alone (LARB). LARB also flags the Cedar Forest episode as an ecological and imperial problem for modern readers.

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Loose four-beat accentual lines, compact like the Akkadian, moving the narrative while 'lingering over each line' (LARB). 'Archaizing without being antiquated' (LARB); 'rhythmic contemporary English' (publisher). Keeps the source's verbatim repetition, e.g. the 23-line dream-journey block repeated five times in Tablet IV (OC Review of Books). Vivid concrete imagery: 'ramparts as straight as woolen thread', 'a savage young brute at home in the outlands'.

**Sample line**

> "his voice is a raging torrent, / his mouth is the fire god, his breath is death" (Humbaba; quoted in Eric Bies, Orange County Review of Books, 19 April 2026)

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Strongly positive. William Giraldi, Wall Street Journal (24 April 2026): 'a reanimation, a voice tugged up from the clay and made to speak again', 'a poet's mastery of rhythm and rupture', 'an epic that breathes, raggedly, unevenly, but thrillingly alive'. Nicolas Liney, Los Angeles Review of Books: 'unhurried and resplendent, archaizing without being antiquated'. Eric Bies, Orange County Review of Books: 'our best, most lucid and fluent Gilgamesh to date', a classroom rival to Andrew George's Penguin. Jordan Spector, Asymptote (2 March 2026): 'Nobody owns Gilgamesh, because everybody owns Gilgamesh.' David Luhrssen, Shepherd Express: achieves 'a historically informed version that speaks to the widest readership'. Victor Mair reposted the WSJ praise on Language Log.

**Controversies**

> The poet-versus-Assyriologist question: Benjamin Foster (Yale) wrote in World Literature Today that he has 'no patience with clueless folk who think that they can translate the epic without going to the trouble of mastering Babylonian'; Armitage, who works from Dahl's crib, called this disheartening and argued poets should handle the poetry. A secondary liberty question is his choice to smooth over lacunae for readability. No provenance disputes reported.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> No machine involvement is reported; the book is a clean example of the older division of labour that AI now mimics. A specialist (Dahl) supplies the literal crib, the poet supplies rhythm, voice and wholeness, and the argument over whether that is legitimate (Foster vs Armitage) is the same one now raised about machine translation of cuneiform: who can claim a text they cannot read in the original. Armitage's defence ('All translation is an illusion. All translation is a compromise'; favouring 'poetic wholeness over the worship of its accidental breakages') marks what still needs a human: judgement about what to smooth, where to break into prose, and how a line should sound. His own nod to the Sulaymaniyah fragment shows new finds keep changing the base text he works on.

**Essay hooks**

> (1) Armitage cannot read Akkadian and worked from Jacob Dahl's crib, yet the WSJ called the result 'a voice tugged up from the clay' (Giraldi, WSJ, April 2026). (2) Foster: 'no patience with clueless folk who think that they can translate the epic without going to the trouble of mastering Babylonian' (World Literature Today, via LARB). (3) 'the worship of its accidental breakages': Armitage's phrase for faithfully showing every gap. (4) No rows of dots: gaps become justified prose, a quiet typographic confession of repair (Asymptote). (5) 'All translation is an illusion. All translation is a compromise.' (Pittsburgh Review of Books). (6) Tablet IV repeats 23 lines five times, kept verbatim (OC Review of Books). (7) 'Nobody owns Gilgamesh, because everybody owns Gilgamesh' (Asymptote).

## 2. N. K. Sandars, The Epic of Gilgamesh (Penguin, 1960)

### Basic Info

**Name:** The Epic of Gilgamesh: An English Version with an Introduction (N. K. Sandars, Penguin Classics)

**Date**

> First published by Penguin Classics in 1960 (Harmondsworth); reprinted with revisions 1964 and 1972; reissued as a Penguin Epic in 2006 and as an ebook. Still in print alongside Andrew George's 1999 Penguin translation.

**Creator**

> Nancy Katharine Sandars (1914-2015), British archaeologist and prehistorian, independent scholar, later a published poet. Commissioned by Allen Lane's Penguin, probably through the Mallowan/Agatha Christie circle and her friend Rachel Maxwell-Hyslop (British Academy memoir).

**Form**

> Continuous English prose narrative, divided into a Prologue and seven chapters (Prologue: Gilgamesh King in Uruk; 1 The Coming of Enkidu; 2 The Forest Journey; 3 Ishtar and Gilgamesh, and the Death of Enkidu; 4 The Search for Everlasting Life; 5 The Story of the Flood; 6 The Return; 7 The Death of Gilgamesh), with a long introduction and glossary of names.

### Source Handling

**Versions used**

> A conflation: Standard Babylonian (Nineveh) tablets as the backbone, supplemented by Old Babylonian fragments, the Hittite version, and the Sumerian Gilgamesh poems (notably Gilgamesh and Huwawa for the forest journey and the Sumerian 'Death of Gilgamesh', which supplies her final chapter). Predates the Sulaymaniyah tablet (acquired 2011) and most modern joins.

**Non standard material**

> Silent merge. Old Babylonian, Hittite and Sumerian material is woven into one continuous prose story with no brackets, italics or marginal flags in the narrative itself; provenance is explained only in the introduction. The memoir notes 'some rearrangings and insertions of her own devising (some dealt with in revised editions)'.

**Gap policy**

> Smoothed over. Lacunae are bridged by drawing on other versions, by paraphrase, or by her own connective prose, so the reader gets an unbroken story rather than a broken text.

**Typography of absence**

> Essentially nothing: justified prose paragraphs with no ellipses, square brackets or blank space marking missing lines. The page looks like a complete, finished tale.

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> The Death of Enkidu. Sandars fuses the Ishtar episode and Enkidu's death into a single chapter ('Ishtar and Gilgamesh, and the Death of Enkidu'), and the next chapter title, 'The Search for Everlasting Life', makes the causal hinge explicit: grief turns into fear of death and a quest. Gilgamesh's lament ('What my brother is now, that shall I be when I am dead') is the pivot line. The loss of the plant to the serpent is the second, smaller hinge that sends him home.

**Arc interpretation**

> Grief and mortality resolved into acceptance and fame. The chapter titles frame the arc as a quest for everlasting life that fails, followed by a 'Return' in which the only permanence left is the story itself: chapter 6 closes by repeating the prologue's claim that he 'returning engraved on a stone the whole story', and chapter 7 opens with Enlil's decree that none 'will leave a monument for generations to come to compare with his'. With Tablet XII gone and the Sumerian death poem added, the reading tilts from failure toward consolation and memorial kingship. Siduri's advice is presented as a clear moral centre ('for this too is the lot of man').

**Key passages**

> Shamhat and Enkidu: the woman is unnamed, 'the harlot', and the scene is frank but decorous: 'She was not ashamed to take him, she made herself naked and welcomed his eagerness; as he lay on her murmuring love she taught him the woman's art.' Siduri: 'As for you, Gilgamesh, fill your belly with good things; day and night, night and day, dance and be merry, feast and rejoice. Let your clothes be fresh, bathe yourself in water, cherish the little child that holds your hand, and make your wife happy in your embrace; for this too is the lot of man.' Death in the bedchamber (Tablet XI): rendered as 'death inhabits my room'. The snake: 'Deep in the pool there was lying a serpent, and the serpent sensed the sweetness of the flower. It rose out of the water and snatched it away.' Walls of Uruk: 'One third of the whole is city, one third is garden, and one third is field, with the precinct of the goddess Ishtar', followed by the prologue refrain 'He was wise, he saw mysteries and knew secret things, he brought us a tale of the days before the flood.'

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> No metre and no line breaks: plain, paragraphed prose that David Jones called 'perfectly straight modern English, but not losing the poiesis'. Mild archaism in set pieces ('great is thy praise', 'nether-earth') and biblical cadence in repetitions ('day and night, night and day'), but mostly mid-century literary English.

**Sample line**

> "I will proclaim to the world the deeds of Gilgamesh. This was the man to whom all things were known; this was the king who knew the countries of the world." (Sandars, Prologue, Penguin Classics 1960)

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Hugely popular: advertised among 'Twenty-five outstanding books for Penguin's 25th Birthday' (the only ancient work on the list) and has sold over a million copies (British Academy memoir; Wikipedia). David Jones: 'It's got everything.' Scholarly judgement is that it is a readable version rather than a translation; the British Academy memoir notes 'some misunderstandings and some rearrangings and insertions of her own devising'. Andrew George credits it as the book that drew him, as a schoolboy, into Assyriology; his 1999 Penguin translation effectively succeeded it as the scholarly standard.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Sandars is a near-perfect pre-machine analogue of an LLM retelling: she could not read the source, synthesised a fluent whole from other people's translations, filled gaps with plausible prose of her own, and presented the result with no visible seams. It worked, a million copies and a generation of readers, but at the cost that the reader cannot see where the ancient text stops and the reteller begins. The human contribution that mattered was editorial and narrative judgement (the chapter shape, the choice to end on the Sumerian death poem, the ring back to the walls and the engraved stone). What it lacked, and what later scholars supplied, was the ability to check against the tablets and mark uncertainty honestly.

**Essay hooks**

> (1) 'Nancy did not read the original languages. She worked from translations' yet sold over a million copies (British Academy memoir, 2020). (2) The book inspired a schoolboy, Andrew George, to learn Akkadian; he later replaced it with his own Penguin translation (1999) and the standard critical edition (2003): a fluent synthesis produced the expert who superseded it. (3) The page has no brackets or ellipses at all: the gaps are there, you just cannot see them. (4) David Jones praising it without knowing he was lunching with its author: 'perfectly straight modern English, but not losing the poiesis'. (5) The only ancient work on Penguin's 25th-birthday list of 25 outstanding books.

## 3. Herbert Mason, Gilgamesh: A Verse Narrative (1970)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Gilgamesh: A Verse Narrative

**Date**

> Copyright 1970 (Houghton Mifflin, Boston); hardcover commonly dated 1971; Mentor/New American Library paperback April 1972 with an afterword by John H. Marks; Mariner/Houghton Mifflin reissue 2003. Finalist, 1972 National Book Award for Translation.

**Creator**

> Herbert Mason (born 1932), poet and later University Professor of History and Religious Thought at Boston University, best known otherwise as translator of Louis Massignon's The Passion of al-Hallaj. Afterword (1972 paperback) by John H. Marks, Princeton Old Testament scholar.

**Form**

> Free-verse narrative in four numbered parts (I to IV), with unrhymed lines of irregular length grouped in verse paragraphs and '***' section breaks. Back matter: a glossary ('Names and Places Appearing in the Narrative'), an essay 'About the Gilgamesh', an 'Autobiographical Postscript', and the Marks afterword. A retelling, not a line-by-line translation.

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Mason did not work from the cuneiform. His postscript calls the book 'a subjective evocation' 'based on literal scholarly translations', and the Marks afterword states that 'the author makes no claim to present an accurate rendering of the cuneiform text.' His entry point was E. A. Speiser's English translation, read in 1954 in Albert Lord's Harvard course on oral epic. He later took a Harvard doctorate in Near Eastern Languages and Literatures (his field was Arabic and Islamic mysticism), but the book itself is built from existing English, German and French versions.

**Scholarly adviser**

> No single named crib-supplier. Sources are the published translations of Heidel, Speiser, Campbell Thompson, Ebeling and Contenau. Personal influences named in the postscript: Albert Lord (Harvard), Louis Massignon (Collège de France), Père Henri Cazelles (Institut Catholique, Paris), Madeleine David. John H. Marks supplied the scholarly afterword for the 1972 edition.

**Versions used**

> The Standard Babylonian (Nineveh) epic as mediated by pre-1960 translations: Alexander Heidel, The Gilgamesh Epic and Old Testament Parallels (2nd ed., 1949); E. A. Speiser in Pritchard's Ancient Near Eastern Texts (2nd ed., 1955); R. Campbell Thompson, The Epic of Gilgamish (1928/1930); Erich Ebeling in Gressmann's Altorientalische Texte zum Alten Testament (1926); G. Contenau, L'Epopée de Gilgamesh (1939). Old Babylonian material entered only as those translators had already merged it (e.g. Siduri's advice 'The gods gave death to man and kept life for themselves' is the OB Sippar tablet speech). No Sumerian poems used directly; no post-1970 finds (Sulaymaniyah tablet etc.) since it predates them.

**Non standard material**

> Silent merge. Nothing in the poem signals which tablet or version a passage comes from, and Mason freely reorders and invents: Enlil is 'merged in the present narrative with Ninurta, the war god'; Urshanabi's role 'is greatly reduced'; Utnapishtim is made monotheistic ('The monotheism of Utnapishtim in this narration will have to be accepted by these scholars as part of any modern retelling'). He also puts invented speeches in characters' mouths, e.g. Enkidu's long deathbed meditation on the gods' drunken contest to create wretchedness, and Utnapishtim's lines 'love is wrung from our inmost heart', which echo a 1959 letter from Massignon quoted in the postscript. Only the Tablet XII material is flagged, and that is quarantined in the back-matter essay.

**Typography of absence**

> Nothing that marks textual loss. No brackets, no editorial ellipses, no blank space for missing lines. The only visual breaks are '***' scene dividers and part numerals. The single ellipsis found ('dead and . . .' in Enkidu's dying speech) is dramatic, marking his voice failing, not a lacuna.

**Tablet xii**

> Dropped from the narrative. The essay 'About the Gilgamesh' summarises Tablet XII (Enkidu's descent to retrieve lost objects, his illusory return) and the Sumerian Death of Gilgamesh, then says: 'These and other stories about Gilgamesh have not been included in the present narrative for reasons of dramatic unity.' Mason then appends a short verse 'afterglimpse' based on the Tablet XII reunion, labelled 'an interpretation only', in which Enkidu's shade tells Gilgamesh 'You must not touch me' and Gilgamesh ends by walking out toward the sound of celebration. The main poem therefore ends at the walls, and Tablet XII is recast as a grief coda rather than an underworld report.

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> Enkidu's death is the hinge, and the whole book is built around it. The opening lines announce the subject as grief rather than kingship: 'It is an old story / But one that can still be told / About a man who loved / And lost a friend to death / And learned he lacked the power / To bring him back to life.' Everything before Part III is set-up for the loss. Part III opens with 'Gilgamesh wept bitterly for his friend' and follows with Mason's longest authorial meditation, a passage on bereavement that leaves the story altogether ('All that is left to one who grieves / Is convalescence. No change of heart or spiritual / Conversion, for the heart has changed'). The quest is recast as grief-work: Gilgamesh seeks eternal life 'Which he might carry back to give his friend', a motive the ancient text does not state. The snake is a second, smaller hinge that ends the journey ('In time he recognized this loss / As the end of his journey'), but it registers as a repetition of the first loss, not a new one.

**Arc interpretation**

> Grief and acceptance, framed autobiographically. Mason's postscript ties the book to his father's death when he was seven and to the death of his Harvard friend Jack Kotteman from Hodgkin's disease in the mid-1950s; he calls the book 'for those who have known loss' and describes Gilgamesh as a story 'understood immediately by anyone who has suffered loss, a loss one has yearned to restore and finally has had to accept.' He follows Albert Lord in reading Gilgamesh as a kind of antihero whose only heroism is to keep walking. Kingship almost vanishes: after Enkidu dies 'He was no more a king / But just a man who now had lost his way.' Maturity through city-building is muted; the SB frame of the proud king showing off his walls is replaced by a man whose townspeople have forgotten his friend's name. The back-matter essay leaves the verdict open ('the reader must decide what that ending means for himself'), offering either tragic despair or the hope 'that for the human being the experience of wisdom is more important than possession of even the highest things.' The Marks afterword endorses this reading: the book is 'an attempt to convey the profound anguish Gilgamesh suffered after his constant companion and friend, Enkidu, died.'

**Key passages**

> Shamhat and Enkidu: the woman is unnamed ('the woman', later 'her'), and the scene is explicit but brief and tender: 'She took his hand and guided it / Across her breasts and between her legs / And touched him with her fingers / Gently and bent down and moistened / Him with her lips then drew him / Slowly to the ground.' Enkidu afterwards feels 'a strange exhaustion, / As if life had left his body' as the animals leave him. On his deathbed he blames her: 'She made me see / Things as a man, and a man sees death in things. / That is what it is to be a man.' Siduri's advice: kept, compressed and made maternal ('She spoke as to a child who could not understand'): 'The gods gave death to man and kept life for / Themselves. That is the only way it is. / Cherish your rests; the children you might have; / You are a thing that carries so much tiredness.' She washes and dresses him and he throws the clothes off, returning to 'the dark pelts'. Death in the bedchamber (Tablet XI): the SB line 'in my bedchamber death dwells' is not rendered as such; the nearest equivalent comes after the sleep test with the seven loaves: 'Wherever I go (even here) I am drawn back / To death.' [original uses dashes] Utnapishtim is recast as a wise, half-blind mystic who speaks of friendship ('Friendship is vowing toward immortality / And does not know the passing away of beauty'). The snake: 'A serpent had smelled its sweet fragrance and saw / Its chance to come from the water, and devoured / The plant, shedding its skin as slough... He sat / Down on the ground, and wept.' Walls of Uruk: no Urshanabi tour and no repetition of the prologue's praise of brickwork. Instead Gilgamesh asks a blind old man if he has heard the name Enkidu, is met with a shrug, and then 'He looked at the walls, / Awed at the heights / His people had achieved / And for a moment (just a moment) / All that lay behind him / Passed from view.'

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Loose free verse, mostly short to medium unrhymed lines with conversational, modern American idiom and occasional bluntness ('a man who had no balls'). No archaism, no attempt at Akkadian parallelism or repetition formulas, few epithets. Long stretches of authorial commentary and psychological narration sit alongside the plot, and Mason admits 'At times my line of narration is very prosy indeed.' Speech is often unpunctuated by quotation marks and folded into the verse. Danny Yee's comparative review groups it with the 'freer versions', noting it rearranges tablet material and uses paragraph white-space.

**Sample line**

> "It is an old story / But one that can still be told / About a man who loved / And lost a friend to death / And learned he lacked the power / To bring him back to life." (Mason, Gilgamesh: A Verse Narrative, opening of Part I)

### Reception

**Controversies**

> The main dispute is category: a book its own author calls a 'subjective evocation', not a translation, was shortlisted for a national award in Translation. Mason anticipated scholarly objection to his 'cavalier view of the gods' and to Utnapishtim's monotheism. Its liberties (invented speeches, reordered episodes, reduced Urshanabi, merged gods, excluded Tablet XII) make it unreliable as a guide to the ancient text, a point Marks's afterword concedes. No provenance or authorship dispute.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Mason is the purest case of the poet who adds nothing a machine could extract from the tablets and everything a machine could not. He worked entirely from other people's literal translations, so on the philological side he contributed zero; his value is the fifteen years of 'inward companionship and meditation' he describes, plus a specific bereavement. The book's most memorable parts are ones no source contains: Enkidu's rant about drunk gods competing to make misery, the meditation on grief as 'convalescence', the blind man who has never heard of Enkidu. A model can reproduce Speiser, and can paraphrase Speiser freely, but the reason readers still pick up Mason is that the reframing comes from a person who had lost someone and says so in a postscript. It also shows the cost: his version erases the gaps, the multiple layers of the text and the gods' theology, so it is a reading, not a record. For an essay on AI and craft, Mason stands for the claim that interpretation is authored by lived experience, and for the risk that a fluent, gap-free retelling can pass for the thing itself (here, to the point of a Translation award shortlist).

**Essay hooks**

> 1) Shortlisted for the 1972 National Book Award in Translation although the author says he could not render the cuneiform and calls it 'a subjective evocation' (NBF page; Mason's postscript). 2) Origin story: a Harvard junior in 1954 skips Albert Lord's lectures to hide in an empty room in Emerson Hall writing his own Gilgamesh in longhand; the same year a friend, Jack Kotteman, is diagnosed with Hodgkin's disease and dies within two years (Autobiographical Postscript). 3) Fifteen years in the making: 'All told it has been fifteen years since I began working on "my Gilgamesh"' (postscript). 4) Massignon's instruction: 'You must decide the end. You must finish your Gilgamesh.' (postscript). 5) The ending swap: instead of the king proudly showing off his walls, Gilgamesh asks a blind man if he knows the name Enkidu and gets a shrug (Part IV). 6) Mason's Utnapishtim speaks his mentor's letter: 'love is wrung from our inmost heart', echoing Massignon's 1959 'Love was to surrender, to be wrung from our inmost heart'. 7) 'This narrative is, in one important sense, for those who have known loss.'

## 4. Maureen Gallery Kovacs, The Epic of Gilgamesh (Stanford, 1989)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> The Epic of Gilgamesh, translated with an introduction by Maureen Gallery Kovacs (Stanford University Press)

**Creator**

> Maureen Gallery Kovacs, an Assyriologist translating directly from Akkadian; published by Stanford University Press

**Form**

> Scholarly translation in plain English prose-like lines laid out as verse (one line per cuneiform line, line numbers in print), with introduction, glossary, appendixes and bibliography (xxxiv + 122 pp., illustrated)

### Source Handling

**Scholarly adviser**

> N/A in the Armitage/Mitchell sense: Kovacs is herself the specialist, so there is no named scholar behind a poet's version

**Versions used**

> Standard Babylonian (Akkadian, eleven-tablet 'Sin-leqi-unninni' version) as the base text, with gaps filled from the Old Babylonian version and, in places, other Middle Babylonian or Hittite fragments. The editorial notes say explicitly: e.g. Tablet II '[The next 33 lines are missing in the Standard Version; lines 57-86 are taken from the Old Babylonian.]'. Pre-dates George (2003) and the Sulaymaniyah tablet (published 2014), so none of that material is present

**Non standard material**

> Marked, not silently merged. Bracketed editorial notes announce where the Standard Version breaks off and Old Babylonian lines are substituted, with line ranges ('lines 103-129 are taken from the Old Babylonian version'). The seams are therefore visible to the reader, though the substituted lines themselves are set in the same type as the Standard text

**Gap policy**

> Shown, not bridged. Lacunae are left open and counted: bracketed notes state how many lines are lost ('[67 lines are missing, in which Gilgamesh convinces the scorpion-being to allow him...]', '[All of the last column, some 40-50 lines, is missing.]'), sometimes with a one-line summary of the missing action. Short breaks inside a line are ellipses. Nothing is invented to smooth the narrative

**Typography of absence**

> Dense and scholarly: square brackets for restorations and editorial notes (e.g. 'The Great Goddess [Aruru]'), ellipses for broken words or lines (about 340 ellipses in the electronic text), '(?)' after uncertain words (over 100 instances, e.g. 'gleams like copper(?)'), '(!)' for surprising readings, and parentheses for words supplied for sense or alternative readings ('sanctuaries (or: cities)'). Siduri's first speech in Tablet X is almost entirely ellipses and fragments. The page looks like a transcript of damage rather than a finished poem

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> The death of Enkidu (Tablets VII-VIII) is the hinge: Gilgamesh's speech to Siduri in Tablet X names it as the cause of everything after ('My friend whom I love has turned to clay. Am I not like him? Will I lie down, never to get up again?'). Because the Standard Version lacks the Old Babylonian 'fill your belly' advice, Siduri in Kovacs is a gatekeeper rather than a philosopher; the final lesson lands instead in Utanapishtim's meditation on death ('No one can see death, no one can see the face of death...') and in the failed sleep test and loss of the plant to the snake in Tablet XI. The closing walls of Uruk turn the failure into acceptance

**Arc interpretation**

> Maturity and acceptance of mortality through grief, framed by kingship. The catalogue description frames the work as 'man's eternal struggle with the limitations of human nature', touching loneliness, friendship, love, loss and fear of death (SearchWorks). Evidence in the text: the prologue promises a king who 'went on a distant journey, pushing himself to exhaustion, but then was brought to peace'; Tablet XI ends not with a moral but with the repeated wall passage, so the reader infers that Gilgamesh's consolation is the city and the record he leaves. Kovacs's literal restraint means the interpretation is left to the reader rather than pressed by the translator

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> No metre. Plain, literal modern American English, one English line per Akkadian line with indentation for speeches; sentence-level fidelity over rhythm. Occasional Akkadian terms left untranslated (magillu-boat, ellu). No archaism, but some clinical or dated word choices ('harlot', 'primitive' for lullu). Uncertainty markers remain in the running text, so it reads as a scholar's working translation

**Sample line**

> "My friend whom I love has turned to clay. Am I not like him? Will I lie down, never to get up again?" (Tablet X, Kovacs 1989; Carnahan electronic edition p. 37)

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Kovacs is the purest example of the human philologist as the pipeline: reading signs, choosing between Standard and Old Babylonian witnesses, counting missing lines and flagging every doubtful word with (?). That is exactly the layer machine tools (sign recognition, fragment matching, neural Akkadian-English translation) now target, and a machine could plausibly produce something close to her literal, marker-heavy output. What her version shows by contrast is what is left for a poet or reader: she deliberately does not smooth, dramatise or interpret, so the emotional arc has to be supplied by whoever reads it. Her version also froze in 1989; later fragments (George 2003, Sulaymaniyah 2014) could not enter it, which makes the case for continuously updated, machine-assisted editions while the human choice of voice stays open

**Essay hooks**

> (1) The final line of the epic in Kovacs still carries a question mark: 'the open area(?) of Uruk it encloses' (Tablet XI). (2) Gaps are counted, not filled: '[67 lines are missing, in which Gilgamesh convinces the scorpion-being to allow him...]'. (3) Over 100 '(?)' markers and around 340 ellipses in the electronic text, a translation that shows its damage. (4) SearchWorks bills it as the first English translation to include newly found tablet pieces, yet it was overtaken within ten years by George. (5) The most-read copy online is a 1998 retyping by Wolf Carnahan that strips her notes and adds typos, a pre-AI example of a text drifting from its source. (6) Siduri, famous for 'fill your belly', says nothing of the sort in the Standard Version Kovacs follows

## 5. Stephanie Dalley, Myths from Mesopotamia (OUP, 1989)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Myths from Mesopotamia: Creation, the Flood, Gilgamesh, and Others, translated with an introduction and notes by Stephanie Dalley (Oxford University Press)

**Creator**

> Stephanie Dalley, Assyriologist at the Oriental Institute, University of Oxford (taught Akkadian and Sumerian there 1979 to 2007; Shillito Fellow in Assyriology from 1988; later Honorary Senior Fellow, Somerville College). Published by Oxford University Press

**Form**

> Scholarly anthology of verse-lineated literal translations. Gilgamesh sits beside Atrahasis, the Descent of Ishtar, Nergal and Ereshkigal, Adapa, Etana, the Epic of Creation (Enuma Elish), the Theogony of Dunnu, Anzu and Erra and Ishum, each with its own introduction, notes and a shared glossary of names

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Direct access. Dalley is a cuneiformist who read and taught Akkadian and Sumerian at Oxford, and she translated from the cuneiform sources and their editions, not from a crib. The 2000 revision updated the text in light of new tablets and philological work (OUP catalogue description)

**Scholarly adviser**

> N/A in the Armitage/Mitchell sense: Dalley is herself the specialist, so there is no named scholar behind a poet's version

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> The death of Enkidu drives the second half, but Dalley's two-text structure gives the reader two hinges. In the Standard Babylonian version the decisive moment is Tablet XI: Ut-napishtim's flood story, the failed test of staying awake six days and seven nights, and the snake taking the plant ('A snake smelt the fragrance of the plant. It came up silently and carried off the plant. As it took it away, it shed its scaly skin'). In the Old Babylonian version the hinge is Siduri, whose advice ('You will not find the eternal life you seek') states the lesson outright. Printing Atrahasis in the same book also shifts weight onto the flood episode, which reads as a borrowed piece of a wider mythology

**Arc interpretation**

> Acceptance of mortality set within a shared Mesopotamian cosmology, rather than a self-contained heroic arc. Because the anthology sets Gilgamesh beside the creation and flood myths, the story reads as one instance of a recurring theme: the gods keep life for themselves ('When the gods created mankind / They appointed death for mankind, / Kept eternal life in their own hands', Old Babylonian Siduri). Dalley's literal restraint leaves interpretation to the reader; her introductions stress textual history and parallels over moral reading

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> No metre. Literal British English in short lines matching the Akkadian line units, with modest archaic colour in phrasing ('Of him who found out all things, I shall tell the land') and Mesopotamian epithets kept ('Uruk the Sheepfold'). Proper names are hyphenated to show their parts (Ut-napishtim, Ur-shanabi). Reads as a careful scholar's working text, clearer than a transliteration but not shaped as poetry

**Sample line**

> "Of him who found out all things, I shall tell the land, / Of him who experienced everything, I shall teach the whole." (Tablet I, opening; Dalley, Myths from Mesopotamia, OUP 1989, p. 50)

### Reception

**Controversies**

> None over the Gilgamesh translation's accuracy or liberties. Dalley is better known for an unrelated dispute: her argument (developed over eighteen years and set out in her 2013 book) that the Hanging Gardens were at Nineveh under Sennacherib rather than at Babylon. The book is also cited heavily on ancient-astronaut websites, a misuse outside her control

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Dalley's book shows the human work that sits around a translation rather than inside a line. A machine can now produce a literal Akkadian-to-English rendering close to her register, and her 2000 revision already shows the text updating as new tablets arrived, which is the part automated fragment matching could speed up. What a machine does not yet do is the editorial framing: deciding to print the Old Babylonian and Standard versions as two texts instead of one blended story, and deciding to put Gilgamesh next to Atrahasis and the Descent of Ishtar so a reader sees the flood and the goddess as shared myth. That curatorial choice changes how the arc reads more than any single word choice does

**Essay hooks**

> (1) The famous carpe diem speech from Siduri is not in the Standard version at all; in Dalley it sits in a separate Old Babylonian section, so the book's most quoted advice comes from a different, older text. (2) Gilgamesh is one myth among ten in the volume, placed next to Atrahasis, whose flood the epic borrows. (3) The opening, 'Of him who found out all things, I shall tell the land', is a claim about knowledge, not strength. (4) The snake 'came up silently and carried off the plant. As it took it away, it shed its scaly skin': the whole quest undone in three lines. (5) The translator is better known in the press for relocating the Hanging Gardens to Nineveh, a reminder that the same philology that reads Gilgamesh also rewrites wonders of the world. Sources: OUP Learning Link excerpt of Dalley 1989; OUP catalogue page; Wikipedia 'Stephanie Dalley'

## 6. David Ferry, Gilgamesh: A New Rendering in English Verse (1992)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Gilgamesh: A New Rendering in English Verse

**Date**

> Farrar, Straus and Giroux hardcover, June 1992 (ISBN 9780374162276); FSG/Noonday paperback 1993 (ISBN 9780374523831), still in print. New York Times Notable Book; Ferry's first book after retiring from Wellesley.

**Creator**

> David Ferry (1924-2023), American poet and translator (later translator of Horace's Odes and Epistles, Virgil's Eclogues, Georgics and Aeneid), Sophie Chantal Hart Professor Emeritus of English at Wellesley College, National Book Award for Poetry 2012 for Bewilderment. Introduction by William L. Moran, Harvard Assyriologist.

**Form**

> Poet's verse rendering, not a line-by-line translation. Loose iambic pentameter laid out in unrhymed two-line stanzas (Ferry calls them 'iambic pentameter couplets'; the Ploughshares review calls them 'blank-verse couplets'), with occasional short lines set off at section openings (the poem begins with the single line 'The Story'). Organised by tablet, Tablets I to XII. Front matter: Moran's short introduction (about three pages per Danny Yee). Back matter: roughly seven pages of notes explaining Ferry's choices and liberties.

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Ferry did not read Akkadian or cuneiform. He worked from existing literal English translations, chiefly E. A. Speiser's version in Pritchard's Ancient Near Eastern Texts Relating to the Old Testament ('Speiser's translation, my main source'), plus word-for-word cribs supplied by his friend William L. Moran of Harvard for the opening passage and for Ishtar's proposal in Tablet VI. He argued the ignorance helped: 'I was working from scholarly word-for-word translations and these had the effect of being, in themselves, less nuanced and complex than I would have found the Babylonian, if I knew Babylonian' (interview with David Eckel, BU Core blog, 2010). He wrote it 'in a matter of months. Time of my life.'

**Scholarly adviser**

> William L. Moran (1921-2000), Harvard Assyriologist and editor of the Amarna Letters, who supplied literal translations of key passages, encouraged the project and wrote the book's introduction.

**Non standard material**

> Silent merge within the poem. No brackets, italics or typographic signals separate Standard Babylonian from Old Babylonian or from Ferry's own inventions; the reader has to turn to the endnotes to learn which passages are freely reworked, abridged or supplied. Ferry's notes acknowledge that the rendering takes many liberties with the surviving tablets and fragments. Tablet XII is kept as a separate, final tablet rather than woven into the narrative.

**Gap policy**

> Bridged and smoothed. Ferry fills lacunae with plausible content of his own, or cuts across them, so the verse runs continuously. Danny Yee notes that when comparing a specific damaged passage across versions, 'in the Mason and Ferry versions these specific lines can't really be identified.' The endnotes, not the page, are where the gaps are admitted.

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> The death of Enkidu. Ferry named 'the heartbreaking death of Enkidu passage' as one of the parts that moved him most when he started, and the Tablet VIII lament, in which Gilgamesh mourns 'like a widow' over his friend, is one of the most concentrated stretches of the rendering. Everything after it becomes a flight from the knowledge of death. The poem's own opening already frames the story as a completed emotional arc rather than a tale of deeds: 'The Story / of him who knew the most of all men know; / who made the journey; heartbroken; reconciled;'. 'Heartbroken; reconciled' is Ferry's own compression of the Standard Babylonian prologue (which speaks of the king who saw everything, found out secrets and returned 'weary but at peace'), and it announces a hinge in grief and an ending in acceptance. The loss of the plant to the snake is a secondary hinge that finally forces the reconciliation.

**Arc interpretation**

> Grief moving towards reconciliation, told with irony. Ferry's own summary of the hero lists the whole arc in one breath: 'such a wonderful terrifying, big-hearted, arrogant, scared, joyful, humiliated, sexy, desperate, grieving, self-deceiving, learning, heartbroken, reconciled character' (BU interview, 2010). The same pairing 'heartbroken; reconciled' sits in the poem's first lines, so the version reads as maturity reached through loss rather than as kingship or simple failure. Critics saw two registers. Joyce Peseroff (Ploughshares, Fall 1992) read Gilgamesh as both the first epic hero and the first anti-hero, whose 'Nietzschean' refusal of mortality ends with him returning home empty-handed, and stressed the poem's celebration of the city and of Enkidu's civilising. Tom Sleigh (New York Times Book Review) heard 'the hero's anguish' and 'the rage and despair of the untouchable'. Publisher copy says the pentameter captures the 'elegiac and ironic undertones' of a failed search for immortality. A lay reader (Jason McIntosh, Fogknife, 2017) even read it as black comedy about a vain demigod, which shows how much Ferry lets Gilgamesh's foolishness show. Because Tablet XII follows, the final note is less the consolation of the walls than a sober look at what death is like.

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Loose iambic pentameter in unrhymed two-line stanzas, flexible enough to take long, Speiser-derived speech lines and repetitions. Diction is modern, plain and spoken American English, with no archaism and no 'thee/thou'. Ferry keeps some of the formulaic repetition of the original (the repeated dreams, travel formulas, 'he spoke and said'), which some readers find laborious in the later tablets, but he also shortens and cuts. Punctuation is used expressively (semicolons in the opening act as a chant: 'who made the journey; heartbroken; reconciled;'). The tone mixes lyric elegy with dry irony; publisher copy calls it 'more lyrical than epic'.

**Sample line**

> "The Story / of him who knew the most of all men know; / who made the journey; heartbroken; reconciled;" (Ferry, Gilgamesh, opening of Tablet I; Poetry Foundation excerpt)

### Reception

**Controversies**

> No serious dispute over provenance or authorship. The standing objection is category and accuracy: a poet without Akkadian producing a 'rendering' from Speiser and Moran's cribs, filling lacunae and silently merging recensions, so that readers cannot tell ancient text from Ferry's supplements. Assyriologically, it predates Andrew George's 2003 edition and so rests on a 1950s text. Some readers also object to its long repetitions and to omitted details. Ferry and the subtitle are candid ('A New Rendering', not 'translation'), which blunted criticism.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Ferry is the clearest case of the division of labour an AI essay could lean on. The philology was done by other people: Speiser's printed translation and Moran's word-for-word cribs were, in effect, a machine-readable intermediate. Ferry says outright that not knowing Babylonian helped, because the flat literal versions left room for him. What he added is exactly what a crib lacks: a verse form ('once I'd found a suitable form, iambic pentameter couplets'), a sense of which passages matter (Enkidu's death, Ishtar's proposition), and a compressed reading of the whole ('heartbroken; reconciled'). Today a model can produce a Speiser-grade crib from a transliteration in seconds, and can produce pentameter too. The question Ferry raises is whether a model would choose to put 'heartbroken; reconciled' in the first lines, or would know that the Enkidu lament is where the poem should be heaviest. The cost is also instructive: Ferry's clean couplets erase the gaps, the recension boundaries and the scholarship, so the reader gets a poem that feels whole when the tablets are not. That smoothing is precisely the risk people raise about fluent machine output.

**Essay hooks**

> 1) The poet who could not read the tablets: 'I was working from scholarly word-for-word translations and these had the effect of being, in themselves, less nuanced and complex than I would have found the Babylonian, if I knew Babylonian' (Ferry, BU Core interview, 2010). 2) Speed: 'I did the whole thing in a matter of months. Time of my life.' (same interview). A version still in print after 30+ years, written in months, from a crib. 3) The human-in-the-loop pair: Moran (Harvard Assyriologist) hands over word-for-word versions of the opening and Ishtar's proposal; Ferry supplies the form. A 1990s precedent for 'machine translation plus poet'. 4) Two words that carry the arc: 'who made the journey; heartbroken; reconciled;' (opening lines). 5) Carne-Ross's backhanded praise: 'as close to the ancient original as we in our ignorance have any right to' (The New Criterion). 6) Danny Yee on Ferry and Mason: 'in the Mason and Ferry versions these specific lines can't really be identified', a neat line on how fluency hides the gaps. 7) Ferry's one-sentence character list for Gilgamesh: 'terrifying, big-hearted, arrogant, scared, joyful, humiliated, sexy, desperate, grieving, self-deceiving, learning, heartbroken, reconciled'.

## 7. Andrew George, Penguin translation (1999) and critical edition (2003)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> The Epic of Gilgamesh: The Babylonian Epic Poem and Other Texts in Akkadian and Sumerian (Penguin, trans. Andrew George) and The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic: Introduction, Critical Edition and Cuneiform Texts (Oxford University Press)

**Creator**

> Andrew R. George (b. 1955), British Assyriologist, Professor of Babylonian at SOAS, University of London; PhD under W. G. Lambert (1985). Publishers: Penguin (translation) and Oxford University Press (critical edition)

**Form**

> Two linked works. (1) Penguin: verse translation, one English line per cuneiform line, of the Standard Babylonian epic in twelve tablets plus separate translations of the Old Babylonian and Middle Babylonian fragments and the five Sumerian Bilgames poems, with introduction, notes and glossary. (2) OUP: critical edition, a composite variorum text in transliteration with a score of every manuscript, cuneiform hand-copies, philological commentary and a literal English translation

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Direct and first-hand. George reads Akkadian and Sumerian and established the text himself by collating the tablets in the British Museum and other collections, making new hand-copies. The Penguin translation was produced alongside the critical edition, so it is the reading-text of the same scholar who built the underlying edition. No crib involved

**Scholarly adviser**

> N/A: George is the specialist. He is the adviser others lean on (later poet-translators and teachers cite his edition as their base text)

**Versions used**

> Standard Babylonian (the eleven-tablet 'Sîn-lēqi-unninni' version) plus Tablet XII as the core text; Old Babylonian tablets (Pennsylvania, Yale, Sippar/Meissner and others) and Middle Babylonian peripheral fragments (Megiddo, Emar, Ugarit, Hattusa) translated separately; the five Sumerian Bilgames poems (Bilgames and Akka, Bilgames and Huwawa, Bull of Heaven, Death of Bilgames, Bilgames and the Netherworld). The Sulaymaniyah Tablet V fragment postdates both books; George published it with Farouk Al-Rawi in 2014 ('Back to the Cedar Forest', Journal of Cuneiform Studies 66)

**Gap policy**

> Shown. George's stated aim is to indicate every lacuna so readers know what is established and what is surmised (Penguin blurb; Literary Review, 1999). Missing lines are marked and often counted, restorations are flagged, and nothing is invented to make the story run smoothly. The critical edition goes further, printing the damage of each manuscript line by line

**Tablet xii**

> Included, as the twelfth tablet after the Tablet XI ending, and discussed in the introduction as an appended Akkadian translation of the second half of the Sumerian 'Bilgames and the Netherworld', not an organic continuation. George suggests it was attached because it complements the epic's concern with death. Readers therefore meet two endings: the walls of Uruk (XI) and Enkidu's report from the underworld (XII)

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> The death of Enkidu (Tablets VII to VIII) is the hinge, and George's apparatus makes the shape clearer than most versions because it separates Standard Babylonian from Old Babylonian. In the Standard text Siduri is a gatekeeper; her famous 'let your belly be full' advice survives only in the Old Babylonian Sippar tablet, which George translates in its own section. So in his main text the lesson falls to Uta-napishti's speech on death and the failed sleep test in Tablet XI, and the story resolves at the walls of Uruk. His reading of the first line ('He who saw the Deep', the Apsû, instead of the older 'He who saw everything') also moves the weight toward wisdom brought back from the edge of the world

**Arc interpretation**

> Wisdom through failure, framed by kingship. George's introduction presents the epic as the story of a king who seeks immortality, fails, and returns with knowledge that makes him a better ruler and a mortal man at peace with that. Evidence: the prologue promises one who 'saw the Deep' and 'brought back a message from before the Flood'; the narrative ends not with the lost plant but with Gilgamesh showing Ur-shanabi the city he built; the Old Babylonian incipit 'Surpassing all other kings' stresses royal identity. The grief over Enkidu drives the quest, but the outcome George emphasises is acceptance and legacy (the wall and the written record) rather than despair

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Loose, stress-based lines that follow the Akkadian couplet structure (each line tends to fall into two halves, echoing the caesura of Babylonian verse). Plain modern British English with a slightly formal, elevated register; no archaism, occasional inversions to keep the rhythm ('death they dispensed to mankind'). Repetitions and formulaic lines kept as in the original. Alan Wall found the rhythm could induce 'narcosis when persisted in for too long' (Literary Review, February 1999)

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> George's critical edition is the human groundwork that machine projects now build on. His score transliterations (every manuscript lined up sign by sign) are exactly the structured data that fragment-matching tools such as the Electronic Babylonian Library use to find new joins, and their open release in 2020 made that easier. What a machine cannot yet supply is the judgement in the Penguin: deciding which ancient version to print, where a restoration is safe, and how to render a Babylonian couplet in readable English while leaving the holes visible. George's refusal to smooth gaps is itself a choice a model trained to produce fluent text would tend to erase. His work also shows an edition is never finished: the Sulaymaniyah tablet arrived eleven years after the OUP volumes

## 8. Benjamin Foster, The Epic of Gilgamesh (Norton Critical, 2001)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> The Epic of Gilgamesh: A New Translation, Analogues, Criticism (Norton Critical Edition), translated and edited by Benjamin R. Foster

**Date**

> 2001 (W. W. Norton, first edition, paperback ISBN 9780393975161; some listings give June 2001, Norton's own description gives December 2001). Second, revised and expanded Norton Critical Edition 2019 (ISBN 9780393643985). Foster's translation also appears in the Norton Anthology of World Literature

**Creator**

> Benjamin R. Foster (Laffan Professor of Assyriology and Babylonian Literature, Yale; curator of the Yale Babylonian Collection), who translated the Akkadian epic. Douglas Frayne translated the Sumerian Gilgamesh poems and Gary Beckman the Hittite version. Published by W. W. Norton

**Form**

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Direct. Foster is a professional Assyriologist who reads Akkadian and translates from the cuneiform editions line by line. In a 2016 interview he said: 'I just do it, line by line, comparing various other versions in other languages to see what they do,' and that he tries 'to use the same English word for the same Babylonian word each time if I can, but I'm not slavish about that' (World Literature Today, Jan 2017). **Scholarly adviser**

**Gap policy**

Harmonising with Old Babylonian material bridges several of the largest lacunae

**Tablet xii**

> Dropped from the epic. Foster treats Tablet XII as a partial Akkadian translation of a Sumerian poem appended later because it seemed germane, and omits it in favour of the fuller Sumerian original (Bilgames and the Netherworld) translated by Douglas Frayne in the Analogues. The epic therefore ends at Tablet XI with the walls of Uruk, closing the ring begun in the prologue

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> Enkidu's death (Tablets VII-VIII) is the hinge, since Foster frames the epic as about 'the two most important human concerns, love and death' and his interview stresses the Babylonian poet's genius in promoting Enkidu from servant (as in the Sumerian poems) to friend. Because Foster harmonises in the Old Babylonian Siduri speech, Tablet X carries an explicit philosophical counter-offer ('The eternal life you are seeking you shall not find'), which Gilgamesh rejects. The practical lesson arrives with the snake in Tablet XI ('I have done a good deed for a reptile!'), and the closing walls of Uruk turn loss into acceptance

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Free lineated verse, one English line per Akkadian line where possible, with the couplet parallelism of the original kept visible. Modern, plain, readable American English with occasional elevated or slightly formal turns ('wherefore do you wander'). Foster tested lines by ear: 'I also felt it was very important to read aloud what I had done. That way the lame lines came through with discouraging clarity.' A reviewer praises it for retaining 'the poetic rhythm of the Akkadian original'

**Sample line**

> "The eternal life you are seeking you shall not find." (Siduri to Gilgamesh, Tablet X, Foster 2001, as quoted by Robert Boyd Skipper, Substack)

### Reception

**Controversies**

> No accuracy dispute. The main scholarly caveat is methodological: harmonising Standard and Old Babylonian material into one narrative ('Gilgamesch-Harmonie') produces a story no ancient reader ever read, and silent restorations hide how uncertain some lines are. Foster defends both choices on grounds of audience. The 2019 edition dropped the Rivkah Harris essay on women in the epic and Hillary Major's poem, replacing them with essays by Susan Ackerman and Andrew George

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Foster is a useful middle case between the philologist and the poet. The work a machine could plausibly assist with (reading signs, aligning Standard and Old Babylonian witnesses, proposing restorations) is exactly what Foster does as a scholar. But his decisive choices are editorial and aural: which centuries-apart fragments to sew together, which breaks are worth showing, and which lines sound 'lame' when read aloud. He openly rejects mechanical marking of every doubtful reading as 'a useless exercise in the typesetter's craft', a judgement about the reader's experience that no alignment model makes. The Norton frame also shows a human curation layer: choosing analogues and critical essays that tell students how to read the poem

**Essay hooks**

> (1) Foster on his method: 'My rendering sews together material often centuries apart in time', a harmony no Babylonian ever read (World Literature Today, 2017). (2) 'Relentlessly marking minor problems with the manuscripts is largely a useless exercise in the typesetter's craft', a direct contrast with Kovacs's hundred-plus question marks. (3) His quality test was reading aloud: 'the lame lines came through with discouraging clarity.' (4) 'The pleasure and pain of translation is to try to play well on two difficult musical instruments at the same time.' (5) Tablet XII is cut from the epic and replaced by the older, fuller Sumerian poem in a separate section, a choice about what counts as the ending. (6) Gilgamesh's verdict after the snake: 'I have done a good deed for a reptile!' (7) Foster calls Andrew George's English translation 'by far the most authoritative in any language', a leading Assyriologist deferring to a rival version

## 9. Stephen Mitchell, Gilgamesh: A New English Version (2004)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Gilgamesh: A New English Version

**Date**

> Free Press (Simon & Schuster), New York, 2004 (hardcover ISBN 9780743261647; paperback 2006, ISBN 9780743261692). UK edition Profile Books, 2005. Held in over 2,700 WorldCat libraries.

**Creator**

> Stephen Mitchell (born 1943, Brooklyn), American poet and translator of sacred and classic texts (Tao Te Ching 1988, Book of Job, Bhagavad Gita, later the Iliad and Odyssey), who describes himself as 'de-educated through intensive Zen practice'. Married to the self-help writer Byron Katie, whom he quotes in the Gilgamesh introduction.

**Form**

> Free verse adaptation, explicitly called a 'version' and not a translation. The poem is divided into eleven 'Books' (Prologue plus Books I to XI) rather than tablets, preceded by a roughly 60-page interpretive introduction ('The Oldest Story in the World') and followed by about 100 pages of notes, a bibliography and a glossary. Paperback runs about 290 pages.

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Mitchell has no Akkadian. From 'About This Version': 'I don't read cuneiform and have no knowledge of Akkadian; for the meaning of the text, I have depended on literal translations by seven scholars.' His method was to read and compare the translations in his bibliography, 'cobbled together a rough prose version', and then begin 'the real work, of raising the language to the level of English verse'. He likens the first stage to being 'rather like a bat, feeling out the contours of the original text by flinging sound waves into the dark.' The cribs he names: A. R. George's two-volume critical edition (2003), which he calls 'superb, meticulous, monumental', plus the translations of Jean Bottéro, Benjamin R. Foster, Maureen Gallery Kovacs, Albert Schott, and Raymond Jacques Tournay with Aaron Shaffer. He also read the literary versions of David Ferry and Raoul Schrott. Bottéro's notes shaped his reading of many passages.

**Versions used**

> Base text is Sin-leqi-unninni's Standard Babylonian version ('a sometimes free, sometimes close adaptation into English verse of Sin-leqi-unninni's Standard Version'). Gaps are filled from the Old Babylonian version (for example Shiduri's speech on enjoying life in Book X) and, going further than scholarly translators, from the Sumerian Gilgamesh poems: 'occasionally, when the Standard Version is particularly fragmentary, I have supplemented it with passages from the Sumerian Gilgamesh poems' (e.g. 'Gilgamesh and Huwawa'). Middle Babylonian material is used too (e.g. 'may wild dogs camp in your bedroom' in Enkidu's curse is drawn from the Ur tablet, per the notes). The Schøyen tablets are cited in the notes. Nothing later than George 2003, so no Sulaymaniyah tablet (published 2014).

**Non standard material**

> Silent merge on the page, documented in the endnotes. The verse carries no brackets, italics or other signals separating Standard Babylonian from Old Babylonian, Sumerian or Mitchell's own additions. Mitchell says so directly: 'I filled in the many gaps in the text; I changed images that were unclear; I added lines when the drama of the situation called for elaboration or when passages ended abruptly and needed transitions; I cut out a number of fragmentary passages; and when the text was garbled, I occasionally changed the order of passages. (All these changes are documented in the notes.)' The notes, keyed by page and phrase, often give George's or Bottéro's literal wording for comparison.

**Gap policy**

> Bridged and smoothed. Mitchell fills lacunae with invented or imported lines, cuts fragments he cannot use, and removes the formulaic repetitions and counting sequences ('the word-for-word repetitions of entire passages and the enumerations from one to seven or twelve'), which he calls 'tedious for us'. Danny Yee's comparative review says he 'fills in the gaps and removes repetitions and stylistic awkwardnesses' while taking 'fewer liberties' than Mason or Ferry, and gives a damaged Tablet V line as an example of a 'simple, straightforward' reading with no marker of loss.

**Typography of absence**

> Nothing on the page. No square brackets, ellipses, blank lines or line numbers in the poem. The only trace of damage is in the endnotes, where Mitchell sometimes reproduces George's bracketed restorations (e.g. '[like a crack in the ground.]') to show what the literal text looks like.

**Tablet xii**

> Dropped from the poem: 'Like many other translators, I have omitted Tablet XII, which most scholars consider as not belonging to the epic.' It is discussed in the introduction and quoted in the notes (in George's translation, alongside the Sumerian 'Bilgames and the Netherworld'). The effect is a clean ring structure: the book ends on Gilgamesh showing Urshanabi the walls of Uruk, repeating the Prologue, so the last word is the city ('the houses, the public squares') and not Enkidu's report from the underworld.

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> Two hinges. The first, and the one Mitchell's introduction stresses most, is the killing of Humbaba. He reads the Cedar Forest expedition as a moral error: 'Gilgamesh's action is the original preemptive attack', and 'Enkidu caused his own death by insisting that Gilgamesh kill Humbaba; if they had let the monster live, all would have been well.' Humbaba is framed as the forest's guardian appointed by Enlil (a refrain in Book III: 'Humbaba is the forest's guardian'), and after his killing Mitchell doubles the line 'A gentle rain fell onto the mountains.' Enkidu's death follows from this, and Gilgamesh's Book VIII lament ('one of the most beautiful elegies in literature') begins the flight from death. The second hinge is the return to the walls of Uruk. Mitchell argues that the quest itself changes nothing ('The quest proves the futility of the quest'); the advice of Shiduri and Utnapishtim is 'beautiful words, but as useless to him'. The turn comes when Gilgamesh gives up: 'Not until Gilgamesh gives up on transcendence can he realize how beautiful his city is.' The loss of the plant to the snake is the release that makes that surrender possible.

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Loose four-beat line: 'a loose, noniambic, nonalliterative tetrameter', modelled, he says, on parts of Eliot's Four Quartets and Elizabeth Bishop's 'Sestina'. He varied it 'so that no two consecutive lines have the identical rhythm'. The Prologue and the end of Book XI move to five beats. Diction is plain contemporary American English, occasionally colloquial and blunt, with no archaism. Repetition and numerical formulae are cut, which speeds the narrative. Jasper Griffin: 'lines with four rhythmic stresses, unrhyming, in a diction raised a little in tone, but not unrecognizably far, from that of speech'. Armitage called it 'very fluid and flowing'.

**Sample line**

> "Death has caught me, / it lurks in my bedroom, and everywhere I look, / everywhere I turn, there is only death." (Mitchell, Gilgamesh, Book XI, Free Press 2004)

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Mitchell's own account of his workflow reads like a human version of a modern machine pipeline. Step one: ingest seven scholarly translations and George's edition, compare them, and produce 'a rough prose version'. That is close to what a model can now do in seconds from a transliteration or a stack of cribs. Step two, which he calls 'the real work', is 'raising the language to the level of English verse': choosing a rhythm, deciding which repetitions to cut, where drama needs an added line, and what the whole poem means. His confessed image for the first stage ('rather like a bat, feeling out the contours of the original text by flinging sound waves into the dark') captures working at one remove from the source, which is what an LLM does too. Mitchell also shows the cost. The fluent result erases every gap, merges three recensions and a set of Sumerian poems without a seam, and carries a strong interpretive thesis (Zen surrender, anti-war Humbaba) into the text itself. That is the machine-output worry, but with a human signature: fluency that hides where the source is missing, and a confident voice that reads its own values into the material. The difference is accountability. Mitchell documents every change in 100 pages of notes and signs the introduction that argues his reading. A useful contrast for Lee: the craft is not the crib, it is the choices, and the obligation is to show them.

**Essay hooks**

> 1) The admission: 'I don't read cuneiform and have no knowledge of Akkadian; for the meaning of the text, I have depended on literal translations by seven scholars' (About This Version). 2) The bat: 'I felt rather like a bat, feeling out the contours of the original text by flinging sound waves into the dark.' A human description of working from other people's outputs. 3) Two-stage pipeline: 'cobbled together a rough prose version' then 'the real work, of raising the language to the level of English verse'. 4) The full list of liberties in one sentence: 'I filled in the many gaps in the text; I changed images that were unclear; I added lines when the drama of the situation called for elaboration... I cut out a number of fragmentary passages; and when the text was garbled, I occasionally changed the order of passages.' 5) Opening of the introduction: 'In Iraq, when the dust blows, stopping men and tanks...' and Humbaba as 'the original preemptive attack'. 6) Growth reading in one line: 'Not until Gilgamesh gives up on transcendence can he realize how beautiful his city is.' 7) The 2026 bridge: Armitage said Mitchell's was 'the first I read front to back, very fluid and flowing' (Pittsburgh Review of Books), and LARB's Nicolas Liney set their Shamhat scenes side by side: Mitchell's 'touched his penis, and put him inside her' versus Armitage's 'more blushing and restrained' language. 8) Numbers: 'slightly fewer than two thousand of the three thousand lines' survive in readable form (Mitchell's introduction), against a version that reads as complete.

## 10. Sophus Helle, Gilgamesh: A New Translation of the Ancient Epic (Yale, 2021)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Gilgamesh: A New Translation of the Ancient Epic, with Essays on the Poem, Its Past, and Its Passion (Yale University Press, trans. Sophus Helle)

**Form**

> Verse translation of the Standard Babylonian epic in twelve tablets, each tablet titled by its opening words (Tablet I 'He who saw the deep', Tablet XII 'Had I only left'), with an introduction, a note on the translation, five interpretive essays ('A Poem for the Ages', 'Study the Brickwork', 'The Storm of His Heart', 'Dying to Be Read', 'Kings, Women, Monsters'), notes and bibliography

### Source Handling

**Scholarly adviser**

> N/A: Helle is himself the Assyriologist. The poet in the partnership is Morten Søndergaard, co-translator of the 2019 Danish version, not the English one

**Non standard material**

> Merged into the running text but flagged. Passages inserted from older versions carry small-capital margin notes such as 'old babylonian version' or 'ugaritic version' (Macfarlane, NYRB, 20 October 2022), so the reader can see the switch without the story stopping. This is the same approach Andrew George takes, but with lighter typography

**Gap policy**

> Bridged where scholars can reasonably reconstruct, shown where they cannot. Helle's note: 'Words that are ambiguous, implicit, emended, or reconstructed appear in regular typeface, giving my best guess at what the text once said.' So reconstructions are not distinguished from surviving text, unlike George or Foster. Genuinely lost text is still marked, and when the number of lost lines is known, that many lines are left blank. Helle has said he values some gaps as meaning in themselves: 'The absence of text as Enkidu dies is very powerful to me' (Full Stop, 2022)

**Typography of absence**

> No square brackets and no italics. A raised dot (like a hovering full stop) marks the start and end of a broken passage, and whole blank lines stand in for lines whose count is known. The convention came out of sessions with Wrong Studio designers and Helle's students, who tested alternatives including redaction-style black bars. Macfarlane says it gives 'a relatively smooth linear journey through the poem, without ignoring the existence of the voids'. In the Danish audiobook Søndergaard knocked with a finger to voice the gaps (Thin End of the Wedge podcast, episode 28)

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> Enkidu's death is the emotional hinge. Helle singles out the break in the tablet as Enkidu dies as one of the most powerful moments, precisely because text is missing. But his structural reading places the real turn at the end: Gilgamesh's return to Uruk and his command to Ur-shanabi to study the brickwork, which sends the reader back to the prologue. The essay 'Study the Brickwork' treats the wall as the poem's key image, and 'Dying to Be Read' ties the failed quest for eternal life to the survival of the written story. So the snake and Uta-napishti's sleep test close off the physical quest, while the walls of Uruk convert failure into something that lasts

**Arc interpretation**

> A loop rather than a straight line, from grief to a kind of mortal wisdom expressed through writing and building. Evidence: (1) Helle argues the wording at the end 'far from being abrupt, is designed to lead us back to its beginning', like 'a snake biting its tail' (Agha-Jaffar review, 2022); (2) he reads the prologue as revealing Gilgamesh as the narrator who set the story down on a tablet, so the king who could not live forever lives on as a text ('Dying to Be Read'); (3) 'The Storm of His Heart' reads the poem through violent, obscure emotions, with Gilgamesh's restlessness and grief as the engine; (4) 'Kings, Women, Monsters' ties the story to kingship and power, including the tyranny the poem opens with. Helle also stresses that Gilgamesh and Enkidu 'do not explicate the nature of their feelings for one another', leaving the bond undefined rather than labelling it

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Free verse in modern, plain English, roughly one English line per Akkadian line, keeping the couplet parallelism and repetitions. No rhyme, no archaism. Helle aims at 'aural patterning' in place of the original's alliteration and assonance, e.g. 'work his words' for a common Akkadian phrase, to recreate 'the sinuous and sonorous pleasure of the poem' (NYRB). Macfarlane calls it 'lively, earthy' and occasionally finds the 'folksy plain speaking' in the essays too much

**Sample line**

> "The Thief of Life has a hold on my heart. / Death is sitting in my bedroom." (Tablet XI, Helle 2021, quoted in Robert Macfarlane, 'A Fireball from the Sands', NYRB, 20 October 2022)

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Helle's version shows where the judgement sits once the tablets are read. A model can propose likely restorations, but Helle chose to print his best guesses in plain type and keep a visible dot for what is truly gone, a design decision made with graphic designers and students, not a philological one. He also argues some gaps should stay: he would find it strange if a new manuscript filled the blank where Enkidu dies, because the silence does work in the poem. That is a reader's call about meaning. His text also depends on new finds (the Sulaymaniyah resin 'like raindrops'), and fragment-matching tools will keep supplying them, but deciding whether a recovered line enters the poem, and how it should sound, still needs a translator with an ear

**Essay hooks**

> (1) A raised dot, 'like a hovering full stop', replaces square brackets: typography designed with Wrong Studio and tested on students (Macfarlane, NYRB 2022; Thin End of the Wedge ep. 28). (2) 'Words that are ambiguous, implicit, emended, or reconstructed appear in regular typeface, giving my best guess at what the text once said': a translator openly choosing fluency over flagging. (3) Helle on the gap as Enkidu dies: 'it would be weird to me if and when... a manuscript is found that fills out this blank space' (Full Stop, 2022). (4) His father voiced the gaps in the Danish audiobook by knocking a finger. (5) 'The Thief of Life has a hold on my heart. / Death is sitting in my bedroom.' (6) A new fragment arrived mid-translation and forced a rework of Tablet II in the Danish version. (7) The ending as 'a snake biting its tail': the walls of Uruk send you back to the first page

## 11. Jenny Lewis, Gilgamesh Retold (Carcanet, 2018)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Gilgamesh Retold (Carcanet Classics)

**Date**

> Published 25 October 2018 by Carcanet Press, Manchester (paperback ISBN 9781784106140, ebook 9781784106157, 136 pp.). Audiobook read by Lewis (ISBN 9781784108779, 2019), Carcanet's first audiobook. Written 2013 to 2018 as the creative component of a Goldsmiths PhD; the accompanying thesis, 'Translating Epic from an Unfamiliar Language', was completed March 2021.

**Creator**

> Jenny Lewis, Anglo-Welsh poet, playwright and songwriter who teaches poetry at Oxford (University of Oxford continuing education). Editor at Carcanet/Oxford Poets: Robyn Marsack. Carcanet's publisher Michael Schmidt later discussed the book at length in his own Gilgamesh: The Life of a Poem (Princeton, 2019).

**Form**

> Verse retelling that Lewis calls 'a response' rather than a translation. Prologue, fifteen titled chapters (1 'The Coming of Enkidu' to 15 'Homecoming'), Epilogue, and a prose Afterword, plus a preface, list of characters, glossary of Babylonian words and a map. Each chapter uses a different verse form, meant to represent different voices retelling the tale over millennia: alliterative/four-beat quatrains with a marked caesura, ottava rima, terza rima, blank verse, dimeter with sung/chanted passages, a specular (mirror) lament, and polyphonic 'script' chapters.

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> No direct access to the cuneiform. Her thesis says plainly: 'as I am unable to decipher cuneiform or to read Babylonian, I based my book Gilgamesh Retold mainly on the scholarly translation by the Assyriologist Andrew George' (Penguin, 1999/2003). In 2012 she tried transliterating the Prologue herself using Martin Worthington's Complete Babylonian, produced a few rough lines, and abandoned the method as 'a fruitless task, resulting in a version like many others'. She then worked, like Ferry, Morgan, Chapman and Pope, 'by studying previous translations and related texts'.

**Scholarly adviser**

> No formal crib-supplier. Primary source is Andrew George's Penguin translation, and George is thanked in the thesis acknowledgements. Other named influences: Samuel Noah Kramer (History Begins at Sumer; Wolkstein and Kramer's Inanna), Fran Hazelton of the Enheduanna Society (Three Kings of Warka), Theodore Ziolkowski (Gilgamesh Among Us), and the Iraqi poet-translator Dr Salah Niazi, who shaped her sense of cinematic perspective and approved the word 'souls' in the Shamhat scene. Iraqi poet Adnan Al-Sayegh was her long-term collaborator on the parallel 'Writing Mesopotamia' project.

**Non standard material**

> Silent merge, with explanation deferred to the Afterword. Sumerian elements, invented scenes and lines from Arabic and modern sources sit inside the verse without any typographic marking. Additions Lewis names: a new terza rima dialogue between Shamhat and Enkidu in the forest (inserted partly to balance the chiastic form); an expanded ascent of Inanna to heaven; Shamash's motive to 'equal great Inanna'; Bibbu the butcher and the underworld 'wise-women, cleaners and sweepers' at the funeral; and a 'redemption' passage in Chapter 14 that she admits is a Judeo-Christian anachronism added 'to give the tale an ending that a contemporary readership would expect'. Cuts include the trapper's father's coarse advice about Shamhat, and Gilgamesh's 47-line insult to Ishtar condensed to 24 lines. The Cannonball Read reviewer complained that the multiple-voice scheme was 'only explained in the afterword'.

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> Structurally, Chapter 8 'Inanna and Gilgamesh', the rejection of the goddess. Lewis laid the chapter summaries out on the floor and built a chiastic book with this scene 'at the dead centre', mirroring its place in Tablet VI of George's eleven-tablet version with five tablets to follow. Chapters 7 and 9 (Humbaba's killing, Enkidu's death) mirror each other as polyphonic scripts; 6 and 10 both use repetition; 5 and 11 are both dimeter with sung passages; 4 and 12 blank verse; 3 and 13 terza rima; 2 and 14 ottava rima; 1 and 15 caesura quatrains; the Prologue and Epilogue form one specular poem. The hinge lines are Inanna's curse 'You! You're only two thirds god! / I'll strike the part of you that's human' (p. 71). Lewis explains: had he been wholly divine he would be immortal, and his one-third humanity 'propels the narrative of the rest of the epic'. Enkidu's death is therefore the goddess's revenge, not an arbitrary decree, which puts a woman's wounded power at the story's centre.

**Arc interpretation**

> Maturity and repentance, read through a feminist lens. Lewis calls the epic 'a rite of passage which questions the value of the heroic in favour of the importance of learning, acquiring wisdom and understanding, and being able to change'. Her ending makes that explicit: in Chapter 14 Gilgamesh must 'acknowledge his mistakes before he can repent and ask forgiveness from the deities he has offended', and only then 'assume the mantle of a chastened and more responsible leader'. The women drive that arc. The Cannonball Read review notes that 'without them helping him along he would not have made it'. Ninsun gets her own chanted chapter, Siduri becomes a veiled folktale guide speaking emesal, and Inanna is given more dignity: the narrator frames Gilgamesh's insults as gratuitous ('Like a shower of stones his insults hit') and has Shamash seize the moment to 'equal great Inanna', reading the episode as the shift from matriarchy to patriarchy. Lewis's framing in 2013 was also political: 'tyranny, hubris, irresponsibility of leaders and destruction of forests'. She says she did not set out to write a 'feminist version per se' but to rebalance 'the masculine and feminine energies of the original'.

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Deliberately polymetric, so every chapter changes form. Unrhymed four-beat quatrains with a marked caesura, echoing Anglo-Saxon alliterative verse, frame the book (Prologue, chapters 1 and 15). Blank verse in iambic pentameter for chapters 4 and 12 ('the easiest storytelling mode'); ottava rima (2, 14) and terza rima (3, 13), which she found hardest; dimeter with chant and plainsong-style call and response (5, 11); a specular lament modelled on the Song of Songs (10); polyphonic 'scripts' for Humbaba's death and Enkidu's (7, 9). The idiom is modern British and performable, with terse Anglo-Saxon diction modelled on Ted Hughes's Tales from Ovid ('A bolus of everything, but / As if aborted' was her benchmark). There is little archaism and heavy use of alliteration and repetition, though repetition is rationed to keep the story moving. Schmidt describes 'a gapped or sprung phrasal line'.

**Sample line**

> "You! You're only two thirds god! / I'll strike the part of you that's human" (Inanna to Gilgamesh, Chapter 8 'Inanna and Gilgamesh', Gilgamesh Retold, p. 71; the fulcrum line of the book's chiastic structure)

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Warm, from mainly UK literary outlets. Gavin Francis, New Statesman Books of the Year 2018: 'innovative, graceful, erudite and utterly unputdownable'. London Review of Books Bookshop Book of the Week (October 2018): 'marvellous new version'. Joan Acocella, 'How to Read Gilgamesh', The New Yorker (7 October 2019): 'very musical, with rhymes and chimes and shifting meters', with 'the nastiest' Humbaba killing, yet 'the most tender, the most tragic, the one, I think, that might be recommended by feminist scholars'. Hetta Howes, TLS (21 December 2018): 'vivid, even cinematic', engaging the source 'on a "subliminal level"' rather than as a historical artefact, in the tradition of Angela Carter. David Cooke, London Grip (2019): 'essential reading' for its 'magisterial synthesis' and 'impressive variety of metrical forms'. Theophilus Kwek, The North: 'daring and deliberate approach to formal experimentation'. Poetry Book Society Winter Bulletin 2018 selection. Theodore Ziolkowski: a 'spirited "response" of a contemporary poet to the original'. Michael Schmidt (Gilgamesh: The Life of a Poem, 2019) credits it with giving Enkidu more agency but calls the Shamhat scene 'fanciful', saying 'The sacramental should not play out in quite this way'.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Lewis is the clearest case of a poet who tried the machine-like route first and gave it up. In 2012 she sat down with a Babylonian grammar and produced word-by-word glosses ('Who sees/saw everything, land foundations / Proper ways knows entirety he understands'), then concluded that finding 'slight alternatives for some words' would only give 'a version like many others'. That is roughly what a translation model yields: a competent gloss with synonyms swapped. Everything distinctive in her book came from decisions no parallel corpus contains. She laid the chapters out on a floor to build a mirror structure around Inanna's curse, and chose a different verse form for each chapter to stand for different tellers over millennia. She rewrote the Shamhat scene because, as a woman, she could not accept the received version. She also took a word ('souls') from a conversation with an Iraqi poet, and tested lines in front of live audiences with oud music. The cost is the same as with other poets' versions. The gaps, the tablet numbering and the line between Sumerian, Babylonian and Lewis herself disappear, and she openly adds a Christian-flavoured redemption. Against Armitage, whose version rests on Jacob Dahl's philology, Lewis shows what a poet does with one scholar's translation and no crib: the value is in the rebalancing and the form, not in any new access to the text.

**Essay hooks**

> 1) The abandoned gloss: her own 2012 attempt at Akkadian, 'Who sees/saw everything, land foundations / Proper ways knows entirety he understands', set beside George's 'He who saw the Deep, the country's foundation' (Lewis thesis, Goldsmiths 2021, pp. 71 to 72). A neat stand-in for raw machine output. 2) 'As I am unable to decipher cuneiform or to read Babylonian...' <a id="michael-schmidt-gilgamesh-the-life-of-a-poem-princeton-2019"></a>

## 12. Michael Schmidt, Gilgamesh: The Life of a Poem (Princeton, 2019)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Gilgamesh: The Life of a Poem

**Date**

> Princeton University Press, hardcover 24 September 2019 (ISBN 9780691195247, 192 pages); paperback ISBN 9780691205168; ebook ISBN 9780691196992. UK price 20 pounds.

**Creator**

> Michael Schmidt (b. 1947), British-based literary historian, poet, novelist and translator; founder of Carcanet Press and PN Review; Fellow of the Royal Society of Literature; OBE 2006; based in Manchester. Also author of The Novel: A Biography and The First Poets. As Carcanet's publisher he brought out Jenny Lewis's Gilgamesh Retold (2018), which the book discusses at length.

**Form**

> Critical essay and reception history (literary criticism), not a translation. A guided walk through the Standard Babylonian poem tablet by tablet, combined with an account of the text's rediscovery and fragmentary state, a comparative chapter ('How You Tell It') that sets many English versions of the same passage side by side, and a survey of modern poets' responses (Schmidt canvassed around fifty poets about their encounters with the poem).

### Source Handling

**Translator access**

> Schmidt does not read Akkadian or Sumerian and says so; he writes as a self-declared non-specialist. He relies chiefly on Andrew George's translations (Penguin 1999/2003 and the 2003 critical edition) and on Jenny Lewis's 2018 version, and quotes many other English versions for comparison. Thomas W. Hodgkinson (The Spectator, 12 October 2019) pointed out the irony: Schmidt is 'enjoyably scornful' of translations by people who cannot read the original languages, yet cannot read them himself.

**Non standard material**

> As a critic Schmidt praises visible marking over silent merging. He singles out George's system of roman type for secure text, brackets for probable restorations and italics for plausible but unattested interpolations (quoted in David Gullette's Arts Fuse review), and faults versions that blend recensions or invent connective material without saying so. His complaint about Sandars is that she 'plays false with sequence' and cut the verbatim repetitions to make the poem easier for modern readers (Literary Review, December 2019).

**Gap policy**

> Schmidt argues that the gaps should be shown, not smoothed over. He treats the fragmentary, multi-lingual state of the poem as a feature: 'We are not dealing with a single world and time. We are not dealing with a single language.' He tells readers to 'follow the scholar's route' and read versions with notes that reveal lacunae and ambiguities, and says George's versions are readable because they 'remain focused on what we have of the original and do not pretend to take independent flight.' Max Norman (LARB, December 2019) summarises the point: George's foreignizing version keeps gaps and brackets, forcing readers to meet the poem as a historical document as well as a story.

### Reading of the Arc

**Arc interpretation**

> Maturity through grief, but held loosely and kept plural. Schmidt reads the story as the education of a wayward king through friendship and loss (Rod Mengham, one of the poets he quotes, says the poem 'enshrines friendship as the bedrock of our negotiations with the world'), and pays attention to Humbaba as 'defender of his luxuriant, perfumed and peaceful kingdom', giving the cedar forest episode an ecological reading against the poem's urban setting. His larger claim is that the poem should not be read as a proto-Homeric epic with a coherent narrator: 'We will never penetrate to the subjectivity of the poem, the "I" of the narrator', which is 'an invention of the translator'. Joan Acocella (New Yorker, 7 October 2019) summarised his view that Gilgamesh is not a finished composition but 'something more like life, untidy, ambiguous'. Max Norman (LARB) describes Schmidt's framing of the poem as an agon, a contest that resists both translation and imitation.

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Schmidt writes prose criticism, not verse. Reviewers describe the register as 'learned yet lively' (John Butler, Asian Review of Books), 'sure-footed in its scholarship but hip and occasionally hilarious in its tone' (David Gullette, Arts Fuse). As a critic of metre he mocks archaising hexameters (Campbell Thompson), dislikes the prim idiom of Speiser, and values Lewis's 'gapped or sprung phrasal line' as 'the most voice-friendly version since Edwin Morgan's theatre adaptation'.

**Sample line**

> "Gilgamesh did not know it was 'the first epic poem.' It did not know it was an epic at all." (Schmidt, Gilgamesh: The Life of a Poem, 2019, quoted by John Butler, Asian Review of Books, 26 September 2019)

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Largely favourable. Joan Acocella, The New Yorker (7 October 2019): 'Schmidt's argument for the poem as poetry, in the modern sense, concrete, unglazed, tough on the mind, is touching and persuasive'; she read it 'spellbound, in one sitting'. James Romm, Wall Street Journal: 'freshly framed observations help renew one of the world's oldest surviving tales.' Publishers Weekly: 'An insightful, stimulating book sure to breathe new life into the would-be immortal king.' John Butler, Asian Review of Books (26 September 2019): 'If you have never read Gilgamesh before, Schmidt could be Virgil to your Dante.' David Cooke, The Manchester Review: 'stimulating, informative and elegantly written', hoped to become 'an indispensable guide for all future readers of Gilgamesh'. David Gullette, The Arts Fuse: 'wonderfully readable'. Max Norman, Los Angeles Review of Books (19 December 2019): praises the 'careful and self-reflexive approach'. Peter McDonald: 'a remarkable and hugely worthwhile book'. The main dissent is Thomas W. Hodgkinson, The Spectator (12 October 2019): 'as many oversights as insights', too much plot summary, and the omission of George Smith's discovery story from a book claiming to tell the poem's 'life'.

**Controversies**

> Two charges. First, consistency: Schmidt attacks 'languageless' translators (they 'drain the stream and harvest the fish by hand') and quotes Gabriel Josipovici that 'imitation as against informed translation is a license for laziness', yet he cannot read Akkadian either (Hodgkinson, Spectator). Second, conflict of interest: he publishes Jenny Lewis through Carcanet and gives her version prominent praise. Butler also notes his dismissal of epic-genre readings 'may raise some eyebrows, scholarly or otherwise'. No dispute over provenance or authorship.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Schmidt gives Lee a ready vocabulary for the machine versus human question without ever mentioning machines. He sorts versions by how honestly they show what is missing: George's brackets and italics on one side, the smooth prose of Sandars and the fluent retellings of poets without Akkadian on the other. That is the same split between a model's fluent output and a scholar's marked-up uncertainty. His line that the narrator's 'I' is 'an invention of the translator' fits generated text especially well: any coherent voice a machine gives Gilgamesh is supplied, not recovered. His 'How You Tell It' chapter works as a benchmark: one passage, many renderings, each revealing its era's taste (Campbell Thompson's 'bosom', Speiser's primness, the Bad Sex Award candidates). A model's rendering would be one more entry in that line-up, and Schmidt's test is whether it takes 'independent flight' or stays with 'what we have of the original'. What he says still needs a human is judgement about form and ethics: choosing to leave the gaps open, noticing a colonial frame, and hearing the 'static' when 'the volume refuses to be evenly controlled'.

**Essay hooks**

> 1) 'Gilgamesh did not know it was "the first epic poem." It did not know it was an epic at all.' (quoted by Butler, Asian Review of Books). 2) 'We will never penetrate to the subjectivity of the poem, the "I" of the narrator', which is 'an invention of the translator' (same review). 3) Translators without the language 'drain the stream and harvest the fish by hand' (Schmidt, Literary Review, December 2019). 4) Some recent versions of the Shamhat scene are 'worthy of the Bad Sex in Fiction Award' (same). 5) Speiser's 1955 version is 'varnished and prim' with 'an instinct for cliché'; Campbell Thompson's 1928 hexameters give Shamhat a 'bosom' and 'comeliness'. 6) Sandars's million-selling Penguin is 'inadvertently colonial' because she gave the poem an Odyssey-style first-person narrator (Spectator). 7) George's versions work because they 'do not pretend to take independent flight'. 8) Is it 'the first road novel, the first trip to hell, the first Deluge, the first heterosexual romance in poetry?' 9) Irony: a critic who cannot read Akkadian scolding translators who cannot read Akkadian (Hodgkinson, Spectator), which mirrors any human reviewing a model's translation. 10) Rilke called it 'das Epos der Todesfurcht', the epic of the fear of death.

## 14. Irkalla: Gilgamesh's Dream (Mohamed Al-Daradji, 2025)

### Basic Info

**Name:** Irkalla: Gilgamesh's Dream (Arabic: Irkala, Hilm Gilgamesh)

**Date**

> 2025. World premiere in the Piazza Grande section of the 78th Locarno Film Festival, August 2025; then Toronto (TIFF, September 2025) and the Red Sea International Film Festival (Arab premiere, December 2025). Iraqi premiere at Al-Jadiriya Mall cinema, Baghdad, 8 February 2026, with Iraqi theatrical release from Eid al-Fitr 2026. Later festival dates include SXSW London 2026 and FESCAAAL 2026 (Milan), where it won the Premio ACEC.

### Source Handling

**Non standard material**

> Not applicable in the textual sense. The film silently fuses epic episodes with other Mesopotamian iconography (the lamassu) and with a new plot about orphans in 2019 Baghdad. The myth is marked off formally by switching to hand-drawn animation, framed as a cartoon series about Gilgamesh and Irkalla that the teacher Maryam shows in her bus school (one review says a VHS tape given to Chum-Chum).

**Gap policy**

> Smoothed over. The film uses the myth as a clean, whole story that a child can believe in; lacunae in the tablets are not acknowledged.

**Typography of absence**

> Nothing on screen marks the gaps in the ancient text. Absence is instead carried by the plot: the missing parents, bodies thrown into the Tigris, a mute child, and a fragmented, disorienting editing style that one critic reads as a sign that 'narrative coherence is no longer possible' (Martin Kudlac, ScreenAnarchy).

### Reading of the Arc

**Turning point**

> The hinge is the loss of the companion and the resolve to go down to the underworld to bring him back: Al-Daradji summarises the epic as 'a king who goes on a journey to find his friend who has been killed, to reach Irkalla, the underworld' (ScreenAnarchy interview, 2025). In the film this maps onto Chum-Chum's parents, killed by ISIS and thrown into the Tigris, and his belief that the river holds the gate to Irkalla. Critics also read a parallel in 13-year-old Moody, who roams the city with street children and faces militia recruitment, as a Gilgamesh figure searching for a lost companion (Panos Kotzathanasis, Asian Movie Pulse). The Utnapishtim, Siduri, snake and walls-of-Uruk episodes are not reported as structural hinges in any review found.

**Arc interpretation**

> Grief and the refusal of death, reframed as a national condition. The epic becomes a child's coping story: a nine-year-old diabetic orphan uses the myth as 'a framework for belief' to imagine his dead parents can be recovered (Paul Emmanuel Enicola, The Movie Buff). The underworld is relocated to Baghdad itself: 'the underworld is not a distant realm but the city itself' (Kudlac, ScreenAnarchy). Al-Daradji frames it as a post-ISIS question: 'What happens to a city when it's liberated but the people still don't understand what happened?' The ending, per reviewers, avoids catharsis; the arc reads less as maturity or wisdom than as imagination used as a shield against unbearable loss, and as a diagnosis of Iraq's 'lost generation'. Myth, in the director's words, 'helps us understand who we are.'

### Voice and Register

**Diction and metre**

> Not verse. Contemporary Iraqi Arabic dialogue spoken by non-professional children cast from orphanages, set against hand-drawn animation for the mythic register. Critics describe a mix of documentary realism, 'post-apocalyptic aesthetics and social realism' (Kudlac) and kinetic protest footage; real 2019 Tishreen protest scenes were intercut with staged ones.

**Sample line**

> "Gilgamesh is about a king who goes on a journey to find his friend who has been killed, to reach Irkalla, the underworld. Chum-Chum also has this hope, to go to Irkalla to bring back his family." (Mohamed Al-Daradji, ScreenAnarchy interview, Locarno 2025). From the film itself, as quoted by Asian Movie Pulse: "I dreamt I was in Holland and Chum-Chum had recovered and was having fun."

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Broadly positive. ScreenAnarchy (Martin Kudlac): 'The film shifts fluidly between intimate character study, societal critique, and kinetic urgency... a sobering commentary on Iraq's lost generation.' Asian Movie Pulse (Panos Kotzathanasis, March 2026): 'an excellent work' showing Iraqi cinema's promising trajectory; the animated segments 'well integrated... providing brief relief from the bleakness.' The Movie Buff (Paul Emmanuel Enicola, TIFF 2025, grade B): 'a necessary, albeit uneasy, watch'; for viewers who do not know the epic 'the mythological layer can feel murky, even distracting', and the supernatural elements risk overshadowing human stakes that need no embellishment. Cineuropa also reviewed it (not accessible). Won the Premio ACEC at FESCAAAL 2026.

**Regional reception**

> A direct return of the epic to its region of origin: an Iraqi director, Baghdad setting, Iraqi orphan cast and Arabic dialogue. Arab premiere at Red Sea IFF (Saudi Arabia); also shown at Doha Film. Baghdad premiere on 8 February 2026 at Al-Jadiriya Mall cinema filled the house; Iraqi artist Hafiz Laaibi called it 'a significant work and transformation in cinema', and others hoped it would revive Iraqi box office (964media). Iraqi theatrical release from Eid al-Fitr 2026. Covered in Arabic press (Asharq Al-Awsat, Sayidaty, Al-Muraqeb Al-Iraqi), where Al-Daradji said the project had been growing for 15 years before writing began in 2019.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> The film is a strong counterweight to machine-assisted recovery of the epic. Its authority does not come from the text at all: no adviser, no tablets, no fidelity to lines. It comes from lived experience, nearly two decades of the director working with war-orphaned children, a cast drawn from orphanages, and real protest footage captured over 45 difficult days. What a human contributes here is the one thing a corpus cannot supply: deciding what the story is for in Baghdad in 2019, turning the search for Enkidu into a child's search for parents dumped in the Tigris. Even the myth itself is rendered by hand-drawn animation rather than generated imagery. For an essay, it shows the far end of the spectrum: when the epic goes home, it is valued as a living story that helps people 'understand who we are', not as a recovered artefact.

**Essay hooks**

> 1) The epic comes home as a children's cartoon inside the film, shown in a double-decker bus turned mobile school (ScreenAnarchy review; Darvideo). 2) Al-Daradji: 'Myth helps us understand who we are' (ScreenAnarchy interview, 2025). 3) The Tigris as the gate to Irkalla, where ISIS threw the bodies of the boy's parents: the waters of death made literal. 4) 'The underworld is not a distant realm but the city itself' (Kudlac, ScreenAnarchy). 5) Numbers: 150 child actors, 109 shooting days, a 15-year gestation, sometimes one shot a day during real 2019 protests (964media; ScreenAnarchy interview). 6) A full house in Baghdad on 8 February 2026 (964media), a contrast with the museum and database afterlife of the tablets. 7) Counterpoint: to outsiders 'the mythological layer can feel murky, even distracting' (The Movie Buff), a reminder that the epic reads differently at home and abroad.

## 16. George Smith and the Flood tablet (1872)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> George Smith and the Flood tablet (British Museum K.3375, Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian Gilgamesh epic)

**Creator**

> George Smith (26 March 1840 to 19 August 1876). At fourteen he was apprenticed to the London firm Bradbury and Evans to learn banknote engraving, taught himself cuneiform, and was taken on at the British Museum to sort and join Kuyunjik fragments. Institutions: British Museum, Society of Biblical Archaeology, and the Daily Telegraph, whose editor Edwin Arnold funded the 1873 dig. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Smith_(Assyriologist)

**Form**

> Physical clay tablet: K.3375, a 7th-century BC Neo-Assyrian cuneiform tablet from Kuyunjik, about 15.2 x 13.3 cm, on display in Room 55 of the British Museum; plus Smith's lecture, journal article and 1876 book presenting his translation. Sources: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_K-3375 ; https://sites.google.com/site/100objectsbritishmuseum/home/flood-tablet

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> The find made Gilgamesh visible to the modern world, but through the wrong door. Smith met the epic first as a flood story, and for decades it was read in the West as a Babylonian parallel to Genesis 6-9 rather than as a poem about friendship, grief and mortality. In 'The Chaldean Account of Genesis' (1876) the hero is 'Izdubar', Enkidu is 'Heabani' and the flood hero is 'Hasisadra'. The name Gilgamesh was only settled in 1890, when Theophilus Pinches published 'Exit Gishtubar!' in The Babylonian and Oriental Record. Smith did see that the flood sat inside a longer narrative, and his 1876 book assembled the first sequence of 'Izdubar' tablets, including the Ishtar episode and Heabani's death, so the arc as a whole began here even if its emotional centre was not yet understood. Sources: https://sacred-texts.com/ane/caog/caog14.htm ; https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60559/60559-h/60559-h.htm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophilus_Pinches ; https://www.wedgepod.org/49-george-smith-the-man-behind-the-headlines-transcript/

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Immediate public sensation. Gladstone, then Prime Minister, was in the audience on 3 December 1872 and rose to make a speech linking the find to Homer. The Daily Telegraph ran three articles within a week and reported that 'an extraordinary public interest' had been aroused. Smith was recognised as the most gifted reader of cuneiform of his generation, and Queen Victoria granted his widow and children a pension of 150 pounds a year after his death. Later assessments are warm but corrective: Andrew George's critical edition (Oxford, 2003) treats Smith as the founder of Gilgamesh studies, and David Damrosch's 'The Buried Book' (2007) makes Smith's story the frame for the epic's modern life. Sources: https://www.wedgepod.org/49-george-smith-the-man-behind-the-headlines-transcript/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Smith_(Assyriologist) ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/epic-hero-153362976/

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Traditional cuneiform scholarship and manual physical joining. Smith sorted Kuyunjik fragments by eye, matching script, clay and content, joining pieces and reading the signs directly. He spotted the account of a ship resting on a mountain and a bird sent out, recognised the parallel with Noah, and looked for duplicates among other Nineveh copies to fill the gaps. Part of the text was crusted over, and he had to wait for the museum conservator Robert Ready to clean it. Sources: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/epic-hero-153362976/ ; https://www.wedgepod.org/episode-49-george-smith-the-man-behind-the-headlines-short-version-transcript/

**Ai pipeline stage**

> None. The whole process was human: eye, memory and hand. The same Kuyunjik fragments are now being rejoined with computer help by Enrique Jimenez's Electronic Babylonian Library team at LMU Munich, which makes Smith's work the natural 'before' picture. Source: https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/49550/1/Jimenez_New_Fragments_of_Gilgames_and_other_Literary_Texts_from_Kuyunjik.pdf

**Scale metrics**

> The Kuyunjik Collection ('Ashurbanipal Library') is a label for around 32,000 cuneiform tablets and fragments from the British Museum's excavations at Nineveh. Most were gathered on the orders of Ashurbanipal (king of Assyria 669 to about 630 BC), though some are earlier. K.3375 dates to the 7th century BC and measures about 15.2 x 13.3 cm. Smith's 1873 fragment preserved most of seventeen lines and was found in about a week of digging (the fifth day, according to Britannica). He made three trips to the region (1873, November 1873, and 1876) and died on the third, aged 36. I did not confirm the exact line count of Tablet XI or of the Flood episode, so I have left it out. Sources: https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/asbp/whatisthelibrary/ ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/epic-hero-153362976/ ; https://www.britannica.com/biography/George-Smith-British-Assyriologist ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Smith_(Assyriologist)

**What was found**

> A Neo-Assyrian copy of Tablet XI of the Standard Babylonian epic. In it Utnapishtim tells Gilgamesh how the god Ea warned him of the flood the gods planned, how he built a boat and loaded it with his family and animals, how he rode out a seven-day storm, landed on Mount Nisir, and sent out a dove, a swallow and a raven. In the epic this is the story Gilgamesh hears at the end of his search for immortality, just before he fails the sleep test and loses the plant of rejuvenation to the snake. Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh_flood_myth ; https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_K-3375

**Provenance and ethics**

> Excavated at Kuyunjik in the 1850s during the British Museum's Nineveh digs (Rassam in the North Palace) under Ottoman permits, and shipped to London, where it has stayed (registration K.3375, Room 55). That was legal under the rules of the time, but it is part of the colonial-era removal of Mesopotamian heritage to European museums. The digital copy is open: the British Museum collection online, the Ashurbanipal Library Project on Oracc, and the Electronic Babylonian Library all publish images or editions, even though the object stays in London. Sources: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_K-3375 ; https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/asbp/whatisthelibrary/

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Smith is the clearest human baseline for the recovery story. What he did by hand is what machines are now asked to do: sort tens of thousands of fragments, spot which belong together, read damaged signs and flag a passage worth attention. What no pipeline would have supplied is the jump he made. He read a line about a ship on a mountain and a bird sent out, knew it was Noah, and knew it would matter to his audience. The later corrections show the limits of a brilliant single reader working fast: 'Izdubar' for Gilgamesh, and a 1873 fragment slotted into the wrong poem. These are the kinds of errors that systematic, machine-assisted matching now catches. The meaning still came from a person, and so did the drama that got a newspaper to pay for a dig.

**Essay hooks**

> A former banknote engraver, self-taught, reads the oldest known flood story in the British Museum and (in Budge's much later and doubtful account) starts undressing from excitement: 'I am the first man to read that after more than two thousand years of oblivion.' The Prime Minister sits in the audience for the lecture on 3 December 1872. A newspaper pays a thousand guineas to send him to Nineveh. On 14 May 1873, about a week into the dig, he pulls out seventeen lines that seem to fill the gap, and they turn out to come from a different, older poem. He calls the hero 'Izdubar' because nobody could yet read the name, and it took until Pinches in 1890 to fix it. He dies in Aleppo in 1876, aged 36. Sources: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/epic-hero-153362976/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Smith_(Assyriologist) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theophilus_Pinches

## 17. Sîn-lēqi-unninni and the Standard Babylonian version

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Sîn-lēqi-unninni and the Standard Babylonian version of the Epic of Gilgamesh (Akkadian incipit 'Sha naqba īmuru', 'He Who Saw the Deep')

**Date**

> Editorial work placed in the late second millennium BC: 'probably in the period between 1300 BC and 1000 BC' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni); Tigay puts it in the Middle Babylonian period, 'some time in the last half or quarter of the second millennium' (Tigay, The Evolution of the Gilgamesh Epic, 1982, https://epdf.pub/the-evolution-of-the-gilgamesh-epic.html). The date is a scholarly inference, not a record. The version was 'current in the eighth to second centuries BCE' (BMCR 2004.04.21, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/). Key surviving copies are 7th-century BC tablets from Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh; the Flood tablet K.3375 was found by Hormuzd Rassam in 1853 and George Smith presented his reading of it on 3 December 1872 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh_flood_myth, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Smith_(Assyriologist)). The authorship attribution comes from W. G. Lambert, 'A Catalogue of Texts and Authors', Journal of Cuneiform Studies 16 (1962) (cited in Helle 2018, https://sophushelle.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/helle-2018-role-of-authors.pdf)

**Creator**

> Sîn-lēqi-unninni ('Sîn (the Moon God) is one who accepts my prayer'), a mašmaššu, i.e. exorcist-priest, of Uruk (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni; Tigay 1982: 'Mesopotamian tradition credited this epic to an exorcist-priest named Sîn-leqi-unninnī'). Known only through later tradition. The Neo-Assyrian Catalogue of Texts and Authors credits the Epic of Gilgamesh to him (Lambert 1962; Helle 2018), and 'his name appears in a catalogue of texts and authors, not on any tablet of the poem itself' (Michael Schmidt, Literary Hub, https://lithub.com/who-were-the-scribes-who-actually-wrote-down-the-epic-of-gilgamesh/). The Uruk List of Kings and Sages, copied at Uruk on 16 May 165 BC by the lamentation priest Anu-belshunu, reads in Helle's translation: '[In the time of] king? [Gilgam]esh?: the scholar was Sin-leqi-unninni.' The copyist signs as 'descendant of Sin-leqi-unnenni' (Helle 2018). Modern reconstruction of the text is chiefly by Andrew George (The Babylonian Gilgamesh Epic, Oxford 2003) and, since 2018, the Electronic Babylonian Library team at LMU Munich led by Enrique Jiménez (https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091)

**Form**

> Cuneiform clay tablets in Standard Babylonian Akkadian: an edited recension of older Old Babylonian material, which 'runs to some three thousand lines, split into twelve standard "Tablets" or chapters' (BMCR 2004.04.21, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/). Tablets I to XI form the epic proper; Tablet XII renders the Sumerian 'Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni) and 'was probably appended at a later date' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). About two-thirds of the twelve-tablet version has been recovered (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). The 'version' is a text reconstructed from many manuscripts, not a single physical object

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> Comparing the recensions shows the Standard version reframed the story. The Old Babylonian version was titled after its incipit 'Shūtur eli sharrī' ('Surpassing All Other Kings'); the Standard version opens 'Sha naqba īmuru' ('He Who Saw the Deep(s)') (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). Tigay (1982) notes that the Old Babylonian hymn 'Surpassing the rulers, renowned...' 'formed the introduction to the Old Babylonian version', while the new prologue virtually ignores heroic deeds and instead 'mentions the outcome of Gilgamesh's quest, the understanding that he gained', stressing the verbs 'see' and 'know'. The prologue tells the reader to open a tablet box and 'read aloud from the lapis-lazuli tablet [How/that] Gilgamesh went through all hardships' (Tigay's translation, I 22-26), and its walls-of-Uruk passage is repeated at the end of Tablet XI ('XI, 303-7 = I, i, 16-21'), framing the poem as a ring that ends on the city rather than the lost plant (https://epdf.pub/the-evolution-of-the-gilgamesh-epic.html). Utnapishtim's Flood story sits in Tablet XI (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni). Siduri's Old Babylonian advice to accept death and enjoy life 'is absent' from the Standard version, where she only directs Gilgamesh to the boatman Urshanabi (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siduri). How much of this is Sîn-lēqi-unninni's own work is not settled: Tigay says 'the prologue and Tablet XII were first added in this version or earlier, at some intermediate stage', and that his achievement was 'some important Middle Babylonian version' rather than the original. The net effect is a wisdom epic about mortality and the limits of kingship, where the older poem opened as praise of a heroic king

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Assyriologists treat Sîn-lēqi-unninni as the nearest thing the epic has to an author, with caution. Lambert's 1962 catalogue publication established the attribution. Tigay (1982) 'helped establish the notion that the standard "late version" of the epic was in ways an original work of art itself, rather than a collection of earlier anecdotes' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_H._Tigay), while concluding that his contribution was a Middle Babylonian version, not the first one (https://epdf.pub/the-evolution-of-the-gilgamesh-epic.html). Andrew George argues he 'gave [The Epic of Gilgamesh] its final, fixed form' (quoted at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni). Michael Schmidt describes the contribution as curatorial, organising scattered Old Babylonian stories into one narrative (https://lithub.com/who-were-the-scribes-who-actually-wrote-down-the-epic-of-gilgamesh/). Sophus Helle (2018) reads the late interest in named authors as a first-millennium development, 'as cuneiform culture waned, the figure of the author waxed', with authors invoked 'as mythical family ancestors' (https://sophushelle.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/helle-2018-role-of-authors.pdf)

**Controversies**

> (1) Author or editor: whether he composed new poetry or compiled and revised older material; Tigay and Schmidt lean to editor or curator, George credits him with the fixed form. (2) Historicity: all evidence is centuries later, and Wikipedia notes 'He may have been a legendary figure', later described as 'Gilgamesh's wise councilor' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni). (3) Scope: Tigay cannot say whether the prologue and Tablet XII were added by him or at an intermediate stage. (4) Dating rests on indirect evidence (1300 to 1000 BC is an estimate). (5) A factual error in circulation: Wikipedia says 'His name is listed in the text itself', but Schmidt states his name appears 'in a catalogue of texts and authors, not on any tablet of the poem itself' (https://lithub.com/who-were-the-scribes-who-actually-wrote-down-the-epic-of-gilgamesh/)

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Cuneiform scholarship and textual criticism: decipherment of the Nineveh tablets (George Smith, 1872), joins of fragments across collections, and collation of manuscripts into a critical edition (George 2003, from 184 fragments, 'some 25 of which are completely new to this edition', BMCR 2004.04.21, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/). Comparison of Old, Middle and Standard Babylonian recensions isolates the editorial layer (Tigay 1982). The attribution rests on reading ancient catalogues (Lambert 1962) and the Uruk List of Kings and Sages (first published by Jan van Dijk, 1962). Photo-based identification of stray fragments continued (Jiménez, 'New Fragments of Gilgameš and Other Literary Texts from Kuyunjik', Iraq 76, 2014, three new Gilgamesh fragments found via the British Museum's online photographs, https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/49550/1/Jimenez_New_Fragments_of_Gilgames_and_other_Literary_Texts_from_Kuyunjik.pdf). Since 2018 the Electronic Babylonian Library has added computational string alignment against its digitised Fragmentarium

**Ai pipeline stage**

> Fragment matching. The eBL uses a 'string alignment algorithm specifically tailored for cuneiform' on transliterated fragments to find pieces that belong together; this is not machine learning. The project's separate AI work concerns disambiguating cuneiform sign readings (https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091). Human Assyriologists verify each match. Deciding what belongs to Sîn-lēqi-unninni's edition remains entirely human

**Scale metrics**

> George 2003: 184 fragments, against 108 available to Campbell Thompson (BMCR 2004.04.21, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/). Standard version: about 3,000 lines in twelve tablets (same source); about two-thirds recovered (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). eBL: before 2018 about 5,000 tablet fragments had been matched; in six years the team matched over 1,500 more, 'including 20 fragments from Gilgamesh that add detail to over 100 lines of the epic' (New York Times, Erik Ofgang, 12 August 2024, as quoted at https://paleojudaica.blogspot.com/2024/08/ai-is-finding-more-gilgamesh-epic.html?m=1). One Nineveh manuscript of the prologue, K.4981+, was rebuilt 'from nine discrete fragments previously transliterated in the Fragmentarium' (https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091). Manuscript dates: 7th century BC (Nineveh) to the 2nd century BC; the edit itself is dated about 1300 to 1000 BC

**What was found**

> Not a single object but an identity and an edition: the name of the epic's editor, recovered from the Catalogue of Texts and Authors (Lambert 1962) and the Uruk List of Kings and Sages (van Dijk 1962; Helle 2018), and the reconstructed Standard Babylonian text, with its knowledge-centred prologue 'He Who Saw the Deep', the ring structure closing on the walls of Uruk, the Flood story in Tablet XI and the appended Tablet XII. This recension is the spine most modern English translations follow

**Provenance and ethics**

> The Nineveh manuscripts were excavated for the British Museum in the mid-19th century under Ottoman rule; the Flood tablet K.3375 was found by Hormuzd Rassam in 1853 on a mission for Austen Henry Layard (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh_flood_myth) and remains in London. That was lawful at the time but belongs to the wider debate over colonial-era removal of Iraqi heritage. The Uruk List of Kings and Sages (excavation number W 20030,7) came from German excavations at Uruk and is in the Baghdad Museum (Helle 2018, https://sophushelle.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/helle-2018-role-of-authors.pdf). The British Museum's online photographs made the Kuyunjik collection searchable worldwide (Jiménez 2014), and the eBL Fragmentarium publishes transliterations openly, with more than 22,000 fragments digitised by 2023 (https://paleojudaica.blogspot.com/2024/08/ai-is-finding-more-gilgamesh-epic.html?m=1)

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Sîn-lēqi-unninni is the first person we can name who did what every later translator does: took an inherited Gilgamesh, cut, added and reordered it, and gave it a new frame. Machines now help rebuild his text. The eBL's alignment software finds pieces that belong to it faster than a scholar reading museum drawers could; 20 fragments and more than 100 lines since 2018. But the software recovers words, not choices. Deciding that a line belongs to his edition and not to an earlier layer (a question Tigay could not settle for the prologue), seeing that the prologue and the close of Tablet XI mirror each other, and reading that as a shift from heroic boast to wisdom about death, all take a human literary reader. There is a neat loop too: the ancient 'author' was himself a compiler working from sources, so the question AI now raises about authorship (is assembling prior text creative?) was already being asked of him. The epic's answer seems to be yes, if the assembler has a point of view

**Essay hooks**

> (1) A list copied at Uruk in 165 BC names him as Gilgamesh's own scholar: '[In the time of] king? [Gilgam]esh?: the scholar was Sin-leqi-unninni.' The copyist, Anu-belshunu, signed as his 'descendant' (Helle 2018, https://sophushelle.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/helle-2018-role-of-authors.pdf). An editor promoted into the story he edited. (2) His name means 'Sîn (the Moon God) is one who accepts my prayer', and his job was exorcist (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%AEn-l%C4%93qi-unninni). (3) The opening changed from 'Surpassing All Other Kings' to 'He Who Saw the Deep(s)' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh): Tigay notes the new prologue stresses 'see' and 'know', 'wisdom' and 'secret(s)'. (4) The prologue tells you to open a box and 'read aloud from the lapis-lazuli tablet' (Tigay's translation): the poem announces that it is a written document. (5) Siduri's famous enjoy-life speech is absent from the Standard version (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siduri), so some of the most quoted lines of 'Gilgamesh' are not in the text most people think they are reading. (6) His name appears 'in a catalogue of texts and authors, not on any tablet of the poem itself' (Schmidt, https://lithub.com/who-were-the-scribes-who-actually-wrote-down-the-epic-of-gilgamesh/). (7) The eBL's algorithm has added 20 Gilgamesh fragments and detail to over 100 lines since 2018 (NYT, 12 August 2024, via https://paleojudaica.blogspot.com/2024/08/ai-is-finding-more-gilgamesh-epic.html?m=1): the latest step in a chain of editors that begins with him

## 18. Sumerian poems, Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian versions

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Sumerian poems, Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian versions of Gilgamesh (the layered textual tradition, including the Bilgames/Gilgameš name question)

**Date**

> Sumerian poems: known from Old Babylonian copies 'especially the eighteenth century bc, but they probably go back to a period of intense creativity under the patronage of King Shulgi of Ur (2094–2047 bc)' (A. R. George, 'The Epic of Gilgamesh', Cambridge Companion to the Epic, 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf). Old Babylonian Akkadian epic ('Surpassing all kings'): eighteenth century BCE manuscripts (same source). Middle Babylonian and peripheral versions, 1600-1000 BCE, from Babylonia, Hattusa, Emar, Ugarit and Megiddo (same source). Standard Babylonian epic ('He who saw the Deep'): standardised by Sîn-lēqi-unninni, who 'probably lived towards the end of the second millennium bc' (George, same source), 'sometime between 1300 and 1000 BCE' per https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh; oldest copies ninth or eighth century BCE, best copies from Ashurbanipal's Nineveh library (7th century BCE), last dated manuscript about 130 BCE (http://ancientworldonline.blogspot.com/2016/04/the-standard-babylonian-epic-of.html). Modern recovery: George Smith's 1872 reading of the Flood tablet; the Old Babylonian Pennsylvania tablet published by Stephen Langdon in 1917 (PBS X/3, https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Epic_of_Gilgamish) and the Yale tablet by Jastrow and Clay in 1920 (https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11000/11000-h/11000-h.htm); Tigay's 'Evolution of the Gilgamesh Epic' 1982; Cavigneaux and Al-Rawi's Me-Turan edition of the Death of Gilgamesh 2000 (https://brill.com/display/title/7385?language=en); George's critical edition 2003 (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/); Rubio's name article 2012.

**Creator**

> Anonymous Sumerian court and school poets; Old Babylonian scribes and singers (George finds 'no sign of any one author who might have been responsible for the poem's original creation'); the Standard Babylonian text 'was associated in Babylonian tradition with the name Sîn-leqi-unninni, a scholar-exorcist who was claimed as an ancestor by scribes of Uruk' (George 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf). Modern reassembly by George Smith, Stephen Langdon, Morris Jastrow and Albert Clay, Samuel Noah Kramer, Jeffrey Tigay, Antoine Cavigneaux and Farouk Al-Rawi, Andrew George, Enrique Jiménez and the eBL team, with Gonzalo Rubio on the name question.

**Form**

> Physical clay tablets in cuneiform across several layers: five independent Sumerian narrative poems, an Old Babylonian Akkadian epic known only from scattered manuscripts of at least two versions, Middle Babylonian and western peripheral fragments, and a twelve-tablet Standard Babylonian Akkadian epic, reassembled by scholars into critical editions (George 2003; the eBL Corpus).

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> Each layer changes the story. The Sumerian poems 'are independent compositions and do not form a literary whole' (George 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf): Gilgamesh and Huwawa, Gilgamesh and the Bull of Heaven, Gilgamesh and Aga, Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld, and the Death of Gilgamesh (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). 'In the earlier Sumerian texts, Enkidu is Gilgamesh's servant' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh); the Babylonian epic makes him a friend and equal, so his death drives the quest for immortality. In the Old Babylonian Sippar (Meissner) tablet the alewife tells Gilgamesh to accept death, enjoy life and start a family, which Bendt Alster called the first recorded carpe diem. In the Standard Babylonian Tablet X 'advice unrelated to the journey itself which she offers in the older version is absent'; she only explains the route and sends him to Urshanabi, and Andrew George treats the change as one of Sîn-lēqi-unninni's innovations (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siduri). The Standard Babylonian version also reframes the poem, opening 'He who saw the Deep' instead of 'Surpassing all kings' (George 2010), and closes on the view of Uruk from the wall. Tablet XII is 'a translation of the latter part of one of the Sumerian poems of Gilgamesh', related to the epic 'but not in form and structure' (George 2010); in it Enkidu goes down to the netherworld alive, after his death in Tablet VII. Names shift: in the Old Babylonian Sippar tablet the ferryman is Sursunabu, a name that 'no longer appears' in later versions (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urshanabi), and the flood hero is 'here called Uta-na'ishtim' (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh), later Uta-napishti. An even odder variant: the oldest Middle Babylonian fragment (MB Priv1, probably 16th century BCE) replaces Gilgamesh and Enkidu with the gods Sîn and Ea (George 2010).

### Reception

**Regional reception**

> Every layer comes from what is now Iraq. George: the eleven Old Babylonian pieces are 'all from Babylonia itself'; Standard Babylonian copies come from Nineveh (Mosul), Asshur, Kalah/Nimrud, Babylon and Uruk (George 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf). Sumerian copies come mainly from Nippur and Ur, and Iraqi excavators at Me-Turan (Tell Haddad) recovered new Death of Gilgamesh fragments (https://brill.com/display/title/7385?language=en). Most Nineveh tablets went to the British Museum in the 19th century. In the Middle Babylonian period the poem was already international: 'parts of it have turned up at Megiddo in Palestine, Ugarit and Emar in Syria and Hattusa in Anatolia', with Hurrian and Hittite prose paraphrases (George 2010).

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Classic cuneiform philology: identifying fragments in museum collections, physical joins of broken pieces, collating duplicate manuscripts line by line into a composite 'score' edition, and aligning parallel versions across periods. George 2003 separates the sources into four periods instead of interpolating early material into the late poem as Campbell Thompson did (George 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf). Since 2018 the eBL Fragmentarium at LMU Munich adds computational matching: a string-alignment algorithm run over human transliterations has 'found several matchings that had escaped human attention', including a new fragment of the latest datable Gilgamesh manuscript (https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/).

**Ai pipeline stage**

> None for the historic reassembly of the Sumerian, Old Babylonian and Standard Babylonian layers, which was human philology. Recent additions to the Standard Babylonian text come partly from machine fragment matching (string alignment over transliterations) in the eBL Fragmentarium; imaging, reading and translation remain human (https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/).

**Scale metrics**

> George 2003: 'where Thompson's edition was based on 112 manuscripts, mine utilizes 218 pieces' (George 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf). Of these, 184 are first-millennium (Standard Babylonian era) fragments, counted at January 2003, against 108 for Campbell Thompson, 'some 25 of which are completely new to this edition' (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/; http://ancientworldonline.blogspot.com/2016/04/the-standard-babylonian-epic-of.html). The remainder match George's second-millennium counts: 11 Old Babylonian pieces giving 'a little over six hundred lines of poetry', from at least two versions, and 23 Middle Babylonian fragments (184 + 11 + 23 = 218). By about 2010 first-millennium sources had reached 'about 190', and 'since 2003 no fewer than ten pieces of the poem have become available' (George 2010). The Standard Babylonian poem 'is about two-thirds recovered; it must once have extended to about 3,600 lines' (George 2010; BMCR's reviewer gives 'some three thousand lines'). Five Sumerian poems. Since 2018 the eBL has added 20 Gilgamesh fragments touching more than 100 lines (NYT, 2024, per the eBL results file). Manuscripts span about 1,800 years.

**What was found**

> Not a single find but a stack of them. The Nineveh Standard Babylonian tablets (Flood story on Tablet XI read by George Smith in 1872) gave the twelve-tablet frame. Old Babylonian tablets showed an earlier, differently shaped epic: the Pennsylvania tablet (Tablet II, bought 1914, said to come from Larsa; Enkidu's arrival in Uruk) and the Yale tablet (Tablet III, the Cedar Forest plans) (https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Epic_of_Gilgamish; https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11000/11000-h/11000-h.htm); the Sippar (Meissner) tablet with the alewife's advice, Sursunabu and Uta-na'ishtim; and pieces from Tell Harmal, Ishchali and Nippur (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epic_of_Gilgamesh). Sumerian school tablets from Nippur and Ur, plus the Me-Turan (Tell Haddad) finds for the Death of Gilgamesh (https://brill.com/display/title/7385?language=en), showed the episodic stories behind both, including the direct source of Tablet XII.

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> The layered history shows that assembly has always been an editorial and literary act. Someone in the Old Babylonian period made a servant into a friend whose death breaks the hero; a later editor cut Siduri's advice about enjoying life (George's reading, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siduri), opened with 'He who saw the Deep', and the tradition kept Tablet XII even though it contradicts the plot. Machines now find matching fragments at scale (eBL string alignment), which extends the recovery work. They do not make the choices ancient editors made or that modern translators still make: which Siduri to print, whether to call the hero Bilgames or Gilgamesh, whether Tablet XII is an ending or an appendix. The name question is a good example. It turns on sign values, phonology and scribal convention, and Rubio's 2012 case is a scholar weighing evidence against 'a long-standing Assyriological convention'.

**Essay hooks**

> 1) 'There is no single Epic of Gilgamesh: parts of different versions survive, spread across eighteen hundred years of history' (George 2010, https://beckassets.blob.core.windows.net/product/readingsample/801345/9780521880947_excerpt_001.pdf). 2) The numbers: Campbell Thompson had 112 manuscripts; George's 2003 edition used 218 pieces, 184 of them first-millennium; the Standard Babylonian poem is still only about two-thirds recovered (George 2010; https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/). 3) In the Sumerian poems Enkidu is Gilgamesh's servant (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh); the friendship that makes the epic work came later. 4) Siduri's 'enjoy life' advice, the part many readers remember, survives on one Old Babylonian tablet and is absent from the standard version (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siduri). 5) The oldest Middle Babylonian fragment swaps Gilgamesh and Enkidu for the gods Sîn and Ea (George 2010). 6) Tablet XII is a translation of half a Sumerian poem, attached so that dead Enkidu walks again. 7) Even the hero's name is contested: Bilgames by convention, but 'there are good reasons to read Gilgameš, instead of "Bilgames," in Sumerian as well as in Akkadian' (Rubio, JCS 64, 2012, quoted at https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=73432). 8) People were still copying it about 130 BCE (http://ancientworldonline.blogspot.com/2016/04/the-standard-babylonian-epic-of.html).

<a id="tablet-xii"></a>

## 19. Tablet XII

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Tablet XII of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh (the appended Akkadian translation of the Sumerian poem 'Bilgames and the Netherworld', also called 'Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld' or, in older literature, 'Gilgamesh and the Huluppu Tree'; see https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilgamesh,_Enkidu,_and_the_Netherworld)

**Date**

> Sumerian source poem: possibly composed at the end of the third millennium BC and known from manuscripts of c. 18th century BC, from Nippur, Ur, Meturan and other sites (eBL introduction, https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4; https://spokenpast.com/articles/epic-of-gilgamesh-tablet-xii/). Akkadian Tablet XII: surviving manuscripts are first-millennium BC; the eBL edition lists eight Neo-Assyrian library copies from Nineveh (Ashurbanipal's library) plus two Nineveh quotations, one further Neo-Assyrian copy (BM 135909, provenance given only as Assyria) and two Neo-Babylonian copies from Babylon (BM 30559, BM 41862) (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII). When the translation was made and attached to the eleven-tablet epic is unknown; Abusch places it after the eleven-tablet version had taken a stabilised form (https://faculty.uml.edu/ethan_spanier/teaching/documents/abuschmeaningofgilgamesh.pdf). Modern milestones: S. N. Kramer, 'The Epic of Gilgameš and Its Sumerian Sources: A Study in Literary Evolution', JAOS 64.1 (1944), 7-23 (https://philpapers.org/rec/KRACTT-3); Aaron Shaffer, 'Sumerian Sources of Tablet XII of the Epic of Gilgameš', PhD dissertation, University of Pennsylvania, 1963 (https://etcsl.orinst.ox.ac.uk/bibliography.htm); A. R. George, critical edition, OUP 2003 (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/); Alhena Gadotti, 'Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld' and the Sumerian Gilgamesh Cycle, de Gruyter 2014, UAVA 10 (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2014/2014.11.15/); eBL online edition of SB XII (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII)

**Form**

> Physical cuneiform clay tablets in Akkadian carrying 153 lines in the eBL reconstruction (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII), the twelfth and last tablet of the series 'He who saw the Deep' (Sha naqba imuru). Textually a close, line-by-line translation of the second half of the Sumerian poem 'Bilgames and the Netherworld' (also 'Gilgamesh, Enkidu, and the Netherworld'): eBL's line notes align Tablet XII line 1 with Sumerian line 172 and the final line 153 with Sumerian line 291 (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII). The earlier '172 onwards' description is corroborated at https://www.mcgoodwin.net/pages/otherbooks/gilgamesh.html. The Sumerian poem's own ending (after line 291) is not rendered

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> Tablet XII gives the epic a second, darker ending and is the classic case of the text being reassembled rather than composed whole. Tablets I to XI form a ring: the poem opens and closes on the walls of Uruk. Tablet XII breaks that ring. Enkidu, who died in Tablet VII, is alive again and appears as Gilgamesh's servant rather than his friend and equal. It opens with Gilgamesh's lament, in eBL's translation: "Today, had I only left the ball in the carpenter's workshop!" (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII). The ball and mallet (pukku and mekku) have fallen into the Netherworld; Enkidu goes down to fetch them, ignores Gilgamesh's instructions on how to behave there, and is seized. Gilgamesh appeals to the gods, Enkidu's ghost rises, and the tablet turns into a catechism of the dead, ending with the unburied corpse whose ghost 'does not lie at rest' and the ghost with no one to make offerings, who "eats the scrapings from the pot (and) crusts of bread that are thrown away in the street." (eBL, lines 150-153, https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII). It ends on bleak information rather than acceptance. Readings split. Speiser's ANET introduction calls it 'an inorganic appendage to the epic proper' (https://www.granneman.com/teaching/washingtonuniversity/hell/readings/ancientneareast/xiigilgamesh). Abusch argues the addition 'is meaningful and intentionally changes the nature of the work': it turns the eleven-tablet story of hero versus king into a twelve-tablet story of hero versus god, supplying the knowledge Gilgamesh needs for his cultic role as judge of the Netherworld (https://faculty.uml.edu/ethan_spanier/teaching/documents/abuschmeaningofgilgamesh.pdf). The ancient scribes counted it as part of the series (see colophons under what_was_found). For translators it is a choice with a visible result: include it and the book ends in the underworld; drop it and the book ends on the walls

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Cuneiform scholarship and comparative philology. The Akkadian tablets came from the Nineveh finds; the link to the Sumerian poem came from Kramer's reconstruction of Sumerian literature (JAOS 1944, https://philpapers.org/rec/KRACTT-3) and Shaffer's comparison of Tablet XII with its Sumerian source (1963). George's 2003 critical edition collated the manuscripts into a score (https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2004/2004.04.21/). The eBL edition now presents Tablet XII as a line-by-line score of 11 manuscript witnesses, with each line cross-referenced to the Sumerian line it translates (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII). No machine step is recorded in the identification

**Scale metrics**

> 153 Akkadian lines in the eBL reconstruction, corresponding to lines 172-291 of the Sumerian poem (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII). 11 manuscript witnesses in eBL: 8 Neo-Assyrian library copies and 2 quotations from Nineveh, 1 further Neo-Assyrian copy, 2 Neo-Babylonian copies from Babylon (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII). The whole 'canonical' epic is known from some 75 manuscripts, the oldest 8th-century BC Assyrian, the latest 2nd-century BC Babylonian (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4). Sumerian source manuscripts date to c. 18th century BC (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4; https://spokenpast.com/articles/epic-of-gilgamesh-tablet-xii/). The Sumerian first half (lines 1-171: the huluppu tree, Inanna, the making of the pukku and mekku) is not translated in Tablet XII

**What was found**

> The twelfth tablet of the Standard Babylonian series: Gilgamesh's lament for his lost ball and mallet ("Today, had I only left the ball in the carpenter's workshop!", eBL line 1; Speiser's older ANET rendering: "That time when I verily ha[d] the Drum in the carpenter's house", https://www.granneman.com/teaching/washingtonuniversity/hell/readings/ancientneareast/xiigilgamesh), his instructions to Enkidu on the taboos of the Netherworld, Enkidu's transgression and seizure, the appeal to the gods, the rising of Enkidu's ghost, and the interrogation about the fates of the dead (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII). Its place in the epic is after the ring-closing return to Uruk in Tablet XI. The ancient colophons count it in the series: Nineveh manuscript K.3475 ends "Tablet XII, series of Gilgameš, [completed to] its end" and K.2774 ends "Tablet XII, 'He who saw the Deeps'" (eBL translations, https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII)

**Provenance and ethics**

> Excavated provenance under 19th-century colonial-era practice: the Nineveh tablets (K, Sm, Rm and BM numbers) and the Neo-Babylonian copies from Babylon are British Museum holdings (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII); no looting controversy attaches to Tablet XII specifically, unlike the Gilgamesh Dream Tablet. The digital copy is open: eBL publishes a full online edition with transliteration of every manuscript and English translation (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII), and George's score editions are open access via SOAS. The objects remain in London, not Iraq

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> Tablet XII is the clearest case where recovering the text is the easy part and deciding what to do with it is the hard part. The identification itself was a human feat of reading two dead languages side by side, but it is also exactly the kind of string matching machines now do well; eBL already cross-references every Tablet XII line to its Sumerian original (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII), and its cuneiBLAST-style alignment is built for exactly that kind of match (https://www.ebl.lmu.de/about). What no pipeline can settle is the editorial question every translator faces: does the poem end on the walls of Uruk or on Enkidu's ghost describing the unburied dead? That is a judgement about meaning, structure and taste, and the spread of answers (George includes, Foster drops, Mitchell omits, Sandars swaps in the Sumerian 'Death of Gilgamesh', Mason adds a short 'afterglimpse') shows it is a literary decision, not a data problem. The ancient scribe who attached it made the same kind of call, so the epic has always had an editor with a view

**Essay hooks**

> (1) The epic has two endings, and translators pick one: Mitchell omits Tablet XII, which he says most scholars consider not part of the epic (https://regarp.com/2018/03/31/review-of-gilgamesh-a-new-english-version-by-stephen-mitchell/). (2) The man who died in Tablet VII walks back into Tablet XII, the continuity error that shows the text was stitched together (https://www.worldhistory.org/gilgamesh/). (3) The whole adventure starts with a lost toy: "Today, had I only left the ball in the carpenter's workshop!" (eBL, https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII). (4) The last line is about the forgotten dead: he "eats the scrapings from the pot (and) crusts of bread that are thrown away in the street." (eBL line 153, https://www.ebl.lmu.de/corpus/L/1/4/SB/XII); Speiser: "Lees of the pot, crumbs of bread, offals of the street he eats." (https://www.granneman.com/teaching/washingtonuniversity/hell/readings/ancientneareast/xiigilgamesh). A much colder close than the walls of Uruk. (5) The ancient scribe signed off with "Tablet XII, series of Gilgameš, [completed to] its end" (K.3475, https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/Standard%20Babylonian/XII), so the appendix was canon to the people who copied it. (6) Abusch: the tablet 'was added in a mechanical fashion' yet 'the addition is meaningful and intentionally changes the nature of the work' (https://faculty.uml.edu/ethan_spanier/teaching/documents/abuschmeaningofgilgamesh.pdf). (7) Contrast for an essay on AI and craft: Kramer's 1944 article and Shaffer's 1963 dissertation did by hand the Sumerian-to-Akkadian matching that eBL's edition now displays line by line; choosing whether to print it is still a human call

## 20. Sulaymaniyah Tablet V (acquired 2011, published 2014)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Sulaymaniyah Tablet V: a new manuscript of Tablet V of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgamesh (Sulaymaniyah Museum, T.1447)

**Creator**

> Farouk N. H. Al-Rawi (identified the tablet and advised the purchase) and Andrew R. George (SOAS, University of London), co-authors of 'Back to the Cedar Forest: The Beginning and End of Tablet V of the Standard Babylonian Epic of Gilgameš'. Institution: Sulaymaniyah Museum, director Hashim Hama Abdullah; visit supported by Hero Talabani. Photographs widely circulated by Osama S. M. Amin

**Form**

> Physical clay tablet in cuneiform, about 11 x 9.5 x 3 cm, made from three fragments already joined when acquired; preserves the beginning and end of Tablet V. Published as a scholarly article with hand-copy, transliteration, translation and commentary

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Traditional cuneiform scholarship: Al-Rawi recognised the text among smuggled tablets; he and George read and collated the tablet in the museum over about five days, identified it as Tablet V of the Standard Babylonian epic by matching it against known manuscripts, and published a hand-copy, transliteration and translation. The three pieces were already physically joined

**Ai pipeline stage:** None. Identification, reading and translation were entirely human

**Scale metrics**

> One tablet, three joined fragments, about 11 x 9.5 x 3 cm; about 20 previously unknown lines added to Tablet V, plus better readings of lines already partly known; Neo-Babylonian manuscript (626-539 BCE); purchase price about $800, from a batch of 80-90 tablets

**What was found**

> The beginning and end of Tablet V of the Standard Babylonian epic: Gilgamesh and Enkidu arrive at the Cedar Forest and see the cedars dripping aromatic sap and hear the din of birds, cicadas and monkeys; Humbaba's court music; and, after the killing, Enkidu's lament over the ruined forest and his fear of Enlil's anger. Sits between the journey (Tablet IV) and the heroes' return to Uruk and Ishtar's proposal (Tablet VI)

**Provenance and ethics**

> Almost certainly looted, sold by a smuggler; findspot unknown. Held legitimately by the Sulaymaniyah Museum (T.1447), which bought it under a policy of intercepting antiquities before they leave Iraq. Photographs are public (Osama S. M. Amin, World History Encyclopedia); the scholarly edition is in the Journal of Cuneiform Studies. No repatriation needed since it never left Iraq, which contrasts with the Gilgamesh Dream Tablet returned from the US in 2021

### Essay Angle

**Essay hooks**

> An $800 purchase from a smuggler added about twenty lines to the world's oldest epic (World History Encyclopedia). Monkeys in the Cedar Forest: George called the forest 'a dense jungle inhabited by exotic and noisy fauna' and its sound 'a symphony (or cacophony)' (Biblical Archaeology Society). Humbaba recast from ogre to 'a foreign ruler entertained with exotic music at court' (World History Encyclopedia). Enkidu's guilt: 'we have reduced the forest [to] a wasteland' (Biblical Archaeology Society). Contrast: a 2,500-year-old ecological lament, found with no machine help, set against AI-assisted recoveries of the 2020s

## 21. Gilgamesh Dream Tablet (repatriated to Iraq 2021)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Gilgamesh Dream Tablet: a looted cuneiform fragment of the Epic of Gilgamesh, bought by Hobby Lobby for the Museum of the Bible, seized by US authorities and repatriated to Iraq in 2021

**Date**

> Tablet written in the Middle Babylonian period, attributed to the First Sealand Dynasty (c. 1732-1460 BC); press accounts round this to '3,500 years old'. Looted from a museum in Iraq during or after the 1991 Gulf War. Bought in London by a US dealer in 2003. Sold with a false provenance letter in 2007. Sold privately through Christie's to Hobby Lobby on 14 July 2014 for $1,674,000. Seized from the Museum of the Bible by Homeland Security Investigations in September 2019. Civil forfeiture complaint filed in the Eastern District of New York on 18 May 2020. Hobby Lobby consented to forfeiture on 15 July 2021, and the court order followed that month. Handed to Iraq at a ceremony at the Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian, Washington DC, on 23 September 2021. Formally received in Baghdad on 7 December 2021 and later placed on display in the Iraq Museum (photographed there in January 2023). Sources: https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/civil-action-filed-forfeit-rare-cuneiform-tablet-hobby-lobby ; https://www.moneylaunderingnews.com/2021/08/doj-obtains-forfeiture-of-the-dream-tablet-of-gilgamesh/ ; https://global.si.edu/blog/historic-repatriation-gilgamesh-dream-tablet ; https://www.thenationalnews.com/mena/iraq/2021/12/07/ancient-gilgamesh-tablet-returned-to-iraqi-national-museum/ ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Gilgamesh_Dream_tablet._From_Iraq._Middle_Babylonian_Period,_First_Sealand_Dynasty,_1732-1460_BCE._Iraq_Museum,_Baghdad.jpg

**Creator**

> Ancient scribe unknown. Modern actors: an unnamed US antiquities dealer who bought and imported it; Christie's (London), which brokered the 2014 private sale; Hobby Lobby Stores Inc. and its president Steve Green, the buyer, for the Museum of the Bible; Homeland Security Investigations New York (Special Agent in Charge Peter C. Fitzhugh) and the US Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of New York (Richard P. ### Reception

**Critical response**

> Coverage treated it as a law-enforcement and cultural-heritage story, not a textual one. UNESCO's Audrey Azoulay called the return 'a major victory over those who mutilate heritage and then traffic it to finance violence and terrorism' (UN News, NYT, September 2021). US Attorney Richard Donoghue said 'a major auction house failed to meet its obligations' by downplaying concerns that the provenance was fabricated (ICE/DOJ, May 2020). Iraq's ambassador Fareed Yasseen said Iraqis could rediscover a heritage 'that long predates the Saddam and ISIS stains on our history' (NYT, Tom Mashberg, 23 September 2021). Legal commentators (National Law Review, Amineddoleh & Associates, Money Laundering Watch) used it as a case study in auction-house due diligence. Steve Green said he had 'trusted the wrong people to guide me'. Sources: https://news.un.org/en/story/2021/09/1100372 ; https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/civil-action-filed-forfeit-rare-cuneiform-tablet-hobby-lobby ; https://torch.si.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/The-New-York-Times-The-‘Dream-Tablet-Nears-the-End-of-a-Long-Journey-Home.pdf ; https://natlawreview.com/article/problems-provenance-gilgamesh-dream-tablet ; https://www.artandiplawfirm.com/fool-me-twice-the-gilgamesh-tablet-has-been-forfeited-from-hobby-lobby/

**Regional reception**

> The object itself went home. It was handed over at Iraq's Ministry of Foreign Affairs in Baghdad on 7 December 2021 before Foreign Minister Fuad Hussein and Culture Minister Hassan Nadhem, who said it had 'symbolic value for restitution' and warned smugglers that stolen pieces would be recovered. Hussein called it a 'victory against the desperate attempts to steal our long-standing history and civilisation'. It was the headline piece among some 17,000 artefacts returned to Iraq in 2021, the largest repatriation of Iraqi antiquities to date, and it is now on show in the Iraq Museum. ### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Cuneiform scholarship plus legal and archival investigation. When the dealer imported it in 2003 the tablet was encrusted and unreadable; after cleaning, cuneiform experts recognised the text as the Gilgamesh dream episode. The recovery itself came from provenance research: a Museum of the Bible curator asked for the provenance letter and details of earlier owners, the story fell apart, and HSI traced the chain of sales through documents and interviews. The return was a civil forfeiture under US customs law (19 U.S.C.), based on illegal import in 2003 and 2014 in breach of the ban on Iraqi archaeological material. Sources: https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/civil-action-filed-forfeit-rare-cuneiform-tablet-hobby-lobby ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/hobby-lobby-forfeits-rare-gilgamesh-tablet-smuggled-iraq-180978314/ ; https://natlawreview.com/article/problems-provenance-gilgamesh-dream-tablet

**Ai pipeline stage**

> None. Cleaning, reading and identification were done by human specialists, and the recovery was investigative and legal work.

**What was found**

> A Middle Babylonian manuscript of the Gilgamesh dream episode, the scene in Tablet I of the standard version where Gilgamesh describes his dreams to Ninsun and she interprets them as foretelling a new friend, Enkidu. HSI quotes her line 'You will see him and your heart will laugh.' It is one of only a handful of Middle Babylonian Gilgamesh texts and has distinctive divine names and an alternate place name, which the Smithsonian treats as evidence of regional variation. Its exact findspot is unknown. Sources: https://www.ice.gov/news/releases/civil-action-filed-forfeit-rare-cuneiform-tablet-hobby-lobby ; https://global.si.edu/blog/historic-repatriation-gilgamesh-dream-tablet ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_Gilgamesh_Dream_tablet._From_Iraq._Middle_Babylonian_Period,_First_Sealand_Dynasty,_1732-1460_BCE._Iraq_Museum,_Baghdad.jpg

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> No machine touches this story, which makes it a useful counterweight to the eBL and AI-reading items. Everything that mattered was human judgement about trust. A dealer wrote a false history; an auction house specialist heard that it would not survive scrutiny and sold it anyway; a museum curator asked the awkward question that unravelled it. The tablet was unreadable dirt until someone cleaned it and recognised a mother telling her son he would find a friend. A model can match fragments or read signs, but it cannot tell you whether the paperwork is a lie, and it cannot decide that a $1.67 million object should go back to Baghdad. The essay can use it to make the point that recovering a text is partly scholarship and partly ethics, and the second part is still entirely people.

**Essay hooks**

> (1) The line itself: Ninsun tells Gilgamesh of Enkidu, 'You will see him and your heart will laugh' (HSI/DOJ, May 2020). A tablet about the promise of friendship spent thirty years being passed between strangers. (2) About $50,000 in 2003, $1,674,000 in 2014 (Money Laundering Watch; NYT). (3) The dealer's own verdict on the forged provenance: 'not verifiable and would not hold up to scrutiny in a public auction' (Money Laundering Watch, summarising the complaint). ## 22. eBL Fragmentarium (LMU Munich, Enrique Jiménez)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> eBL Fragmentarium: the fragment database of the Electronic Babylonian Library (eBL) project at LMU Munich, led by Enrique Jiménez

**Date**

> Project began April 2018 with a Sofja Kovalevskaja Award from the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation. A public launch was first planned for autumn 2021; the Fragmentarium and the first digital eBL edition of Gilgamesh went public in February 2023. Main press coverage of the Gilgamesh results: LMU/phys.org (February 2023), Jiménez in The Conversation (25 May 2023), Erik Ofgang in the New York Times (12 August 2024). Follow-on work: Hymn to Babylon press release (28 July 2025) and an arXiv paper on automated sign detection across the eBL (21 June 2026). Sources: https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/ ; https://phys.org/news/2023-02-ai-texts-thousands-years-readable.html ; https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091 ; https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/12/books/booksupdate/ai-ancient-tablets-gilgamesh.html ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22608

**Creator**

> Enrique Jiménez (Professor of Ancient Near Eastern Literatures, Institute of Assyriology, Ludwig-Maximilians-Universität Munich) and the eBL team: developer J. Laasonen; contributors including L. Sáenz, E. Gogokhia, H. Stadhouders, A. C. Heinrich, Zs. Földi, T. Mitto and A. Hätinen; conservator C. Gütschow; translators Benjamin R. Foster (English) and Anmar A. Fadhil of the University of Baghdad (Arabic). Partners: the British Museum (liaison Jon Taylor), the Iraq Museum (liaison Anmar Fadhil), and the Leibniz Supercomputing Centre of the Bavarian Academy of Sciences and Humanities. Sources: https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/ ; https://www.lmu.de/en/newsroom/news-overview/news/hymn-to-babylon-readable-again-thanks-to-ai-2ae70bee.html

**Form**

> Online dataset and search platform: a database of transliterated cuneiform fragments linked to museum photographs, stored as parsed JSON in MongoDB and lemmatised against the Concise Dictionary of Akkadian, with a fragment-matching search. It sits alongside the eBL Corpus, a digital edition of Babylonian literary texts, Gilgamesh among them. Sources: https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/ ; https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Mostly human cuneiform scholarship made searchable. Team members transliterate fragments from museum photographs (above all the British Museum's Kuyunjik and Babylon collections) into the database. A string-alignment algorithm adapted for cuneiform, like the sequence alignment used in genetics, then compares each fragment's text against everything else in the Fragmentarium and the Corpus. It allows for spelling variants, since one syllable can be written up to 25 ways. Suggested matches are checked by an Assyriologist and, where possible, confirmed as physical joins or as new manuscripts of a known text. A separate model trained on fewer than 20,000 lines, without sign lists, disambiguates cuneiform readings with about 98 per cent success. Since 2025/26 the group has also built image-based sign detection (OCR) across the eBL photographs. Sources: https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091 ; https://www.freethink.com/robots-ai/cuneiform-tablets ; https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/ ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22608

**Ai pipeline stage**

> Fragment matching: the machine proposes which pieces belong to which text. Imaging is done by museum photographers and first transliteration by humans. Translation is human (Foster in English, Fadhil in Arabic). Sign recognition from photographs is newer and experimental: a 2026 paper by Che, Garcés Arias, Niaz, Bender and Jiménez ran a detection transformer over 87,668 eBL fragments and produced about 2.9 million sign detections. Sources: https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22608

**Scale metrics**

> About 13,500 fragments and 140,000 lines transliterated by February 2020, with 75% of unpublished Nineveh fragments covered. About 17,500 fragments and 215,000+ lines by February 2021. Nearly 22,000 fragments by the February 2023 launch, with over 300,000 lines reported by Freethink. Joins: about 5,000 made across 150 years of Assyriology before 2018, then 1,500+ more by the eBL team since 2018, plus several thousand further pieces placed. Gilgamesh: 20 new fragments adding to more than 100 lines (NYT, 2024), and roughly two-thirds of the text now reconstructed. Latest datable Gilgamesh manuscript: c. 130 BC. The Hymn to Babylon was rebuilt from 15 new fragments. About 200 scholars had access before the public launch. By 2026 the eBL image corpus used for sign detection held 87,668 fragments. Some 500,000 cuneiform tablets are thought to be in museum collections. Sources: https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/ ; https://phys.org/news/2023-02-ai-texts-thousands-years-readable.html ; https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091 ; https://paleojudaica.blogspot.com/2024/08/ai-is-finding-more-gilgamesh-epic.html?m=1 ; https://www.freethink.com/robots-ai/cuneiform-tablets ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.22608

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> The Fragmentarium is a good case of a machine doing the drudgery and a person doing the reading. The algorithm is tireless at one narrow job: take a string of damaged signs and find where else in 22,000 fragments that sequence, or a spelling variant of it, turns up. That is the job George Smith did by eye in the 1870s, and it is why 1,500 joins arrived in about five years after 5,000 in a century and a half. Everything on either side is still human. Someone had to transliterate each fragment from a photograph before the search could run. Someone has to check the suggested match against the clay, decide which tablet and column it belongs to, and translate it. The part that makes a find matter, seeing that 'prodigal' feeding of doomed workers means Utnapishtim feels guilty, is Foster's reading, not the algorithm's. Popular coverage says 'A.I. deciphers Gilgamesh'. The more honest line is that the machine finds the pieces faster, and the meaning still comes from someone who knows the poem.

**Essay hooks**

> (1) 5,000 joins in 150 years of Assyriology, then 1,500+ in about five years with the Fragmentarium (Jiménez, The Conversation, 2023). (2) 'Without the Fragmentarium, the reconstruction process would have taken 30, 40 years' (Jiménez, phys.org, February 2023). (3) One syllable can be written up to 25 ways in cuneiform, which is why plain text search fails. (4) The latest Gilgamesh copy dates to about 130 BC: 'people were still copying Gilgamesh at this late period' (Jiménez). (5) A single new word, 'prodigal', gives the flood hero a conscience: 'he feels guilty because he knows that all the people helping him build the ark will be drowned in a few days' (Benjamin Foster, via NYT coverage, 2024). (6) The method borrows from genetics: sequence alignment like that used on DNA, applied to clay. (7) Iraqi colleagues to Jiménez: 'You make us proud to be Babylonians' (LMU, July 2025). (8) Contrast with Smith: the same Kuyunjik drawers he sorted by hand are now being searched by an algorithm. ## 23. eBL Corpus online edition of Gilgamesh

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Poem of Gilgameš (eBL Corpus text L I.4), the online critical edition of the Epic of Gilgamesh in the electronic Babylonian Library at LMU Munich

**Date**

> The Standard Babylonian chapters (Tablets I to XII) carry a publication timestamp of 13 October 2022 in the eBL API. The eBL annual report for 2022 says the Corpus was 'greatly enlarged' that year, chiefly with Gilgamesh. The platform went public in February 2023. The edition is continuously revised: the database records bibliography merges as recent as August 2026, and the parser version differs between chapters (6.1.0 and 6.2.0), which shows chapters being re-processed after first publication. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/XI ; https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/ ; https://phys.org/news/2023-02-ai-texts-thousands-years-readable.html

**Creator**

> Edited by Andrew R. George (SOAS, author of the 2003 Oxford critical edition), with revision by Geraldina Rozzi and Enrique Jiménez of the eBL team at LMU Munich. English translation by George. Arabic translation of Tablets I to III by Anmar A. Fadhil (University of Baghdad) and a second translator recorded as Khatabe. Standard citation form: George, A. R. (2022), Poem of Gilgameš Chapter Standard Babylonian I, with contributions by E. Jiménez and G. Rozzi, electronic Babylonian Library, https://doi.org/10.5282/ebl/l/1/4. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/I ; https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091

**Form**

> Online critical edition and dataset. Each line is given as a normalised Akkadian reconstruction (marked for metrical feet and caesura), then a score: the line exactly as it survives on every manuscript that preserves it, stacked one under the other, with breaks shown in square brackets. Translation, notes, parallels to other chapters and colophons sit alongside. Every word is lemmatised and linked to the dictionary, and every manuscript to its museum number, photographs and bibliography. The whole thing is also exposed as JSON through a public API. It covers 39 chapters: Standard Babylonian I to XII, 14 Old Babylonian tablets (including the Schøyen and Penn pieces), 11 Middle Babylonian ones from Nippur, Ur, Hattusa, Emar, Megiddo and Ugarit, and two Neo-Assyrian outliers. The text record gives about 3,150 verses. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4 ; https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> The edition's biggest effect is on what counts as 'the text'. George's 2003 volumes fixed the poem at one moment. The eBL version keeps absorbing finds made since, including the Sulaymaniyah Tablet V material (al-Rawi and George 2014) and the Babylon-collection fragments Jiménez published in 2020. The site's own introduction says it 'incorporates all discoveries since then, both large and small.' The clearest change to a character sits in Tablet XI, line 74. Before the flood, Utnapishtim now says 'I lavished on my workforce like the waters of a river'. The reading rests on a new fragment, and the note openly lists rival restorations, one of them suggested by George himself. Foster reads the lavishness as guilt towards men who will drown, which makes the flood hero more troubled than the older text allowed. The edition also makes the poem's circularity easy to check: the note to XI 324 points out that the epic closes by repeating the prologue's description of Uruk's walls, and the score cross-links the two passages line by line. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4 ; https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/XI ; https://www.nytimes.com/2024/08/12/books/booksupdate/ai-ancient-tablets-gilgamesh.html

### Reception

**Regional reception**

> Arabic sits in the edition itself: Tablets I to III have line-by-line Arabic translations beside George's English, credited to Anmar A. Fadhil of the University of Baghdad and to Khatabe. That is rare for a critical edition of Gilgamesh and means the poem can be read in Arabic in the country where the tablets were written. The later tablets are English only so far. The Iraq Museum is a formal eBL partner, and the edition includes Iraq Museum manuscripts such as IM 67564 from Kalhu (Nimrud) in Tablet XI. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/I ; https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/XI ; https://iaassyriology.com/in-the-spotlight-the-electronic-babylonian-literature-project/

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Traditional cuneiform scholarship, set in a database. George's 2003 score was converted into eBL's structured ATF format, and Rozzi and Jiménez then revised it against new joins and newly identified fragments. Every manuscript line is aligned word by word to the reconstructed line, so a reader can see which tablet supplies which word. The edition is tied to the eBL Fragmentarium: its string-alignment search compares every transliterated fragment against the Corpus, and a new hit shows up as a new manuscript or join in the score (for example, the note on BM 35380 in Tablet XI records 'New joins: BM 36114 + BM 36225 (Jiménez)'). Humans still decide restorations and translate. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/XI ; https://theconversation.com/ai-is-helping-us-read-ancient-mesopotamian-literature-205091

**Ai pipeline stage**

> Fragment matching only, and upstream of the edition. The Fragmentarium's alignment search proposes new Gilgamesh pieces, and that is how the fragment of the latest datable manuscript was found. Building the score, restoring gaps and translating are all human work.

**What was found**

> The edition is the container for the finds rather than a single find. It holds George's 2003 text plus everything since: the Sulaymaniyah Tablet V lines on the Cedar Forest (2014), the Ugarit and 'civilizing of Ea-Enkidu' tablets George published in 2007, Jiménez's 2020 fragments from the British Museum's Babylon collection (source of the new XI 74 reading), later Fragmentarium joins, and uncertain fragments flagged but not yet placed (Sm 1754 and K 16024 in Tablet I). It sets the Old and Middle Babylonian tablets beside the Standard version as separate chapters instead of merging them. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4 ; https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/I

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> The eBL edition sharpens the contrast between a book and a database. George's 2003 edition is a fixed object: two volumes, one reading per line, one date on the spine. The eBL version keeps changing. Every reading carries its manuscripts beneath it, every gap stays visible in brackets, and a new fragment turned up by the matching algorithm can change a line next month. Machines do the plumbing well here. They align each tablet's signs to the reconstruction, cross-link the parallel passages and surface candidate fragments. They do not choose between restorations. At XI 74 the editors print 'I lavished', then give half a dozen alternatives in a note, including one from George himself and one from Eckart Frahm, and call the problem 'not easy'. A human judgement settles the line. It can be undone, and it is honestly labelled as provisional. A poet translating from this edition has more evidence than any before, and more uncertainty to face. The score shows how little any single tablet holds. Turning that into a line of English still takes someone who can hear the poem.

**Essay hooks**

> (1) A printed edition versus a living one: George's 2003 text is fixed on the page, while the eBL edition says it 'incorporates all discoveries since then, both large and small' (eBL introduction to L I.4). (2) Tablet XI line 74: 'I lavished on my workforce like the waters of a river', printed with a note listing rival readings from George and Frahm and calling them 'not easy' (eBL SB XI 74). (3) Tablet IX, where Gilgamesh grieves for Enkidu, survives on only 3 manuscripts; Tablet I survives on 17 (eBL API). (4) Manuscripts span about 600 years of copying, from 8th-century BC Assyria to 2nd-century BC Babylonia. (5) The poem's last lines repeat the opening description of Uruk's walls, and the edition links XI 323 to 328 directly back to I 18 to 23. (6) Arabic beside English for Tablets I to III, translated at the University of Baghdad. (7) The text is a DOI and a JSON endpoint as well as a book, openly licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Sources: https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4 ; https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/XI ; https://www.ebl.lmu.de/api/texts/L/1/4/chapters/SB/I ; https://openhumanitiesdata.metajnl.com/articles/10.5334/johd.148

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> This is not a Gilgamesh find, so it changes no character in the epic. What it changes is the picture of Babylonian literature that Gilgamesh sat inside. A 'classic' that literate Babylonians probably knew by heart, copied in schools for some six centuries and quoted in other texts, was entirely unknown until 2025. The editors rank it with Enuma elish, Ludlul and the Hymn to Shamash by number of school excerpts (eleven so far). Its content is surprising: a long description of spring arriving with the Euphrates (one of only two extended nature descriptions in Mesopotamian literature), and praise of Babylonians for protecting orphans, freeing captives and not humiliating the foreigners among them, followed by a passage on three classes of priestesses. For an essay on Gilgamesh the point is that the canon is still moving: if a text this popular could vanish so thoroughly, the gaps in Gilgamesh are not the only holes in the record. ### Reception

**Regional reception**

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Conventional cuneiform scholarship accelerated by fragment search. **Ai craft tension**

> This is the clearest case of where the machine helps and where it stops. ## 25. Gutherz et al., Akkadian-to-English neural machine translation (PNAS Nexus, 2023)

### Basic Info

**Name**

> Translating Akkadian to English with neural machine translation (PNAS Nexus 2(5), pgad096); the model is also publicised as the 'Babylonian Engine', with code released on GitHub as Akkademia

**Date:** Received 10 August 2022; accepted 8 March 2023; published 2 May 2023 (PNAS Nexus, vol. 2, issue 5)

**Creator**

> Gai Gutherz (School of Computer Science, Tel Aviv University), Shai Gordin (Digital Pasts Lab, Ariel University), Luis Sáenz (Ariel University and Heidelberg University), Omer Levy and Jonathan Berant (Tel Aviv University)

**Form**

> Peer-reviewed research article plus an open-source neural machine translation model (convolutional sequence-to-sequence network built with Fairseq) and code

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> None directly. The model recovered no new Gilgamesh text and was not tested on the epic. Its relevance is methodological: it shows how far machine translation gets with formulaic Akkadian and how badly it does with literary Akkadian, which is the register Gilgamesh is written in

### Reception

**Critical response**

> Widely covered in 2023 (Times of Israel, Decrypt, ZME Science, Language Log). Coverage stressed the headline BLEU4 score of about 37 and the claim of translation close to a novice human level. Language Log noted that Google Translate scores around 60 BLEU for Spanish to English, putting the result in perspective. Assyriologist Nathan Wasserman (Hebrew University), quoted in the Times of Israel, said that 'for deeper and less formulaic texts, this is still very far from being useful' and that having the words right 'doesn't mean you understand'. The authors themselves frame it as a tool to assist scholars and students, not a replacement

**Controversies**

> No dispute over the paper's honesty; the authors report their own weaknesses. The debate is about hype: headlines such as 'AI translates 5,000-year-old cuneiform at the push of a button' overstated a model that works best on repetitive royal inscriptions and administrative letters and produces 'total hallucinations' (fluent English unrelated to the source) on literary and unfamiliar texts. BLEU as a measure of translation quality for poetry is itself questionable, and the training data (ORACC translations) encode the choices of the human translators who made them

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Machine translation. A convolutional neural network (Fairseq CNN encoder-decoder) trained on parallel Akkadian-English sentences from ORACC projects (RINAP, RIAo, RIBo, SAAo, Suhu). Two tasks: cuneiform Unicode signs straight to English (C2E) and Latin-script transliteration to English (T2E). Compared against a translation-memory baseline; evaluated with BLEU4 plus human review by Assyriologists of the error types

**Ai pipeline stage**

> Translation (the final step, after a tablet has been read into Unicode signs or transliteration by a human or another model)

**Scale metrics**

> 8,056 texts; about 56,160 sentences for T2E and 50,299 for C2E. BLEU4: 37.47 (T2E) and 36.52 (C2E), versus translation-memory baselines of 23.51 and 27.09. Best on royal decrees and inscriptions, administrative letters and astrological/divination reports (mainly first millennium BCE Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian material); worst on legal transactions, literary texts and Old Babylonian texts. Quality drops for sentences over about 118 characters. Over half a million cuneiform tablets are thought to exist, most untranslated

**What was found**

> No text or object. The contribution is a working translation tool and a measurement of where machine translation of Akkadian succeeds (formulaic genres) and where it fails (literature, names, broken lines). The paper tests an unknown passage from the Epic of Erra, a Babylonian poem, as an example of literary difficulty

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> This is the clearest contrast with Simon Armitage's Gilgamesh. Armitage cannot read Akkadian and works from a literal crib by Assyriologist Jacob Dahl, then turns it into verse. The Gutherz model does the crib step: it turns signs into rough literal English, and does it well only where the Akkadian is repetitive and formulaic (royal boasts, reports, letters). On literary texts it hallucinates, producing confident, fluent English that has nothing to do with the tablet. That is precisely the failure a poet must never commit and a scholar exists to catch. The machine can triage a backlog of hundreds of thousands of administrative tablets; it cannot yet produce a trustworthy crib of an epic, and it has no notion of what a line should sound like, why a gap matters or which version to follow. Human judgement stays at both ends: the Assyriologist who checks the literal sense and the poet who decides what it means in English

**Essay hooks**

> (1) Gai Gutherz: 'What's so amazing about it is that I don't need to understand Akkadian at all to translate' (Times of Israel, 2023). Set this beside Armitage, who also cannot read Akkadian but relies on a human expert. (2) Nathan Wasserman: 'For deeper and less formulaic texts, this is still very far from being useful' and 'even when you have the words correct, it doesn't mean you understand' (Times of Israel). (3) BLEU score about 37 for Akkadian versus about 60 for Google's Spanish to English (Language Log). (4) Literary texts produced 'total hallucinations': fluent English unrelated to the source. A machine version of smoothing over a gap. (5) Trained on roughly 50,000 sentences, a tiny corpus by modern AI standards, against over half a million surviving tablets. (6) The model is best at kings bragging and officials reporting, the least poetic Akkadian there is. Gilgamesh is exactly where it breaks down

## 26. ProtoSnap (Cornell and Tel Aviv University, ICLR 2025)

### Basic Info

**Name:** ProtoSnap: Prototype Alignment for Cuneiform Signs

**Date**

> arXiv preprint 2502.00129 submitted 31 January 2025; Cornell Chronicle press release 4 March 2025; presented at ICLR 2025 (International Conference on Learning Representations, Singapore, April 2025)

**Creator**

> Rachel Mikulinsky and Morris Alper (co-first authors, Tel Aviv University), Shai Gordin (Ariel University), Enrique Jiménez (LMU Munich, head of the Electronic Babylonian Library), Yoram Cohen (Tel Aviv University, archaeology) and Hadar Averbuch-Elor (Cornell Tech / Cornell Bowers CIS, formerly TAU). Code released by TAU-VAILab on GitHub

**Form**

> Machine-learning method and model: an unsupervised alignment technique using features from a fine-tuned Stable Diffusion model, plus a ControlNet generator for synthetic sign images and a new expert-annotated benchmark (272 images). Code, data and trained models released

### Reading of the Arc

**Effect on arc**

> None directly. ProtoSnap has not recovered any Gilgamesh text, and the paper does not mention the epic. Its effect is upstream: better sign recognition on photographed tablets could speed the reading of unpublished fragments, some of which may belong to the epic, as the eBL Fragmentarium (whose data it uses) has already shown for Gilgamesh

### Reception

**Controversies**

> No notable controversy. The authors state their own limits: the method 'will still fail if the scan displays a structurally different variant' of a sign, and struggles with extreme deformation or low-quality tablets. Wider debate about AI in Assyriology (hype versus the scarce pool of human experts) applies but is not specific to this paper

### Recovery Method

**Method**

> Sign recognition via prototype alignment. Given a photo of a single cuneiform sign and a 'prototype' (a clean font rendering with an annotated stroke skeleton, from the Santakku and SantakkuM fonts by Sylvie Vanséveren), a fine-tuned Stable Diffusion model supplies per-pixel features. The system builds a 4D similarity volume, finds 'best-buddy' mutual nearest-neighbour matches, fits a global affine transform, then refines each stroke separately by optimisation so the template 'snaps' onto the wedges actually impressed in the clay. The aligned skeletons then condition a ControlNet that generates realistic, structurally correct synthetic sign images to train a sign classifier

**Ai pipeline stage**

> Sign recognition (the reading step): locating the structure of individual signs in tablet photographs and generating synthetic training data for OCR. It does not do imaging, fragment joining or translation

**Scale metrics**

> Diffusion model fine-tuned on 34,868 sign images covering 362 sign types from the eBL dataset (about 40% Neo-Babylonian, about 20% Neo-Assyrian, plus Ur III, Old Babylonian, Middle Babylonian and other periods; Akkadian and Sumerian). Test benchmark: 272 expert-annotated images of 25 sign types with 2 to 8 strokes each. Adding ControlNet synthetic data lifted classifier accuracy from 58.43% to 64.14%, balanced accuracy from 34.57% to 43.57%, and accuracy on rare signs (under 50 examples) from 25.84% to 53.17%. Context: an estimated 500,000 cuneiform tablets in museums and over 1,000 distinct signs

**What was found**

> No text was found. The output is a tool: a way to map the stroke structure of a sign on a real tablet photo, a released benchmark, and a generator of synthetic training images that roughly doubles recognition accuracy for rare signs

### Essay Angle

**Ai craft tension**

> ProtoSnap is a clear case of the machine sitting at the reading step and still needing humans on both sides of it. Before: the prototypes are human-made fonts and the 272 benchmark signs were annotated by expert paleographers. After: the model only says 'this is where the strokes of sign X are'; deciding which sign a damaged wedge cluster is, what word it spells and how the line reads is still the Assyriologist's job. The authors admit it fails when a scribe used a structurally different variant, which is exactly where human paleographic judgement matters most. Its real contribution is scarcity relief: in Averbuch-Elor's words, there are endless 2D scans but very little labelled data, and synthetic signs let a small expert labour pool go further. For Gilgamesh, that means faster first-pass reading of the unpublished fragments where missing lines might hide, not a machine that reads the epic

**Essay hooks**

> (1) Averbuch-Elor: 'There's an endless amount of 2D scans of these cuneiforms, but the amount of labeled data is very scarce' (Cornell Chronicle, 4 March 2025). (2) Yoram Cohen: the aim is 'to increase the ancient sources available to us by tenfold' (Cornell Chronicle). (3) Rare-sign accuracy jumped from 25.84% to 53.17% with synthetic training data (ICLR 2025 paper). (4) An image model built to make pictures (Stable Diffusion) repurposed to read 3,000-year-old wedges. (5) The template comes from a modern font (Santakku), so the machine learns the ancient hand by comparing it to a typeface. (6) Its own admitted failure case, a scribe's variant form of a sign, is the thing a trained human reader handles best. (7) About 500,000 tablets in museums, only a fraction published (Cornell Chronicle). Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.00129 ; https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/03/ai-models-make-precise-copies-cuneiform-characters ; https://tau-vailab.github.io/ProtoSnap/
