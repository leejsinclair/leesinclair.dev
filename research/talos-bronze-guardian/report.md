## Apollonius Rhodius, Argonautica 4

### Source

- **Text and author:** Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica, Book 4, Hellenistic epic, 3rd century BCE.
- **Passage reference:** Arg. 4.1638-1688 (Talos episode, on the Argonauts' approach to Crete).

### The figure

- **Maker or origin:** 
> Apollonius does not say Hephaestus made him. Talos belongs to the bronze race sprung from ash trees, a survivor of the demigods, and Zeus gave him to Europa as a guard for Crete. Other sources (Apollodorus, Simonides via scholia) have Hephaestus as maker or give other parentage.
- **What it does:** 
> Guards Crete, hurling rocks to keep ships from landing and circling the island. He drives the Argonauts off the coast.
- **Failure mode:** 
> A single point of failure: one vein under the skin, sealed by a bronze nail at the ankle. Medea works on him at a distance with spells and the evil eye, invoking the Keres. He grazes his ankle on a rock and the ichor runs out. This maps loosely to AI failures such as adversarial manipulation and hidden single points of failure, but that is a modern comparison.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Killing method varies: here Medea's enchantment makes him hurt himself on a rock, elsewhere she pulls the nail with a promise of immortality or drives him mad with drugs (Apollodorus), and Poeas shoots his ankle in another version. Maker varies too.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** 
> Nobody deliberates in the scene. Zeus's gift sets the guard; Medea decides to kill him, and Jason and the Argonauts need landing. Enforcement is Talos, and the decision to remove him comes from a sorceress.
- **Origin of intelligence:** 
> None stated for Talos. He is a bronze man with life-fluid, but the poem gives no source of mind, in contrast to the handmaidens with noos in the Iliad.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> Complicates. Talos with no stated maker in this text weakens 'Hephaestus had the forge but not the mind'. It supports only the looser point that a tireless bronze guard has no visible judgement and is undone by cunning, from a woman known for craft of a different kind (Medea's drugs).
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> Best case against: Apollonius never says Talos lacks a mind, and he is not made by Hephaestus here, so he cannot illustrate the difference between making and metis. He may even be cunning enough to be worth deceiving. The essay should answer by using Apollodorus and the Minos for the maker link and law-carrying role, and by admitting that Apollonius gives the failure, not the missing judgement.
- **Claim status:** Ancient fact for the plot details; scholarly or modern interpretation for anything about mind or AI.

### Usable material

- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> One vein, sealed by a nail, was the whole of his defence. The most feared guard in the Aegean fell when a sorceress talked to him and he scraped his ankle.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> Apollonius does not call Talos made or mindless. Do not say he was built by Hephaestus on Apollonius's authority, and do not present 'robot' as the poet's word.

## Apollodorus, Bibliotheca

### Source

- **Text and author:** Apollodorus (pseudo-Apollodorus), Bibliotheca, mythographic handbook, probably 1st-2nd century CE.

### The figure

- **What it does:** Guards Crete for Minos, circling the island three times a year, and keeps ships off.
- **Failure mode:** 
> A single vein closed by a nail, and Medea beats him by deception: she promises immortality and pulls the nail, or drives him mad with drugs. The Poeas variant has an arrow to the ankle. Maps to social-engineering and single-point-of-failure risks only by modern analogy.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Apollodorus himself lists competing versions: Medea by madness, Medea by promising immortality and removing the nail, and Poeas shooting the ankle. Also bronze man versus bull, and maker versus gift.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** 
> Minos rules Crete and Talos guards it. The killing decision is Medea's (or Poeas's in the other version).

### Reception

- **Reception accuracy:** 
> Partial. Modern retellings usually flatten Apollodorus's variants into one made-by-Hephaestus robot with one death; the source itself gives several.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> Supports, moderately: Hephaestus gets the making and no story credits him with the mind. But Apollodorus offers this only as one variant beside others.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> Best case against: the maker link is one variant among several, and a bull or bronze-race Talos would drop the making versus judgement contrast. The essay should say 'in one tradition' and not treat it as canonical.
- **Claim status:** 
> Ancient fact (a late handbook reporting variants); reading it as a claim about mind is scholarly or modern interpretation.

### Usable material

- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> Apollodorus records at least three ways Talos died, and one of them is an arrow in the ankle from Poeas. There was never one canonical Talos.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> It is a late compilation. Do not treat its variants as an early or authoritative tradition, and do not attribute Zeus and Europa to it.

## Pseudo-Plato, Minos

### Source

- **Text and author:** 
> Pseudo-Plato, Minos, short dialogue on law, attributed to Plato but generally regarded as spurious; probably 4th century BCE.

### The figure

- **What it does:** Patrols Crete and carries Minos's laws to the villages, in a rationalised version of the myth.
- **What it lacks:** 
> He carries the law but does not make or judge it. The dialogue keeps decision with Minos, the lawgiver who studies with Zeus. Lack of judgement is a reading of the passage.
- **Failure mode:** The passage is not about failure. There is no vulnerability story here.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> This is a rationalising version, where Talos is a law-carrier rather than a bronze automaton. It differs from the poets' killing story.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** Minos, who is presented as a good judge and lawgiver taught by Zeus. Talos only delivers.
- **Origin of intelligence:** Not stated. Wisdom sits with Minos and Zeus.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> Supports strongly, but as a rationalised tradition: the enforcer carries law and the judge is someone else. Best evidence for enforcement versus deliberation.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> Best case against: the dialogue is pseudo-Plato, is rationalising, and may not treat Talos as a machine at all. The essay should present it as a Greek reading of the figure as enforcer and not as evidence for a mechanical Talos.
- **Claim status:** Ancient fact for what the text says; authorship and dating are scholarly.

### Usable material

- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> In one Greek account, Talos is a law-carrier who circles Crete, and the man who wrote the law is Minos.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> The Minos is probably not by Plato, and the passage is a rationalising reading. Do not cite it as Plato.

## Cinaethon (via Pausanias)

### The figure

- **What it does:** Nothing in the story. He is a genealogical link.
- **What it lacks:** Not applicable; no traits are given.
- **Failure mode:** Not applicable.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Shows that Talos's parentage and relation to Hephaestus varied widely: he is son of Cres here, maker-gifted by Hephaestus elsewhere, and a bronze-race survivor in Apollonius.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** Not applicable.
- **Origin of intelligence:** Not applicable.

### Reception

- **Reception accuracy:** Not applicable. Danger is conflating this Talos with the bronze guard.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> Complicates. If Talos is Hephaestus's father, the neat 'Hephaestus made Talos' story does not hold in every tradition. It says nothing about metis.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> Best case against: it undercuts any single canonical Talos, and the essay's 'Hephaestus made him' line would look overconfident. Answer it by saying 'in most accounts' and keeping the variance visible. Also, it may be a different Talos, so it should be a footnote rather than an argument.
- **Claim status:** 
> Ancient fact (as reported by Pausanias) for the genealogy; identity with the bronze Talos is scholarly interpretation.

### Usable material

- **Essay paragraph fact:** In one genealogy Talos is the father of Hephaestus, not his product.

## Talos on coins and vases

### Source

- **Text and author:** 
> Silver staters of Phaistos, Crete (Talos type, roughly late 4th to 3rd century BC) and the Attic red-figure volute krater from Ruvo di Puglia attributed to the Medea Painter (c. 400 BC), Museo Nazionale Jatta, Ruvo. [uncertain: coin date range]
- **Passage reference:** 
> Not a text. Krater: Jatta collection, Ruvo di Puglia (often cited as J 1501 [uncertain: inventory number]). Coins: Phaistos Talos types catalogued in Svoronos, Numismatique de la Crète ancienne (1890) and later BMC Crete.
- **Greek term and translation:** 
> Vase inscriptions name figures in Greek capitals (Talos, Medea, Kastor and Polydeukes/Polydeuces). The word for 'bronze man' comes from literary sources (chalkeos), not the images. 'Robot' is a modern gloss; the images show a muscular man, not a machine. Coin legend is the city name (Phaistion). [uncertain: exact inscriptions]

### The figure

- **Maker or origin:** 
> Images do not state a maker. Literary sources split: Hephaestus's gift to Minos (Apollodorus 1.9.26), last of the bronze race of ash-born men (Apollonius 4.1641-44), or son of Kres/Crete (Apollodorus, some accounts).
- **What it does:** 
> On coins he is shown throwing or holding a stone, guarding the island, winged and nude in some types, sometimes with a bull. On the krater he is the victim, collapsing.
- **What it lacks:** 
> The images give him a full human body, wings on some coins and no visible machinery. The vase shows no mechanism, only a figure losing strength and being held up by the Dioscuri. No image tells us about his mind.
- **Failure mode:** 
> The krater shows the moment of failure as bodily collapse under Medea's influence, with her holding a herb jar and the Argonauts catching him. This is the point the essay wants: he goes down to an outside act that works on him, not a fault inside him. Some scholars read the ankle as the weak point (the bronze nail). [uncertain: whether the vase shows the ankle wound]
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Coins show a winged Talos with a stone; the krater shows a wingless, powerful figure in a swoon. The images therefore do not agree with each other, and neither shows the 'single vein' of Apollonius directly.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** 
> Not stated by the images. Minos is the authority Talos serves; Medea is the agent of his end. The scene is enforcement being undone, with no judge visible.

### Reception

- **How it is used:** 
> Adrienne Mayor (Gods and Robots, 2018) uses the Ruvo krater as evidence that Talos was pictured and thought of as an artificial being. Her reading puts the ancient images at the centre of a 'ancient robotics' argument.
- **Reception accuracy:** 
> partial. The images are real and can be checked. Calling the figure a robot is a modern gloss, and the vase does not show an artificial being any more than a bronze man of myth.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> complicates. Images show Talos as guardian and victim with no visible judgement, which fits 'making without metis', but they say nothing directly about Athena or metis.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> The images make Talos look human and pitiable, which cuts against 'tireless machine'. A sceptic can say the Greeks pictured a tragic person, not a device, so the essay's machine framing is imposed. The essay should answer by using images only for what they show (Talos guarding, Talos falling), and take the 'lacks judgement' claim from the texts, not the pictures.
- **Claim status:** 
> Images and coins are ancient fact. Reading the krater as a 'machine dying' is scholarly interpretation, and 'robot' is a modern gloss.

### Usable material

- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> The Talos krater at Ruvo (c. 400 BC) shows the bronze guardian sinking as Medea works her herbs while the Dioscuri support him. The Greeks drew his death as something done to him from outside, in front of witnesses, not as a breakdown from within.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> Coin and vase iconography is not consistent, and the 'robot' reading is modern. Details such as wings, the ankle and the inscriptions should be checked against the museum record before quoting. Dates are approximate.

## Homer, Iliad 18.417-421

### Source

- **Text and author:** Homer, Iliad, Book 18 (Hephaestus's workshop scene), c. 8th century BC.
- **Passage reference:** 
> Il. 18.417-421 (golden attendants); self-moving tripods at 18.373-379, especially 18.376 automatoi; also the gates of Olympus 5.749-750.
- **Greek term and translation:** 
> noos (mind) in ll. 419-ish: 'en men noos esti meta phresin, en de kai audē kai sthenos', and 'from the immortal gods they know works'. Handmaidens are amphipoloi chruseiai, 'golden attendants', likened to living young women. Tripods move automatai. Translators: Lattimore ('intelligence in their hearts'), Fagles, Murray/Wyatt. 'Robot' and 'AI' are modern glosses. [uncertain: exact line numbering within 417-421 and exact wording of translators]

### The figure

- **Maker or origin:** Hephaestus makes them. No dispute in Homer. Later writers do not contradict this.
- **What it does:** 
> They support and serve the lame god, walking and working beside him. The tripods roll into the assembly of the gods and back on their own.
- **What it lacks:** 
> Nothing is stated as lacking. They are given noos, voice, strength and skill learned from the gods. That is the strongest ancient counter to 'machines had no mind'.
- **Failure mode:** 
> No failure in the passage. It is a picture of machinery working well. Failure modes belong to Talos and Pandora, not here.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Not a Talos passage. Later writers (Apollonius, Apollodorus) do not carry the handmaidens over, so the Iliad shows one strand of the tradition, not the norm.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** 
> Hephaestus is the master and Thetis is the visitor. The handmaidens do not deliberate at all in the scene shown, though they are given noos.
- **Origin of intelligence:** 
> Given. The poet states directly that mind (noos) is in them, and that the gods taught them skills. So made things can have a mind, and it comes from the gods.

### Reception

- **How it is used:** 
> Mayor (Gods and Robots) treats the passage as Homer imagining artificial intelligence and 'made minds'. Popular AI pieces cite it as the first robot passage.
- **Reception accuracy:** 
> partial. The text does say noos, so the claim of a mind is supported. Calling it a robot or AI goes beyond the poem, which gives no mechanism and says the mind comes from the gods.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> complicates, and in one reading contradicts. If the handmaidens have noos, then making and mind were not separate gifts in Homer.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> The best case against the angle: Hephaestus's own products think. The essay should answer by distinguishing noos (perception and understanding) from metis (cunning, judgement under uncertainty), and note that the handmaidens serve and do not decide. Also note that Homer's handmaidens are a separate strand, and Talos has no stated noos. Say this openly instead of hiding the counterexample.
- **Claim status:** 
> Ancient fact for the text. The contrast noos versus metis is scholarly interpretation. 'AI' or 'robot' is modern gloss.

### Usable material

- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> In Iliad 18 Homer gives Hephaestus golden attendants with noos, voice, strength and skills taught by the gods, and self-moving tripods that roll into the assembly by themselves. The Greeks could imagine made things that thought, and this passage is the honest counterweight to the Talos angle.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> Noos here is not the same as metis, and Homer does not say what the attendants can decide. Do not present the passage as the Greek word for AI. Quotes must be checked against a named translation.

## Athena: metis and techne

### Source

- **Text and author:** 
> Detienne and Vernant, Cunning Intelligence in Greek Culture and Society (1974, English 1978); Hesiod, Theogony (c. 700 BC); Homeric Hymns to Athena and Hephaestus; Plato, Protagoras 321d.
- **Passage reference:** 
> Hes. Theog. 886-900, 924-926; Hom. Hymn 20 (to Hephaestus); Plato Prot. 321d (Prometheus stole technical wisdom from Athena and Hephaestus); Il. 15.410-413 (carpenter guided by Athena); Od. 13.291-299 (Athena on metis). [uncertain: some line numbers]
- **Greek term and translation:** 
> metis (cunning intelligence, practical judgement), techne (craft, skill), sophia (skill, wisdom), Ergane (Athena as worker). Translators: Detienne and Vernant (Lloyd), Race, Evelyn-White, Lattimore. 'Judgement' is a modern gloss for metis; 'AI' is not in the Greek.

### The figure

- **Maker or origin:** 
> Athena is daughter of Zeus and Metis, born from Zeus's head. Sources place the origin differently (Hesiod Theogony, Homeric Hymn 28, Pindar Olympian 7).
- **What it does:** 
> Goddess of war strategy, crafts and counsel. She teaches skills such as weaving and helps craftsmen, helps heroes (Odysseus, Heracles) and helps build the Argo.
- **What it lacks:** 
> Not applicable as a figure without a mind. She is the counter-case: a goddess who has both judgement and craft. She does not make the great metal works herself. Those are Hephaestus's.
- **Failure mode:** 
> The overlap itself is the problem for the angle. Metis includes skilful making, so a clean division of gifts is hard to defend. Divine craft is not failure-prone in the sources.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Athena's role varies. Hesiod stresses counsel and strength. The Homeric Hymns stress craft. Later writers (Plato) join Athena and Hephaestus as joint patrons of technical skill.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** 
> Athena, and behind her Zeus. In the Pandora story Athena dresses the made woman but does not give her mind. Hermes does.
- **Origin of intelligence:** 
> For Athena it is inherited from Metis, through Zeus. For made things it is contested: Homer gives some noos, Talos none stated.

### Reception

- **How it is used:** 
> Detienne and Vernant argue that metis is a wide type of intelligence covering craft and cunning, which includes techne. Popular AI essays rarely use metis at all.
- **Reception accuracy:** 
> partial. Detienne and Vernant read the sources carefully, but their thesis (techne as a form of metis) is one scholarly reading, not universally held.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> complicates. 'Making and metis are different gifts' holds for Hephaestus versus Athena at the level of patronage, but the sources let Athena share craft, and Detienne and Vernant fold techne into metis.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> Strongest counter: Athena is herself a craft goddess, so the Greeks did not separate judgement and craft cleanly. The essay should answer by saying the gifts are distinct but overlapping, and by pointing at what is separated in the myths: Hephaestus makes bodies, Athena brings the judgement to them, as in Pandora and the Argo.
- **Claim status:** 
> Athena's dual role is ancient fact. Treating techne as a kind of metis is scholarly interpretation. 'Judgement' for metis is modern gloss.

### Usable material

- **Quotable line:** Metis as 'cunning intelligence' (Detienne and Vernant, trans. Lloyd). [uncertain: exact page]
- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> Athena is the child of Metis, and she is also the patron of weaving, carpentry and shipbuilding. In Plato's Protagoras, technical skill is what Prometheus steals from Athena and Hephaestus together, so the Greeks tied craft and cunning closely, while giving different tasks to each god.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> The overlap is real. Do not claim the Greeks kept craft and judgement fully apart. Say the myths give them different patrons and different stories.

## Zeus swallows Metis (Hesiod, Theogony 886-900)

### Source

- **Text and author:** Hesiod, Theogony (c. 700 BC), and Works and Days (c. 700 BC).
- **Passage reference:** 
> Theog. 886-900 (Zeus swallows Metis), 924-926 (Athena born from Zeus's head); Works and Days 60-82 (Pandora made). Compare Theog. 570-589.
- **Greek term and translation:** 
> metis (886, 'wisest of gods and mortal men'), dolos (deception, 889 [uncertain: line]), boule (counsel), 'good and evil' (900, to agathon te kakon te), noos and epiklopon ethos in WD 67, 77-78 ('a dog's mind and thievish nature'). Translators: Most (Loeb), West, Evelyn-White, Lattimore. 'Judgement internalised' is a modern gloss.

### The figure

- **Maker or origin:** 
> Athena comes from Zeus's head after he swallows Metis. Pandora is made by Hephaestus from earth and water, dressed by Athena, given gifts by Aphrodite and Hermes. Sources vary on Athena's birth (Homeric Hymn 28, Pindar).
- **What it does:** 
> Zeus takes in Metis so that judgement is his and no rival can rule. Athena then acts as his counsel and strength. Pandora is a 'beautiful evil' sent to humans.
- **What it lacks:** 
> Pandora lacks nothing structural: Hermes gives her a bitch's mind and a deceitful nature. Athena gives skill and dress, not judgement.
- **Failure mode:** 
> Pandora's jar is opened, releasing evils. It is a human act and a divine trap, not a machine fault, and it fits less well than Talos to modern AI failure modes.
- **Tradition variance:** 
> Athena's birth: Hesiod puts Metis inside Zeus first, with Athena born after. Homer and the Hymns do not tell this. Pandora in Theogony is unnamed, named in Works and Days.

### Judgement

- **Who is the judge:** 
> Zeus, having swallowed Metis, so that 'the goddess might devise for him good and evil'. Athena carries out that judgement. In Pandora Zeus commands, Hephaestus makes, Athena dresses, Hermes gives the mind.
- **Origin of intelligence:** 
> Judgement is taken in by the ruler and then embodied in his daughter. For Pandora the mind is given by Hermes, not by Hephaestus or Athena.

### Reception

- **How it is used:** 
> Popular AI essays use Pandora as a warning that a powerful gift opens harm. Some use Prometheus. Few use Metis.
- **Reception accuracy:** partial. Pandora as a made being is accurate. Reading her as an early AI is a modern stretch.

### Angle fit

- **Angle fit:** 
> supports. Judgement is kept by the sovereign and embodied in Athena, not passed to the maker. The Pandora contrast shows Hephaestus making and Athena dressing, with the mind supplied elsewhere.
- **Counter argument strength:** 
> Pandora does get a mind from Hermes, so the maker did not have to supply judgement. That sits close to 'made things get minds too'. The essay should say Pandora is a contrast case and not a metis-carrier: her 'dog's mind' is cunning without good judgement, which is the point.
- **Claim status:** 
> Ancient fact for the text. Reading Metis swallowing as 'judgement internalised by the ruler' is scholarly interpretation. Anything about AI is modern gloss.

### Usable material

- **Quotable line:** 
> 'That the goddess might devise for him good and evil.' (Theog. 900, paraphrase in the style of Evelyn-White) [uncertain: exact wording]
- **Essay paragraph fact:** 
> Hesiod says Zeus swallowed his first wife Metis, the wisest of all, so that she would counsel him from inside. Athena was then born from his head. The Greeks let the ruler keep judgement rather than hand it to a maker.
- **Honest caveat:** 
> Hesiod's motive is succession (avoiding a son who would overthrow Zeus), not a theory of judgement. The judgement reading is an interpretation. Pandora is a contrast case only.

## Medea's spell as death by perception

### Source

- **Text and author:** 
> Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica, Book 4 (3rd century BC); Pseudo-Apollodorus, Bibliotheca (1st-2nd century AD); Ruvo krater (c. 400 BC).
- **Passage reference:** 
> Ap. Rhod. Arg. 4.1638-1688 (Talos episode, spell 4.1665-1672 [uncertain: exact lines], ankle wound 1679-1680, fall 1685-1688); Apollodorus, Bibl. 1.9.26; Plato, Minos 320c (Talos and the laws).

### The figure

- **Maker or origin:** 
> Apollonius: last of the bronze race, born from ash trees. Apollodorus: Hephaestus's gift to Minos, or son of Kres. Sources disagree.
- **What it does:** 
> Guards Crete, circles the island (three times a year in Plato's Minos, carrying the laws) and throws rocks at ships.
- **What it lacks:** 
> No mind or choice is stated. He is unable to see through the trick, which is the point: he is fooled through his senses.
- **Failure mode:** 
> Deception and vulnerability, not a hardware fault. Medea bewitches his sight or mind and he wounds his ankle on a rock, so the ichor drains. In Apollodorus one version has Medea drive him mad with drugs; another has her promise immortality and pull the nail. This matches AI failures where the system is fooled by crafted input and has a single point of failure.
- **Tradition variance:**

### Reception

- **How it is used:**

- **Honest caveat:**
