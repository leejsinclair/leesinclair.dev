## Rieseberg Hunger beats tenure

### Source

- **Citation:** 
> Felix Rieseberg, 'Hunger beats tenure: Advice for junior engineers', felixrieseberg.com (personal blog, tagged 'management'), 27 October 2026. https://felixrieseberg.com/hunger-beats-tenure-advice-for-junior-engineers/ . Secondary: Gergely Orosz, 'The Pulse: Anthropic kills the coding interview', The Pragmatic Engineer newsletter, 30 October 2026 (paid issue). https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pulse-anthropic-kills-the-coding
- **Evidence tier:** 
> Practitioner post (first-person account by an Anthropic hiring manager on his personal blog; not an official Anthropic policy statement). The Pragmatic Engineer item is secondary press.
- **Access status:** 
> Rieseberg post: fully read (full HTML text retrieved 21 November 2026). Pragmatic Engineer: only the free portion read (title, subtitle, summary bullet and the lead-in sentence); the section quoting and analysing Rieseberg is paywalled ('This post is for paid subscribers').
- **Sponsor and incentive:** 
> Self-published, no sponsor. Author is an engineering manager at Anthropic, which sells Claude and Claude Code; the post credits Claude with making coding tests pointless ('Claude is now better at both') and promotes Claude Code Desktop as 'a very popular product', so he has an employer interest in the AI-capability framing. Pragmatic Engineer is a paid newsletter; a dramatic headline drives subscriptions.
- **Exact quotes:** 
- Opening paragraph: 'This month, I laid the traditional coding interview to rest. As a hiring manager at Anthropic, I stopped asking candidates to do them. It had a good run: Being able to quickly define problems and find elegant solutions was a weak but useful predictor for how successful you'd be on my teams. Small coding challenges in real code did tell us something meaningful about candidates.'
- Second paragraph: 'That's no longer true. There's no more point in giving you a little React challenge than there is in quizzing people about assembly; Claude is now better at both. Our most impactful builders are successful regardless of how good they are at these tests.'
- Third paragraph: 'The coding interview was, despite its ubiquity, never popular.' ... 'Now that I've seen junior people work with AI for a bit, I have a more hopeful take: Impact is less bounded by how many years of accumulated knowledge in frameworks and libraries you've gathered.'
- Fourth paragraph: 'I'm seeing engineers who are comparatively new fly past people who are probably better coders and have been around much longer. Hunger beats tenure.'
- Fifth paragraph: 'This post is my best advice for junior engineers, though "junior" is the wrong word. It's really for engineers without social capital, status, or power.'
- 'All vision, no voice' section: 'Well, good news: Execution isn't expensive anymore.'
- 'What to do' (1): 'Pick business impact over backlog' (citing Patrick McKenzie, 2011: 'engineers are hired to create business value, not to program things.')
- 'What to do' (2): 'Pick a radical idea: Come up with a few ideas that would, if successful, dramatically improve the chances that your organization achieves those goals. Be ambitious!'
- 'What to do' (3): 'Build it over a weekend: I have seen small groups and solo engineers fly past the usual process of writing product requirements documents (PRDs), getting buy-in, and lobbying for time with designers or product managers by building a working prototype.'
- 'What to do' (4): 'Focus on one good idea' ... 'The point of creating a working prototype is to communicate an idea so well that leadership quickly understands both the opportunity and that you're the one who can run with it.'
- 'What to do' (5): 'Lean into your fresh perspective' ... 'you have a unique amount of empathy for users'.
- 'The future is bright' section: 'I don't think the coding interview is going to come back.'
- Final paragraph: 'I'm probably not the only manager trying to figure out how to interview for people who build well with AI. I haven't quite gotten there yet — but I'm scanning resumes and portfolios for little prototypes that exhibit taste, empathy, and principled thinking. If you have figured out something better, I'd love to hear how.'
- Author bio at foot of post: 'I'm an EM at Anthropic, where I've so far led engineering for Claude.ai, Cowork, and Claude Code Desktop.'
- Pragmatic Engineer title: 'The Pulse: Anthropic kills the coding interview'. Subtitle: 'AI is rendering the Leetcode-style interview pointless and now Anthropic has deleted it from its interview loop.'
- Pragmatic Engineer summary bullet: 'Anthropic kills its coding interview. A team at the leading frontier AI company gets zero signal from DSA interviews and so has removed it from the engineering recruitment process. The rest of the company, and other AI-native startups, could follow.'
- Pragmatic Engineer section lead-in: 'A hiring manager at Anthropic who has led Claude Cowork and Claude Desktop, revealed that they have stopped asking coding questions to candidates for software engineering jobs. From Felix Rieseberg's blog post (emphasis mine):' [remainder paywalled]
- **Date and recency:** 
> Post dated 27 October 2026; change made 'this month' (October 2026). Capability claim ('Claude is now better at both') refers to Claude as of October 2026, no model version named. Pragmatic Engineer summary dated 30 October 2026.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- He personally stopped giving traditional coding interviews in October 2026, as a hiring manager at Anthropic, for his own hiring.
- Historically, the ability to 'quickly define problems and find elegant solutions' was 'a weak but useful predictor' of success on his teams, and small coding challenges 'in real code' told them something meaningful. Note the tense: he puts 'weak but useful' in the past and says 'That's no longer true.'
- Claude now outperforms candidates on such tests (a 'little React challenge'), and the most impactful builders succeed regardless of how they score on them.
- Impact is less bounded by years of accumulated framework and library knowledge; comparatively new engineers outpace better coders with longer tenure ('Hunger beats tenure').
- Execution is no longer expensive, so engineers without status can win buy-in with a working prototype instead of PRDs and lobbying.
- Main advice (five steps): pick business impact over backlog; pick a radical idea; build it over a weekend; focus on one good idea; lean into your fresh perspective.
- He has not found a replacement interview format ('I haven't quite gotten there yet'); meanwhile he scans resumes and portfolios for little prototypes showing 'taste, empathy, and principled thinking', and invites better ideas.
- He does not expect the coding interview to return.
- **Headline figures:** 
- No quantitative data. The only numbers are anecdotal: a Claude Code desktop prototype built by two relatively new engineers 'over one weekend', with 'The first public version shipped roughly eight days later'; Patrick McKenzie quote dated 2011; James March quote dated 1991.
- 'Weak but useful predictor' is a qualitative judgement, with no correlation coefficient or hiring-outcome data behind it.
- **Sample and method:** 
> Personal experience and anecdote from one hiring manager's teams at Anthropic (plus earlier stints at Stripe, Notion, Slack). No systematic data, sample size, or outcome study. The subjects are software engineers.

### Interviewing

- **What is measured:** 
> Old format: short coding challenges 'in real code' (e.g. a 'little React challenge'), measuring the ability to 'quickly define problems and find elegant solutions', which is convergent coding. Interim replacement (not an interview format): resume and portfolio screening for small prototypes that show 'taste, empathy, and principled thinking', in effect product judgement, user empathy and design reasoning, plus initiative ('hunger').
- **Ai policy in interview:** 
> Not stated as a policy. He removed the coding test instead of banning or permitting AI in it, reasoning that Claude outperforms candidates on it. No AI-use rule is described for the rest of his process.
- **Scope and status:** 
> Applies to his own hiring ('I stopped asking candidates to do them'; predictor for 'my teams'). He leads engineering for Claude.ai, Cowork and Claude Code Desktop. Nothing in the post says Anthropic dropped coding interviews company-wide. Status: in place since October 2026, no replacement format yet.
- **Validity evidence:** 
> None. 'Weak but useful predictor' is his qualitative recollection, with no criterion-validity data. No evidence is offered for the portfolio-screening approach either.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports 'stop testing convergent coding': a frontier-lab hiring manager dropped short coding tests because an AI model beats candidates on them. Partly supports the design-judgement angle ('taste, empathy, and principled thinking', 'principled thinking'). Complicates the essay's case: he has no replacement interview, he does not describe testing how candidates work with an agent, communication or team fit, and he admits the old test was a 'weak but useful' predictor, which concedes it had some value. Useful as a candid admission that the problem is unsolved.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> 'I haven't quite gotten there yet' (no replacement found); 'I'm probably not the only manager trying to figure out how to interview for people who build well with AI'; 'If you have figured out something better, I'd love to hear how.' He also concedes coding interviews once 'did tell us something meaningful about candidates.'
- **Counter evidence:** 
> His own words cut against the strongest version of the claim: the old interview was a predictor (weak, but useful), not useless. The secondary coverage inflates it. Pragmatic Engineer's headline 'Anthropic kills the coding interview' and subtitle 'Anthropic has deleted it from its interview loop' present a company-wide change, though its own summary says 'A team'. 'Zero signal from DSA interviews' and 'Leetcode-style' do not match Rieseberg, who described small real-code challenges (a React task, not data-structures-and-algorithms puzzles) as a weak but useful predictor in the past. Portfolio screening for side prototypes may favour candidates with spare time, a known equity concern with portfolio-based hiring (not raised in the post). The evidence is anecdotal and comes from someone at a company that sells the AI tool involved.

## Anthropic AI-resistant technical evaluations

### Source

- **Citation:** 
> Primary: Tristan Hume (a lead on Anthropic's performance optimization team), 'Designing AI-resistant technical evaluations', Anthropic Engineering blog, published 21 January 2026, https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/AI-resistant-technical-evaluations. Secondary: Russell Brandom, 'Anthropic has to keep revising its technical interview test as Claude improves' (URL slug: '...so you can't cheat on it with Claude'), TechCrunch, 22 January 2026, https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/22/anthropic-has-to-keep-revising-its-technical-interview-test-so-you-cant-cheat-on-it-with-claude
- **Evidence tier:** 
> Primary company statement (Anthropic engineering blog, first-person practitioner account by the test's designer). TechCrunch is secondary press summarising that post.
- **Access status:** 
> Anthropic post fully read (full page text retrieved and quotes checked by text search). TechCrunch article read via a fetch summary; its quotes are reproductions of the Anthropic post, plus its own correction note.
- **Sponsor and incentive:** 
> Anthropic sells Claude and Claude Code; a post saying its model matches top human candidates also showcases the product's capability. The post doubles as recruiting (open challenge with a recruiting email address). Same company as Felix Rieseberg's statements, so not independent corroboration.
- **Exact quotes:** 
- "Since early 2024, our performance engineering team has used a take-home test where candidates optimize code for a simulated accelerator. Over 1,000 candidates have completed it, and dozens now work here, including engineers who brought up our Trainium cluster and shipped every model since Claude 3 Opus. But each new Claude model has forced us to redesign the test." (Anthropic post, opening section)
- "When given the same time limit, Claude Opus 4 outperformed most human applicants. That still allowed us to distinguish the strongest candidates—but then Claude Opus 4.5 matched even those. Humans can still outperform models when given unlimited time, but under the constraints of the take-home test, we no longer had a way to distinguish between the output of our top candidates and our most capable model." (Anthropic post, opening section; note the original contains an em dash, keep it verbatim only inside quotation marks)
- "By May 2025, Claude 3.7 Sonnet had already crept up to the point where over 50% of candidates would have been better off delegating to Claude Code entirely. I then tested a pre-release version of Claude Opus 4 on the take-home. It came up with a more optimized solution than almost all humans did within the 4-hour limit." (section 'Then Claude Opus 4 defeated it')
- "I watched Claude Code work on the problem for 2 hours, gradually improving its solution. It solved the initial bottlenecks, implemented all the common micro-optimizations, and met our passing threshold in under an hour. Then it stopped, convinced it had hit an insurmountable memory bandwidth bottleneck." (section 'Then Claude Opus 4.5 defeated that')
- "When I told Claude the cycle count it was possible to achieve, it thought for a while and found the trick. ... By the 2-hour mark, its score matched the best human performance within that time limit—and that human had made heavy use of Claude 4 with steering. We tried it out in our internal test-time compute harness for more rigor and confirmed it could both beat humans in 2 hours and continue climbing with time." (same section)
- "We were about to release a model where the best strategy on our take-home would be delegating to Claude Code." (same section)
- "Some colleagues suggested banning AI assistance. I didn't want to do this. Beyond the enforcement challenges, I had a sense that given people continue to play a vital role in our work, I should be able to figure out some way for them to distinguish themselves in a setting with AI— like they'd have on the job." (section 'Considering the options')
- "A human trying to steer Claude would likely be constantly behind, understanding what Claude did only after the fact. The dominant strategy might become sitting back and watching." (section 'Considering the options')
- "Nowadays performance engineers at Anthropic still have lots of work to do, but it looks more like tough debugging, systems design, performance analysis, figuring out how to verify the correctness of our systems, and figuring out how to make Claude's code simpler and more elegant. Unfortunately these things are tough to test in an objective way without a lot of time or common context." (section 'Considering the options')
- "Anthropic's general candidate guidance asks candidates to complete take-homes without AI unless indicated otherwise. For this take-home, we explicitly indicate otherwise. Longer-horizon problems are harder for AI to solve completely, so candidates can use AI tools (as they would on the job) while still needing to demonstrate their own skills." (section on design goals)
- "Unlike Zachtronics games, I intentionally provided no visualization or debugging tools. ... Building debugging tools is part of what's being tested ... Judgment about how to invest in tooling is part of the signal." (section 'Attempt 2: Going weirder')
- "Early results are promising: scores correlate well with the caliber of candidates' past work, and one of my most capable colleagues scored higher than any candidate so far. I'm still sad to have given up the realism and varied depth of the original. But realism may be a luxury we no longer have. The original worked because it resembled real work. The replacement works because it simulates novel work." (end of 'Attempt 2')
- "Over the next year and a half, about 1,000 candidates completed the take-home, and it helped us hire most of our current performance engineering team. It proved especially valuable for candidates with limited experience on paper: several of our highest-performing engineers came directly from undergrad" (section 'Early results')
- "We're curious how long it lasts." (closing line)
- TechCrunch correction: "An earlier version of this article misstated Anthropic's policy towards the use of AI tools on the take-home test. In fact, AI use is expressly permitted." [TechCrunch wording via fetch summary]
- **Date and recency:** 
> Anthropic post published 21 January 2026; TechCrunch 22 January 2026. Covers the take-home from its origin (November 2023, used from early 2024) to the Claude Opus 4.5 launch period (late 2025). Model claims refer to Claude 3.7 Sonnet (by May 2025), pre-release Claude Opus 4, Claude Sonnet 4.5, and pre-release and launched Claude Opus 4.5. Ten months old at time of research (November 2026); newer models may change the picture.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> 1) Anthropic's performance engineering take-home (optimise code for a simulated accelerator) was predictive and helped hire most of the current performance engineering team. 2) Each new Claude model forced a redesign: Opus 4 beat most applicants within the 4-hour limit; Opus 4.5 matched the best human within the 2-hour limit of version 2. 3) Under the take-home's time constraints, Anthropic 'no longer had a way to distinguish between the output of our top candidates and our most capable model'; humans still win with unlimited time. 4) Hume rejected banning AI (enforcement problems, and wanting candidates to distinguish themselves 'in a setting with AI ... like they'd have on the job') and rejected simply raising the bar above Claude-alone (the dominant strategy might become 'sitting back and watching'). 5) A first redesign around a harder, realistic optimisation problem (2D TPU register transposition avoiding bank conflicts) failed because Claude drew on prior experience. 6) Version 3 uses out-of-distribution Zachtronics-style puzzles on a tiny, heavily constrained instruction set, optimised for minimal instruction count, with no debugging or visualisation tools supplied, so tooling judgement is part of the signal. It trades realism for novelty: 'The original worked because it resembled real work. The replacement works because it simulates novel work.' 7) The real job now looks more like debugging, systems design, performance analysis, verification and simplifying Claude's code, which are hard to test objectively in a short format. 8) The original take-home was released as an open challenge; beat 1487 cycles and email recruiting.
- **Headline figures:** 
- "Over 1,000 candidates have completed it" / "about 1,000 candidates completed the take-home" over roughly a year and a half from early 2024; "dozens now work here".
- "over 50% of candidates would have been better off delegating to Claude Code entirely": Claude 3.7 Sonnet, by May 2025, on the original 4-hour version.
- Original time limit 4 hours; version 2 cut to 2 hours (partly because scheduling overhead caused 'multi-week delays').
- Pre-release Claude Opus 4: 'a more optimized solution than almost all humans did within the 4-hour limit'.
- Pre-release Claude Opus 4.5 in Claude Code: met the passing threshold 'in under an hour'; by 2 hours matched the best human performance within that limit, after being told the achievable cycle count. That best human 'had made heavy use of Claude 4 with steering'.
- Published benchmarks on the released challenge (clock cycles, lower is better): 2164 Claude Opus 4 after many hours in the test-time compute harness; 1790 Claude Opus 4.5 in a casual Claude Code session, 'approximately matching the best human performance in 2 hours'; 1579 Opus 4.5 after 2 hours in the harness; 1548 Claude Sonnet 4.5 after many more than 2 hours of test-time compute; 1487 Opus 4.5 after 11.5 hours in the harness; 1363 Opus 4.5 in an improved harness after many hours.
- Challenge threshold: below 1487 cycles ('beating Claude's best performance at launch'). The fastest human solution ever submitted 'substantially exceeds' Claude's best with extensive test-time compute (no number given).
- **Sample and method:** 
> Practitioner account, not a study. About 1,000 software/performance engineering candidates took the take-home over ~18 months. Model comparisons are the author's own runs of pre-release and released Claude models (in Claude Code sessions and in an internal test-time compute harness), scored by cycle count against the human score distribution. Some Opus 4.5 runs had a human hint (target cycle count). Version 3 validity claim rests on early, unquantified results and colleague testing. Subjects are software engineers (performance engineering roles).

### Interviewing

- **What is measured:** 
> Original: performance optimisation skill on a realistic task (parallel tree traversal on a simulated accelerator, progressing through multicore, SIMD and VLIW packing; version 1 also included a bug to debug), with design goals of representing real work, high signal, no specific domain knowledge, and compatibility with AI use. Version 3: problem solving on novel, out-of-distribution work (minimal-instruction puzzles on a constrained instruction set), including judgement about how much to invest in building one's own debugging tools. Hume says the real job increasingly involves debugging, systems design, performance analysis, verification and simplifying Claude's code, which the take-home does not directly measure.
- **Ai policy in interview:** 
> AI permitted (explicitly, overriding Anthropic's general no-AI take-home guidance), with an AI-resistant redesign. Banning AI was considered and rejected. TechCrunch initially misstated the policy and corrected it: 'AI use is expressly permitted.'
- **Scope and status:** 
> Rolled out for Anthropic's performance engineering team hiring (one team, not company-wide). Version 3 is the current 'Claude-resistant' take-home used in the typical process; the original is public as an open challenge.
- **Validity evidence:** 
> No criterion-validity coefficients. Anecdotal: the test 'proved predictive' (one top scorer quickly found a workaround for a launch-blocking compiler bug); it 'helped us hire most of our current performance engineering team'; several top engineers hired from undergrad on its strength. Version 3: 'scores correlate well with the caliber of candidates' past work' (early, no r reported). No adverse impact or reliability data.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strongly supports 'stop testing convergent coding': a well-scored, convergent optimisation task stopped separating top humans from the model within a timed format. Complicates it too: Anthropic's response was not to drop the coding test for a design conversation, but to keep a coding take-home and move it out of distribution (novel puzzles), explicitly giving up realism. Supports 'working with an agent': AI is allowed because that is the job, and tooling judgement is scored. It also raises a problem for agent-directed tests, since if the model is better alone, 'the dominant strategy might become sitting back and watching'. Hume's list of what the job now is (debugging, systems design, verification, simplifying Claude's code) supports the design-judgement angle, but he says these are hard to test objectively in limited time. Nothing on communication or team fit.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> "Humans can still outperform models when given unlimited time." On version 3: "I'm still sad to have given up the realism and varied depth of the original. But realism may be a luxury we no longer have." Results are early ("Early results are promising"; "It might have lower variance than the original"). The real job's skills are "tough to test in an objective way without a lot of time or common context. It's always been hard to design interviews that represent the job, but now it's harder than ever." The durability of the fix is unknown: "We're curious how long it lasts." Attempt 1 failed because Claude could draw on 'a larger toolbox of experience' for well-known optimisation problems.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Single company, single team, single role type (performance engineering), and the company sells the model being compared, so there is an incentive to present the model as matching top humans. The comparison is under a strict time limit; with unlimited time the best human beat the model, so 'cannot distinguish' is a property of the timed format, not of the people. One Opus 4.5 result required a human hint (the target cycle count). The best human in that comparison was themselves using Claude 4. Validity claims are anecdotal with no published coefficients. The redesign shows a coding take-home can be made AI-resistant again, which cuts against a claim that coding tests are obsolete, though Hume concedes it no longer resembles the job. Secondary coverage framed it as 'cheating', which TechCrunch had to correct.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> Anthropic wants candidates 'who excel at collaborating with AI'. Policy by stage: (1) Application and resume: AI allowed for refining a first draft the candidate wrote; using AI to invent experiences or write application answers is not allowed. (2) Take-home assessments: no AI by default; AI allowed only where Anthropic explicitly says so. (3) Interview preparation: AI use encouraged. (4) Live interviews: no AI by default ('This is all you'), unless Anthropic indicates otherwise. Exceptions: the 'unless we indicate otherwise' carve-outs for take-homes and live interviews, and accommodations requested through the recruiter. Candidates are expected to be transparent about their AI use. Anthropic discloses it uses Claude in its own hiring workflow but says Claude does not make hiring decisions and candidate data is not used for training. The rationale for the default ban is to assess 'your unique skills and strengths' and 'how you think through problems in real time'.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- The five common ways meta-analysts build range restriction artifact distributions (deriving U from a subset of studies, estimating U from hiring rates, using test publisher norms, assuming a distribution, and related approaches) each have flaws that usually cause substantial overcorrection, so the validity of many selection procedures has been substantially overestimated.
- Most validation studies are concurrent (incumbents tested). In concurrent studies range restriction can only be indirect, and indirect restriction only shrinks predictor variance much if the original selection method correlates very highly with the predictor (around .80 or more), which the authors judge implausible since observed predictor intercorrelations top out around .50.
- Applying a correction factor derived from predictive (applicant) studies to concurrent studies inflates validity. The dominant flaw found was failure to separate predictive and concurrent studies (21 of the meta-analyses judged inappropriately corrected).
- Principle of conservative estimation: absent a credible basis for a range restriction correction, report the validity corrected only for criterion unreliability (using .60 interrater reliability) and call it conservative.
- Revised estimates keep a similar ranking to Schmidt and Hunter (1998) but lower magnitudes by roughly .10-.20. Structured interviews become the top-ranked predictor (.42).
- The strongest predictors are job-specific measures (structured interviews, job knowledge tests, empirically keyed biodata, work samples), suggesting a closer behavioural match between predictor and criterion helps; 'sample' measures fare well relative to 'sign' measures.
- Cognitive ability's validity advantage largely disappears (.31), similar to biodata, integrity tests and personality-based EI, while it keeps much larger Black-White subgroup differences.
- Structured interviews have a large residual SD (.19), so results vary a lot across settings; a risk-averse employer might rank by the lower credibility value instead, where biodata, contextualized conscientiousness, job knowledge, work samples and structured interviews lead.
- Sensitivity analysis: using .52 criterion reliability adds about .02 on average (max .03); a deliberately generous indirect range restriction correction (rzx = .50, selection ratio .05) adds about .02 on average (max .04).

For coding interviews this maps most closely to realistic take-home or pair-programming tasks, not to abstract algorithm puzzles, which arguably sit closer to GMA or job-knowledge measures.
- **Ai policy in interview:** Not applicable (pre-AI research; no tooling policy discussed).
- **Scope and status:** Not applicable (academic research syntheses, not an employer's hiring rollout).
- **Validity evidence:** 
> Work sample tests: .54 in Schmidt & Hunter (1998), corrected for criterion unreliability only, inherited from Hunter & Hunter (1984). Roth et al. (2005) re-estimate: .26 observed, .33 corrected for criterion unreliability; no range-restriction correction because data were lacking. Structured interviews: .51 in Schmidt & Hunter (1998), corrected for both criterion unreliability and range restriction; revised to .42 by Sackett et al. (2022). Important comparability point: since neither the .54 nor the .33 work sample figure is corrected for range restriction, the drop from .54 to .33 reflects cleaner data and study selection, not a change in correction method. By contrast, much of the drop for structured interviews and GMA in Sackett et al. comes from removing what they argue were range-restriction overcorrections.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports 'stop testing convergent coding' indirectly: the classic evidence base rewards realistic task samples and structured conversation over unstructured judgement, and gives no validity evidence at all for algorithm-puzzle interviews. Complicates the angle in two ways. First, the often-quoted claim that work samples are the single best predictor (.54) rests on 1984 data that Roth et al. found inflated; the better current estimate (.33) puts work samples below structured interviews (.42 in Sackett et al. 2022), which favours a structured design conversation and team-fit interview over a hands-on coding exercise. Second, Schmidt & Hunter note work samples only suit people who already know the job, which matters if 'the job' has shifted to directing agents: a pre-agent work sample may sample the wrong tasks. Supports 'design conversation' and 'communication and team fit' via the structured interview evidence; supports 'working with an agent' only if the work sample is redesigned to sample that task.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> Guilford (1967) formally defines divergent production as 'generation of information from given information, where the emphasis is upon variety and quantity of output from the same source; likely to involve transfer'. Convergent production also generates information from given information, but it is the 'prevailing function when the input information is sufficient to determine a unique answer'. It is the home of deduction: divergent production generates 'logical possibilities', convergent production 'logical necessities'. In convergent production the problem is rigorously structured, the search narrow, output limited and success criteria sharp. Divergent tasks are loose, the search broad, output plentiful and criteria lax. The two are not exclusive: people often use 'much divergent production on the way to a convergent answer'. In 1959 Guilford put it in plain terms: convergent thinking 'proceed[s] toward one right answer, or one that is more or less clearly demanded by the given information', while divergent-thinking abilities 'emphasize searching activities with freedom to go in different directions'. He linked creativity mainly to divergent thinking but warned it would be 'incorrect to say that divergent thinking accounts for all the intellectual components of creative production'. Both are two of five operations in his structure-of-intellect (SI) model. The definitions were drawn from the tests used to measure the abilities ('This definition arose directly from consideration of the kinds of tests needed').
- **Headline figures:** 
> SI model: five operations, six products, contents (four in 1967: figural, symbolic, semantic, behavioral), giving 120 hypothesised abilities in the 1967 version; APA Dictionary: 150 factors in the later version ('120 in an earlier version of the theory'). Guilford 1967 Ch. 7: 'Of the 24 hypothesized [convergent-production] abilities, 13 have not been investigated.' Guilford 1959: interest-inventory correlations with fluency, flexibility and originality test scores were statistically significant 'but the coefficients were all below 0.30'.
- **Sample and method:** 
> Factor analysis of batteries of psychometric aptitude tests, mostly given to adults (many in military and USAF samples) and later to schoolchildren, in Guilford's Aptitudes Research Project. Convergent and divergent were defined by test format: completion items scored for the number and variety of responses (divergent) versus items with one specified right answer (convergent). Not a study of software engineers.

### Interviewing

- **What is measured:** 
> Two intellectual operations. Convergent production: producing the one correct or logically required answer from the given information (deduction). Divergent production: producing many varied candidate answers from the given information (fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration). This maps onto the angle as follows: a classic coding test with one correct, testable output is a convergent-production task in Guilford's sense.
- **Ai policy in interview:** Not applicable.
- **Scope and status:** Not applicable.
- **Validity evidence:** 
> None for coding interviews. Guilford 1959 (Vernon reprint, c. p. 182) cites only small criterion correlations, all from Guilford 1956a: ideational fluency 0.37 with 'performance of aircraft engineers whose chief work was in designing aircraft parts', where the criterion was 'rate of increase in pay over a limited period of time'; adaptive flexibility 0.31 with the same criterion; expressional fluency 0.25 with astronomy grades 'for some unknown reason'. Uncorrected r, small samples not stated in the reprint. The APA Dictionary calls the evidence for the overall SI theory 'weak'.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports 'stop testing convergent coding' by giving the term a precise, citable origin. In Guilford's words, a convergent task is one where 'the input information is sufficient to determine a unique answer', with 'one right answer, or one that is more or less clearly demanded by the given information'. That describes a LeetCode-style problem with a fixed correct output, which an agent can now produce. Design conversation and directing an agent lean on divergent production (generating several possibilities) and evaluation (judging them), which is a separate SI operation. There is a complication: Guilford says people use 'much divergent production on the way to a convergent answer', so even a convergent coding test involves some divergent search. Calling it purely convergent is a simplification. Also, Guilford's 'evaluation' operation, not divergent production, is the closer fit for reviewing agent output.

- **Limitations stated:**

In tests there is 'sometimes a twilight region, if not an apparent continuum' (Ch. 7). Guilford 1959: 'it would be incorrect to say that divergent thinking accounts for all the intellectual components of creative production.' Guilford 1967 Ch. 7: convergent production 'is one of the less explored regions of intelligence', with 13 of 24 hypothesised abilities uninvestigated.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Hiring in elite professional service firms is not only skills sorting but 'cultural matching' between candidates, evaluators and firms.
- Cultural fit was a formal, firm-mandated evaluative criterion, not just personal taste; firms required evaluators to rate fit alongside technical and communication skills.
- 'Fit' as used meant similarity in play styles (leisure pursuits, extracurriculars such as lacrosse, squash, marathons), background experiences and self-presentation, not work styles or values; evaluators separated it from client-facing 'polish'.
- Evaluators justified fit by long hours (wanting colleagues as 'playmates' or friends) and by retention; they described the work as 'not rocket science' and believed training made prior technical knowledge less important.
- Fit was measured two ways: similarity to the firm's perceived personality (more common in resume screens) and similarity to the evaluator's own self (more common in interviews), e.g. the 'airplane test' and icebreaker chat seeking shared experiences.
- Three processes link similarity to outcomes: organizational (fit as formal criterion), cognitive ('looking-glass merit', evaluators define merit in their own image), and affective (shared interests create a 'spark' that makes evaluators champion a candidate in deliberations).
- Fit mattered more in less structured interviews: most in law (pure conversation), least in consulting (case interviews), and it did not track the interpersonal demands of the job.
- Structured technical case questions reduced but did not remove fit; once a baseline of competence was met, fit rather than absolute case performance routinely drove decisions, and some interviewers lowered the technical bar for candidates they liked.
- Consequences: firms seek demographic diversity but cultural homogeneity; valued signals are tied to white upper-middle-class, parent-funded leisure, creating inequality by parental class and some gendered disadvantage, even though women and minorities with the right signals were hired.
- Selecting on play-style similarity may lead evaluators to 'hire for themselves rather than for the organization'; effect on organizational performance is untested.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Being watched while solving a whiteboard coding problem and thinking aloud significantly lowered correctness compared with solving it alone in a private room.
- Self-reported stress (NASA-TLX frustration and stress item) and physiological proxies of cognitive load (fixation duration, pupil dilation) and stress (slower saccade velocity) were significantly higher in the public condition.
- A standard technical interview resembles the Trier Social Stress Test (a cognitively demanding task plus social-evaluative threat), so it may measure stress handling rather than problem-solving ability.
- Concurrent think-aloud adds extraneous load; participants in private more often mentally executed their code against test cases.
- Post-hoc, no women passed in public (n=5) while all women passed in private (n=4).
- Recommended (as hypotheses): retrospective think-aloud (solve privately, then explain), treating the problem as a vehicle for conversation, separating time-pressure stress from performance anxiety, warmups, free resets, partial program sketches, laptop or paper instead of whiteboard.
- **Headline figures:** 
- Failure rate (not passing all 3 test cases): 61.5% public vs 36.3% private (paper's own figures; 36.3% appears to be 8/22 rounded down). Authors describe this as 'nearly twice as many' failing; the ratio is about 1.7x.
- Correctness score on a 0-3 scale, excluding timeouts: public mean 1.58 (SD 1.29), median 1; private mean 2.29 (SD 1.12), median 3. Mann-Whitney Z=180.5, p=0.038, d=0.57. The 'reduced by more than half' claim rests on the medians (3 to 1, a two-thirds drop); the means drop by about 31%.
- NASA-TLX frustration and stress (1-21 scale), median 11 public vs 7 private; U=184.0, p=0.004, d=0.64. No significant differences for effort, mental demand, physical demand or performance.
- Mean fixation duration (first 10 minutes): 251.48±13.37 ms public vs 238.99±17.36 ms private; p=0.0028, d=0.78.
- Mean pupil dilation: 0.13±0.009 mm public vs 0.12±0.003 mm private; p=0.0022, d=1.43.
- Mean saccade velocity: 94.41±45.59 deg/s public vs 120.77±39.43 deg/s private (medians 84 vs 119.25); Z=277, p=0.003, d=0.59.
- Time: public participants finished on average 1 min 36 s sooner, not significant; more than 50% of public participants finished within 10 minutes.
- Women, post-hoc: 0 of 5 passed in public, 4 of 4 passed in private.
- Sample: 50 recruited (12 female), 2 withdrew, 48 analysed: 22 private, 26 public. Power analysis called for about 18 per group.
- **Sample and method:** 
> Block-randomized controlled trial, between-subjects. 50 undergraduate and graduate CS students at NC State recruited for extra course credit (12 female); 48 analysed (22 private, 26 public). Students, not working software engineers, though many were job-hunting. Single task: 'Longest Substring Without Repeating Characters' (a common LeetCode/industry interview question), any language, on a whiteboard, 30-minute limit. Public condition: experimenter present, participant thinks aloud, experimenter silent apart from brief replies. Private condition: participant alone in a closed room, no think-aloud. All wore SMI mobile eye-tracking glasses. Scored 0-3 against three given test cases; pass means all three. Post-task NASA-TLX and survey. Interviewers were women in all but one session.

### Interviewing

- **What is measured:** 
> Convergent coding: correctness of a single algorithmic whiteboard solution (0-3 test cases), plus time, self-reported workload and eye-tracking proxies for cognitive load and stress. The study does not measure design judgement, debugging of existing code, collaboration, or job performance. Its argument is that the public format confounds problem-solving ability with tolerance of social-evaluative stress (performance anxiety).
- **Ai policy in interview:** Not applicable (2020 study, no AI tools; participants coded on a whiteboard without any tooling).
- **Scope and status:** Not applicable (lab experiment, not an interview format rolled out by a company).
- **Validity evidence:** 
> None. The study has no criterion-validity data: it does not link either format to later job performance. It shows the observed format lowers scores and raises stress relative to a private format, which bears on construct validity (what the score reflects), not predictive validity.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports 'stop testing convergent coding' in its classic form: a watched, think-aloud whiteboard puzzle partly measures stress tolerance, and the authors explicitly propose letting candidates solve privately and then explain. Supports the 'design conversation' and 'communication' parts by recommending the problem be a 'shared vehicle' for dialogue and retrospective explanation. Complicates the design-conversation idea too: an observed design conversation is still performed in front of an evaluator, so the social-evaluative threat does not go away just because the task changes. The study cannot say how much of the penalty comes from being watched versus having to talk while coding, and the authors note an interactive interviewer may help or hurt. Mitigations the paper offers (warmups, advance detail, free resets, familiar tools) transfer to a design conversation or an agent-pairing exercise.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
- Interviewers were almost all women; real interviews are mostly run by men, so stereotype threat could make effects larger.
- "Our interview settings may be producing less stress than real interviews with real stakes, while adding discomfort from the eye-tracker."
- The interviewer was silent and non-interactive; "it is unclear whether an interactive interviewer has an additional positive or negative effect on performance."
- Students "may not fully represent industry developers".
- One question only; "other coding questions may be easier or more difficult" and "small amounts of stress can enhance performance... we are not claiming that all interview questions will result in the same barriers."
- Participants may have seen the problem before (none said so).
- Eye-tracker accuracy affected by lighting and movement; four participants lacked pupil data and one recording failed.
- Guidance is offered as "hypotheses that need further evaluation rather than outright policy."
- **Counter evidence:** 
- Confounded manipulation (not stated as a limitation by the authors): the public condition added both an observer and concurrent think-aloud, while the private condition had neither, so the effect cannot be attributed to being watched alone, despite the abstract's 'simply being watched' wording.
- Small sample (22 vs 26) and a single task; the primary correctness result is p=0.038, not far below 0.05. The gender finding is post-hoc on 9 people.
- Scores exclude timeouts, and the 'more than half' headline uses medians on a 4-point scale; mean scores fell by about 31%.
- Private-condition participants had no one to ask; one participant misunderstood and withdrew, so the private format has its own costs.
- The authors cite Google research (Shaper) that four interviews suffice for a consistent hire decision, though they note this addresses consistency, not validity.

- **Limitations stated:**

> Exact match against the developer's commit is strict: a different but correct merge counts as a failure, so true capability is higher than solve rates suggest (Merge-Bench's 'lower bound'). Shen finds LLMs beat traditional merge tools by 18-22 points, mostly by always attempting a resolution, so LLMs are already more useful than existing automation. ConGra's lenient scoring shows 2024 models getting 75-84% on some languages. GitGoodBench's 76.67% 'success rate' is often misread as accuracy; it only means no error occurred.

- **Sample and method:** 
> Stack Overflow: online survey, 49,009 usable responses from 177 countries (~15K excluded), mostly developers and people learning to code, recruited through Stack Overflow's own channels (onsite messaging, blog, newsletter, banners, social), so skewed toward engaged Stack Overflow users. Per-question response counts vary (AI questions roughly 29K-34K). Descriptive percentages only. DORA: global survey of nearly 5,000 technology professionals (software engineers and adjacent roles) run 13 June-21 July 2025, plus 78 in-depth interviews; latent-factor measures (AI adoption = use + reliance + trust), structural equation and Bayesian models reported with 89% credible intervals; capability effects are interactions (moderation) with AI adoption. Cross-sectional and self-reported.

### Interviewing

- **What is measured:** 
> Neither source measures an interview format. Stack Overflow measures self-reported trust in AI output accuracy, frustrations with AI tools, agent use and when developers seek human help. DORA measures self-reported AI adoption, trust, and outcomes (individual effectiveness, throughput, instability, code quality, burnout, friction) and which organisational capabilities moderate AI's effects. Both bear on the construct 'judging and verifying AI output', which is a candidate thing for an interview to assess.
- **Ai policy in interview:** 
> Not applicable. (DORA's 'clear and communicated AI stance' is about workplace AI policy, not interviews.)
- **Scope and status:** Not applicable.
- **Validity evidence:** 
> None. Neither source reports criterion validity for any hiring method. DORA reports model fit and credible intervals for associations between capabilities, AI adoption and outcomes, not predictive validity of skills assessments.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports 'working with an agent' as a thing to assess: the dominant failure mode developers report is output that is almost right (66%), so the scarce skill is spotting what is wrong in plausible code, not producing code. DORA says this directly: low trust indicates 'a need for critical validation skills', training should teach how to 'critically guide, evaluate, and validate AI-generated work', and individual work shifts to 'deciding and verifying' including 'assessing code that looks remarkably similar to correct code'. That is a ready-made case for interview tasks that hand a candidate agent output to review. Supports 'basic fluency' too: adoption is near universal (80-90%), so not using AI is unusual. The amplifier framing complicates a purely individual view: DORA says outcomes depend on systems (version control, small batches, platforms), so an interview that measures individual judgement captures only part of what makes AI pay off. Caution on wording: DORA's seven capabilities include strong version control practices but NOT code review; code review appears only as an AI use case (56%), a reason small batches matter ('harder to review larger batches of code') and in leadership guidance. Do not write that DORA names code review as a foundation.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Stack Overflow methodology: respondents 'recruited primarily through channels owned by Stack Overflow', so 'highly-engaged' users are likely over-represented (statement truncated in extraction). DORA footnote 20: 'Although we try to do the work to create the conditions to speak causally, we don't want to give false assurances that we understand the underlying causal structure... ultimately, we're doing comparisons.' DORA footnote 19: 'given we have a snapshot, we're treating these highly co-determinant phenomena as unidimensional. If we had panel data, we could be more explicit about the cycle.' DORA, p. 50: 'we will continue validating, revising, and refining the DORA AI Capabilities Model with further research.' DORA p. 31 cautions 'against interpreting these findings of AI's ubiquity as an indication that all organizations should rapidly move to adopt AI'.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> The two surveys disagree on trust: DORA finds 70% have some confidence in AI output quality and 24% 'a great deal' or 'a lot', while Stack Overflow finds 46% distrust accuracy and only 3.1% highly trust. Question wording differs (quality vs accuracy; DORA has a 'somewhat' midpoint counted as confidence), so neither number should be quoted as 'developer trust' without its wording. DORA reads skepticism as healthy ('trust but verify' as 'a sign of mature adoption') and suggests developers 'may also already be accounting for this limitation', which cuts against framing distrust as a crisis. Stack Overflow's own blog swaps the 66%/45% figures, so secondary coverage citing '45% almost right' or '66% spend more time fixing' is wrong relative to the results page. Both are self-report from mid-2025, before wider agent use; agent use was a minority (31% use agents at work in any frequency). Neither shows that verification skill can be assessed in an interview or predicts job performance.
