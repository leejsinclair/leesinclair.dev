### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> Release: 'the great snake who was the main enemy of the sungod Ra'; the ritual 'would help the sun on its journey through the underworld during the night, when he had to fight off the serpent in order to reemerge in the morning'. Box: 'Apophis was the serpent of chaos that attempted each night to prevent the sun god Ra from completing his journey through the underworld'; priests performed rituals 'to protect the order of the world' and 'symbolically destroy Apophis and his powers'. Danish: 'solgudens ærkefjende og hans allierede' (the sun god's arch-enemy and his allies). Abstract: 'the arch-enemy of the sun god Re'. The nightly framing implies repeated, never-final combat, but none of the press says outright whether Apophis is ever finally defeated. archaeologymag cites the decree's 'like Apophis on the morning of the New Year Festival', tying the ritual to the start of the year.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> Release box: Apophis 'drawn in the shape of a snake and his name written on papyrus in red ink, or represented as a wax figure', then 'pierced with knives and burned while magical spells were recited'. Hagen: the drawing was 'trampled on, spit on, pierced with a knife and ultimately burned'. Fragments are 'mass-produced copies of drawings and texts'; the caption shows a near-complete example 'with three knives cutting into his body, with his name in the hieratic at the top'. archaeologymag: the MMA sheet has 'squares of drawings' that 'would have been cut along the dividing lines before use'. Performers: 'typically priests'; the paper 'looks at the types of priests who were responsible for carrying out the ritual in the temples'; Hagen also says literate non-priests acted as magicians and people went to scribes for help. Place: in temples (Thutmose III mortuary temple) and, per Hagen, 'widely used outside temples'. Danish box omits the drawing and red ink, saying only that the name was written on papyrus or the snake made as wax figures.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports: mass-produced sheets cut from grids, burnt and dumped, read as consumables of a routine, repeated temple job rather than a one-off rite; the nightly-attack framing fits chaos held off by maintenance; Hagen's emphasis on 'unglamorous rubbish dumps' and the temple's 'daily economy and logistics' is itself a maintenance-over-monuments argument. Complicates: the press stresses widespread and private use (curses on personal enemies, illness, weather, personal success), which shifts the ritual from cosmic maintenance toward everyday magic and personal attack; 'regular' is not quantified; and the strongest frequency wording is press-release interpretation, not demonstrated counts.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Charred papyrus fragments from the rubbish dump outside the north enclosure wall of Thutmose III's Temple of Millions of Years at Thebes are the material remains of the 'Overthrowing Apophis' curse ritual, and are the earliest direct evidence for it found so far.
- Two types: Type I shows a single serpent, head turned downwards, cut by three knives, captioned 'Apophis who is the Roarer, who is the Terrifying-of-face'; Type II shows three serpent-like beings with non-serpent (possibly human) heads, captioned 'Children of the Rebel'. Both on new (non-palimpsest) papyrus, one side only, red ink only.
- Red grid lines show they were mass produced in squares on larger sheets and then cut up; P. MMA 28.3.113, a later uncut sheet from Deir el-Bahri Tomb MMA 801 (with mirror-written hieratic on the recto), shows what such a sheet looked like. Small differences in hand and iconography mean the fragments came from more than one sheet and more than one scribe.
- The fragments match the instructions in P. Bremner-Rhind closely (new papyrus, red ink, serpent with head down, the two epithets), and Bremner-Rhind 23.12 explicitly pairs 'the Roarer and the Terrifying-of-Face' when Apophis is put on the fire.
- Burning (carbonised edges) and holes possibly from stabbing match the ritual's spells for spitting, trampling, spearing, binding, stabbing and setting fire.
- About 40 fragments imply a minimum of twenty separate performances if each used one of each type: not enough to prove a daily rite, but suggesting regularity; a lower limit only, because the ritual was designed to destroy its own evidence.
- Because the materials were burnt, physical survival is rare; clay execration figures are probably overrepresented in the record.
- Mass-produced papyri suggest the wax figures were also made quickly, perhaps with stamps (though all known stamps are first millennium BC).
- In temples, the Roman-period Book of the Temple assigns the ritual to the 'scribe of the god's book' (who likely drew and wrote the papyri) and the 'scorpion charmer' (who recited the Book of Overthrowing Apophis).
- Bremner-Rhind lists times for the ritual (sunrise, midday, sunset, sixth hour of night, eighth hour of day, festivals) but also 'every hour of the day and night' and 'every day'; its claim that it was done at Karnak 'in the course of each day' may be literal or a device to lend authority.
- Use outside temples is harder to establish; the text envisages private use against personal enemies, for protection and promotion, against storms and bad weather, and there are hints of therapeutic use against venom.
- The Decree for Amenhotep son of Hapu's threat that offenders will 'become like Apophis on the morning of the New Year Festival' refers to a well-known curse ritual tied to the start of the year; the authors suggest the ritual's fame was probably not far behind the Opening of the Mouth and the Daily Temple Ritual.

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> Apophis is the great serpent and main enemy of the sun god Re during his perilous night journey through the underworld, after which Re rises and brings a new day; the battle is 'recurring' and a key concern of New Kingdom netherworld books (Amduat 7th hour: 'one slaughters Apophis in the Netherworld'). The Amduat says the magic used to repel Apophis 'is done in the secrecy of the Netherworld, and likewise it is done on earth', which the authors say has 'some truth in it' given the ritual. Footnote 76 notes the episodic, recurring nature of the nightly battles and quotes Müller-Roth's conclusion that 'Die ultimative Vernichtung ist unmöglich' (ultimate annihilation is impossible), reiterated by Backes 2025. Epithets used: 'the Roarer' (hmhm.ty) and 'the Terrifying-of-face' (nHA-Hr); 'Children of the Rebel' are a manifestation of Apophis or beings in their own right. The ritual's times track solar events and the hours of the main mythical battles. Mythic conflict is mirrored on earth in the king's struggle against his enemies, and the text also blames Apophis for storms and bad weather.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> Per P. Bremner-Rhind and the fragments: drawings of Apophis and the Children of the Rebel in red ink on new papyrus, captioned with names; wax figures of Apophis (red wax, name inscribed in red ink), sometimes bound and placed in a container; enemies of the king (with names of parents and children) drawn and modelled in wax. Spells accompany spitting on Apophis, trampling with the left foot, spearing, binding, stabbing with a knife and setting fire to him, using bryony (xAs) kindling and 'the fire in the furnace of the coppersmiths'. The temple fragments show charring and holes that may be from stabbing. Papyri were mass produced in gridded sheets and cut into squares; wax figures were perhaps stamped. Performed presumably inside the temple, then the debris swept out to the dump. Personnel (from the Roman-period Book of the Temple): the 'scribe of the god's book' made the written objects; the 'scorpion charmer' recited the Book of Overthrowing Apophis; lector priests also feature. The officiant speaks as Pharaoh. Later temple inscriptions (Esna, Medamud, Kom Ombo, Edfu) link recitations to specific festivals; Esna adds four red goats and throwing the remains into water. Possible funerary/embalming use (MMA papyrus findspot; Apis embalming list), and possible private and therapeutic use.
- **Prescribed vs attested frequency:** 
> Prescribed: P. Bremner-Rhind says the ritual was done at Karnak 'in the course of each day' and lists sunrise, midday, sunset, the sixth hour of the night, the eighth hour of the day, the new-moon, monthly, sixth-day and fifteenth-day festivals, and 'every hour of the day and night' and 'every day'; one instruction says to put Apophis on the fire 'every day' (28.16); BD 15A speaks of performing it 'every day'; storms call for doing it 'very often'. The authors caution that such meta-textual statements tend to hyperbole and may lend authority rather than describe practice. Attested: about 40 fragments (12 Apophis, 14 Children of the Rebel, ~16 unassigned) imply at least 20 performances if each used one of each type, 'not necessarily enough to confirm it as a daily occurrence in this temple' but suggesting 'a certain regularity'; if performances used more than one of each, the count says less about frequency; and it is only a lower limit because most burnt papyri would not survive or be recognised. The authors conclude it was 'carried out repeatedly, but precisely on which days and at what time is impossible to say'. Separately, the Amenhotep son of Hapu decree ties it to the New Year Festival morning.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strongly supportive. The myth frames Apophis as a recurring enemy who is never finally destroyed (the article footnotes Müller-Roth: ultimate annihilation is impossible), and the ritual's prescription for every day, every hour and recurring festivals reflects routine upkeep rather than a single victory. The archaeology shows mass-produced, disposable, gridded papyrus squares made in batches by temple scribes and burnt over and over (at least twenty occasions), then swept out with the temple rubbish: maintenance as routine institutional work. The storm passage ('do this very often ... to prevent bad weather') is explicitly preventative. Complications: the authors refuse to confirm daily performance; minimum counts depend on an assumption (one of each type per rite); the 'each day' and 'every hour' claims may be authority-lending hyperbole; and the Amenhotep son of Hapu decree links the ritual especially to the New Year, suggesting calendar peaks as well as routine.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> (1) Pottery dating 'tentatively' suggests mid/late 18th to early 19th Dynasty. (2) Minimum of twenty occasions holds only 'if each occasion featured a single example of both types'; 'not necessarily enough to confirm it as a daily occurrence'; multiple examples per event would make the count 'less indicative of frequency'; 'this would only provide a lower limit'. (3) About 16 fragments too damaged to type; only the best preserved published here, the full series reserved for a final publication; 2025 groups not yet conserved or fully studied. (4) Holes 'may be related' to stabbing, judged unusual for insect damage, not proven. (5) Original square size cannot be established precisely because no fragment preserves both sides. (6) Findspots say little beyond multiple depositions; where and when the ritual was done is inferred. (7) Meta-textual claims in Bremner-Rhind (daily use at Karnak, secrecy of the House of Life) may be hyperbole or authority-lending; 'caution must be exercised'. (8) Palaeographic dating of P. MMA 28.3.113 'must be rather uncertain'. (9) Funerary use 'conceivable' but evidence not 'conclusive in any sense'. (10) Use outside temples 'more difficult to pin down'; evidence for private use is limited. (11) Stamps for wax figures are attested only in the first millennium BC; their use in the second millennium is 'unknown'. (12) The supporting sources for the ritual's status are 'suggestive rather than conclusive when analysed individually'. (13) How well known the ritual was 'is not easy to say'.
- **Source discrepancies:** 
> (1) P. Bremner-Rhind date: the article's own summary says '3rd Century BC', while the body says 'late 4th Century BC' and gives the owner's colophon as 'around 305 BC' / 'c. 305 BC'; the body is more specific (c. 305 BC falls at the very end of the 4th century). Other sources should be checked against c. 305 BC. (2) Fragment count: 'around 40' total versus 12 + 14 + 'sixteen or so' = about 42; press reports repeating '40' and the 12/14/16 split are consistent with the paper. (3) Footnote 68 says single-name curse figures were widely used 'also in the 2nd Millennium CE, not just in the 1st Millennium CE', evidently a slip for BCE given the 18th Dynasty date. (4) Press coverage (e.g. PopSci) reportedly named the journal differently ('Journal of the Egyptian Language and Antiquities'); the paper and Crossref give ZÄS. (5) Ink: the paper says exclusively red ink on both types; no conflict seen. (6) Press '3,400 years old' is a rounding of the 18th Dynasty pottery date; the paper itself gives no year count. (7) The paper says the ritual was 'presumably enacted somewhere inside the temple itself'; press phrasing that priests performed it in the temple overstates certainty slightly. (8) Footnote 18 says the enemies are called Snty 'five times' but lists six references (24.1, 27.22, 28.12, 29.10, 30.7, 30.20).

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- The mortuary temple of Thutmose III (Egyptian Henkhet-ankh) on the Theban west bank, slightly north of the Ramesseum, has been excavated since 2008 by a Spanish-Egyptian team led by Myriam Seco Álvarez (spelled 'Alvarez-Seco' in the article text, 'Seco Álvarez' in the bibliography). Earlier work: Daressy 1888-9, Weigall 1905, Ricke 1934-38.
- The areas outside the temple walls had never been excavated. A side entrance in the northern part of the wall led to the temple's rubbish heap, where ostraca and papyrus fragments with hieratic texts were found (Fig. 2 caption: heap east of the northern side entrance).
- Most texts come from the temple's administrative archive, chiefly the temple journal (dated entries in red ink, lists of offering deliveries, often from Karnak, and festivals including Thutmose III's coronation feast on I Shemu 3).
- This is the first temple archive from a New Kingdom temple; earlier comparanda are Abusir (Old Kingdom), Lahun (Middle Kingdom) and Tebtunis and Edfu (Graeco-Roman). Hagen calls it a 'missing link'.
- Literary ostraca (Teaching of Khety, Teaching of Amenemhat, Hymn to the Nile, A Man's Teaching for his Son, Kemit) reflect the mid-18th Dynasty school canon.
- At least one copy of the Daily Temple Ritual, in columns of cursive hieroglyphs, was found; previously the oldest known papyrus copies dated to the 22nd Dynasty, several hundred years later.
- This is the first time cultic texts have been found in a New Kingdom temple, and they shed light on rituals performed daily by the temple's priests.
- A handful of fragments carry the text of a ritual against Apophis known from the Ptolemaic Papyrus Bremner-Rhind (draw Apophis in red ink on unused papyrus, write his name, burn it while reciting spells). This shows the tradition goes back at least to Thutmose III, about 1000 years earlier than the handbook.
- Fig. 4-5 show a papyrus fragment with Apophis drawn as a serpent and his name in hieratic, in red ink, described as used in a cursing ritual in the temple.

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> Very little about the myth itself. Apophis is the target of a ritual 'mod Apophis' (against Apophis) and the fragment is called part of a 'forbandelsesritual' (cursing ritual). Hagen does not discuss the sun's night journey or whether chaos is finally defeated. (Elsewhere in the same issue, Rune Nyord's article mentions the fight against the serpent Apophis that threatens the sun's course, but that is not Hagen's article.)
- **Prescribed vs attested frequency:** 
> Hagen gives no prescribed frequency for the Apophis ritual. He frames the cultic texts generally as shedding light on rituals 'udført til daglig' (performed daily) by the priests, with the Apophis ritual as 'a good example', but this does not state that the Apophis ritual itself was daily. On attestation he is explicit that the act leaves few archaeological traces, so whether it was common temple practice, and since when, was uncertain; the fragments prove only that the tradition existed by Thutmose III's reign. No count of performances can be inferred. Any 'daily' reading of the Apophis rite specifically from this article would be an overstatement.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports the maintenance reading moderately: the Apophis rite sits in the same priestly rubbish as the Daily Temple Ritual and offering journals, i.e. inside the routine paperwork of a working temple, and the tradition is pushed back about 1000 years before Bremner-Rhind. It complicates it in that Hagen does not state the rite's frequency and stresses how little trace such acts leave, so the 'routine' claim must rest on other sources (e.g. Bremner-Rhind's own prescriptions).

Texts not yet published: 'Første skridt ... er naturligvis at publicere teksterne.' He also notes official offering lists are 'muligvis ideelle' (possibly idealised) compared with administrative practice.

- **Key claims:** 
- The book's own title says it 'is performed daily in the temple of Amen-Rê, Lord of the Thrones of the Two Lands, who dwells in Karnak' (col. 22).
- The opening sequence is a set of named actions on ʿApep: spitting, trampling with the left foot, spearing, binding, the knife, setting fire, and putting on the fire (col. 22).

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> ʿApep is 'the foe of Rê', a serpent 'who hast neither arms nor legs', who threatens the sun-bark; the rites are done 'that he may not attack the Night-bark' (cols. 23, 29). Each spell declares him destroyed in absolute terms: 'Thou shalt nevermore come forth from this thine hell' (col. 24); 'thou shalt submerge and not emerge again' (col. 32); 'Rê has felled thee for ever' (col. 30). Yet the same text has the destruction repeat without end: 'the Eye of Rê shall have power over thee and it shall consume thee every day' (col. 24); 'thou art given over to the fire every day as Rê commanded' (col. 28); 'Thy head shall be cut off with this knife in the presence of Rê every day' (col. 31); 'the knife shall be stuck fast in his head in the presence of Rê every day' (col. 27). Storms and the 'storm-red' in the eastern sky are treated as ʿApep's activity, answered by the rite 'so that the sun may shine' (col. 23). He is also defeated within the creation account (cols. 26-29), where the creator's offspring 'created the magic spells for felling ʿApep'. In this text chaos is declared beaten in every spell and has to be beaten again every day.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> In the temple of Amun-Ra at Karnak (col. 22 title), a man 'pure and clean' (cols. 24, 26) recites the spells over a figure of ʿApep drawn on a new sheet of papyrus in green ink and a wax image with his name written on it in green ink. The figure is spat on, trampled with the left foot (four times), speared, bound, cut with a knife by the 'warrior priest', and burnt in a fire of bryony; the remains go into a pot of urine and are pounded into one mass (col. 23), or the figures are quenched with a woman's urine (col. 29). At sunrise the officiant tramples ʿApep four times and speaks 'before Rê with thine arms bent when he rises'. A variant puts the drawn figure 'within a box (?)', bound and fettered (col. 28). Wax figures of the king's human enemies, living or dead, with names of their parents and children on their breasts, bound with black thread, are treated the same way and thrown into 'the melting-furnace of the coppersmiths' (col. 26). The names of ʿApep and the king's foes are also written 'in pigment (?) on the ground' and trampled (col. 29). The book is also used 'when the sacred bark ferries over' at Yat-Pega (col. 26).
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strong support. The book is framed as a daily temple service, scheduled by hour and festival, and extended to an on-demand response whenever storm clouds gather. Every spell proclaims final, total destruction ('nevermore', 'for ever and ever'), but the rubrics tell the priest to do it again tomorrow, at the next hour, at the next storm. That gap between the rhetoric of final victory and the practice of endless repetition is the routine-maintenance reading in the text's own words. The complication: the language is triumphalist, not resigned. The text never says chaos cannot be defeated; it keeps claiming it has been, and its 'every day' can be read either as maintenance or as a cosmic re-enactment of one victory.

- Typologically it matches 'Type I' of the burnt New Kingdom fragments from the temple of Thutmose III, but unlike them it is uncut and unburnt: it shows the sheet before cutting, i.e. grid production of curse squares.

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> The sheet names Apophis with two epithets, 'the Roarer' (hmhm.ty) and 'the Terrifying-of-face' (nHA-Hr), the same pair that P. Bremner-Rhind 23.12 tells the officiant to drive off while burning Apophis, and that sit side by side in its list of names (32.16-17). The image is Apophis as a serpent with head turned down (a posture of defeat prescribed in Bremner-Rhind) and cut by a knife. The object itself says nothing about the night journey or final victory; the surrounding article places it in the myth of Re's nightly journey and the recurring battle with Apophis.
- **Prescribed vs attested frequency:** 
> The MMA sheet gives no direct count of performances, but its format (many identical squares on one sheet) implies batch production for repeated use. Prescribed: P. Bremner-Rhind's heading places the ritual at Karnak 'in the course of each day', and 28.16 says the drawing is to be put on the fire 'every day'; other passages give specific hours (sunrise, midday, sunset, etc.). Attested: the c. 40 burnt Thutmose III fragments imply at least twenty occasions, which the authors say is 'not necessarily enough to confirm it as a daily occurrence' but 'still suggests a certain regularity'. The scribe's practised mirror writing is read as a hint he had made similar sheets before.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strong support for the maintenance reading. The object is a production sheet: the same curse, ruled into a grid and copied over and over, ready to be cut into single-use squares and burnt. That is the material form of a repeated, routine task rather than one decisive act, and it fits texts that prescribe the ritual daily. Complication: it is not a 'complete sheet'. It is a 7.5 x 6 cm piece with about five squares preserved per side and broken edges; the Met calls it a fragment. It is also unused, so it shows preparation, not performance, and its date (c. 7th-6th century BCE) is centuries later than the Thutmose III fragments.

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> Apophis (Apep) is the giant chaos serpent, the embodiment of disorder and non-being, and not a god: no cult, no statues (Kockelmann; Morenz's 'anti-god' label points the same way). Every night he tries to stop the sun barque by drinking its water and blocking it with his coils, and he comes back again and again across the night. Defenders: Seth spearing from the prow (BD 108, New Kingdom iconography), and in the Amduat Hour 7 Isis and the Elder Magician (Heka), who disable him with spells while other deities lasso, bind and stab him; in the Book of Gates the Ennead, the Children of Horus and goddesses with nets and knives. Is he ever finally destroyed? No. He is 'destroyed' in scene after scene and his very names call him already cut up, yet he must be 'annihilated again' in the last hour, the knife goddesses punish him 'daily', and the fight carries on through the day hours. Darnell and Darnell state it plainly: 'an ongoing process ... rather than a single event'. No source read here describes a final, permanent end to Apophis.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> Neither source is a ritual manual, but both point to practice on earth. Amduat Hour 7 says the spells of Isis and the Elder Magician against Apep are 'done on earth likewise', and that one who knows the image 'on earth' is one whose water Terrible of Face will not drink (Darnell and Darnell pp. 199-200). Kockelmann notes Apophis's name is often written in red in papyri as a mark of proscription, and that the ritual destruction of the sun's enemy under all his names is set out in the Book of Apophis (late 4th century BC papyrus). The mythic methods (spells, lassoing, binding, knives, spears) mirror the ritual acts of the Bremner-Rhind manual, but the who and where of performance are not covered by these sources.
- **Prescribed vs attested frequency:** 
> Within the myth the combat is nightly and repeated: Kockelmann's 'taglichen Kampf' (daily battle), Darnell and Darnell's punishment of Apep 'daily' (Amduat Hour 7 label) and repeated destruction across several hours of each night, plus the day hours. That is a mythic frequency, not evidence of how often priests performed a ritual. These sources say nothing about attested ritual frequency.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strong support. The scholarly reading matches the essay's angle: chaos is pushed back every night by a crew working together (Isis, Heka, Seth, lasso and knife deities, the Ennead, the Children of Horus), not killed once by a lone hero. Darnell and Darnell's 'ongoing process ... rather than a single event' is a quotable scholarly statement of this. Complications: (1) the texts repeatedly say Apep is 'destroyed' and 'annihilated', so the myth narrates victory each time, just a victory that does not last; (2) Re's own spells and Seth's spear give some heroic individual focus, so 'no single hero' needs care; (3) the Ramesses VI scene where Khepri emerges from Apep's coils suggests chaos is also part of renewal, not purely an enemy, which nuances a simple order-versus-chaos framing; (4) Seth, a defender in the New Kingdom, is himself demonised in the Late Period, so who counts as a maintainer shifts over time.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Kockelmann hedges the etymology: the name 'möglicherweise' (possibly) means 'Great of Rumbling'. Darnell and Darnell flag textual variants: the Seti I tomb and the sarcophagus of Tjaihorpata write 'he being sealed up' rather than 'from Re' (p. 199 n. 103); the word for 'lasso' is misplaced in all versions except Useramun (p. 200 n. 105); 'eye' could be read 'disk' (n. 104). Morenz's own caveats are unknown because the article was not read.
- **Source discrepancies:** 
> (1) Who enchants Apophis in Amduat Hour 7: the Royal Collection Trust page summary (search snippet only) and the Egypt Centre blog summary say 'Isis and Seth'; Darnell and Darnell (p. 11 n. 26) explicitly say the Elder Magician is 'not a hypostasis of Seth' but Heka. Older readings (e.g. Hornung) did equate the figure with Seth, which is the source of the popular version. Safe wording: Isis and the Elder Magician in the Amduat; Seth with his spear in other New Kingdom images and BD 108. (2) Length: Kockelmann gives 120 cubits as typical; Darnell and Darnell attribute the 120 cubits to Ptolemaic texts, while the New Kingdom Amduat gives 440 cubits for his sandbank (not his body). (3) Morenz 2004 dates the Ankhtifi attestation to the 9th Dynasty according to the Egypt Centre blog summary; Kockelmann says only First Intermediate Period. Not checked against Morenz. (4) Kockelmann says Apophis was not a deity, while Darnell and Darnell casually call Apep 'the god Apep' (p. 13). That is a wording difference, not a real disagreement: Darnell and Darnell show no cult either.

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> The 2008 entry names 'The Book of Felling Apophis' (Faulkner's Bremner-Rhind sections) as the example of political and preternatural enemies being tied together: the rite fells the enemies of Ra, Horus and Pharaoh. It treats Bremner-Rhind as the Theban version of the execration ritual and as the best indication of materials (wax figures, fresh papyrus with the name). It cites Faulkner for the idea that those who know creation can eliminate their own enemies, linking execration with re-creation. It says that at least by the Late Period there were daily and cyclical festival execrations, citing Faulkner 1937 (the Apophis book). The 2015 Violence entry calls it 'the ongoing battle between Apophis and the Sun God', a personification of chaos against order, and says destroying such enemies 'represents a restoration of order'. Neither entry claims Apophis is ever finally defeated; neither entry describes the sun's night journey in detail.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> Objects: red pots (earliest, smashed probably with a pestle), papyrus, hairballs, figurines, statues and statuettes of clay, stone, wax or wood, likely live animals, and in at least two cases humans (Mirgissa, Avaris). Wax figures and a fresh papyrus sheet bearing the target's name were among the commonest materials per Bremner-Rhind. Actions: bound, smashed, stomped on, stabbed, cut, speared, spat on, locked in a box, burned, soaked in urine, then almost always buried, sometimes upside down. A quoted rite (Schott 1929: 5): spit four times, trample with the left foot, smite with a spear, slaughter with a knife, place on the fire, spit on him in the fire many times. Each action could need its own rite and a full rite could repeat actions 'numerous times with numerous figures'. Who: state-sponsored performances (standardized texts, foreign geography) and small private rites by individuals; in the Ptolemaic period a specific priestly line performed them for generations in the temple of Montu at Karnak (after Gee). Where: deposits mostly in cemeteries; temple versions at Abydos and Thebes.
- **Prescribed vs attested frequency:** 
> Prescribed: 'at least by the Late Period there were also both daily and cyclical festival execrations (Faulkner 1937: 169, 171, 174)'; other rites 'arose due to specific circumstances'. Attested: over 1,000 deposits, but Muhlestein states that perishable victims (animals, hair, papyrus, wood, wax) are 'largely archaeologically invisible', so the mostly clay remains are 'a vast under-representation of the frequency of the rite'. Private, small-scale rituals are 'far less archaeologically visible'. He gives no count of performances; the gap between daily prescription and surviving evidence is explained by material survival rather than shown to be a gap in practice.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strongly supports the maintenance reading. The rite is proactive and preventive: victims are named for what they 'would in the future' do, and the stated purpose is 'to prevent chaotic forces from acting before they could even begin'. Daily and festival execrations (Late Period) make it a recurring routine, not a one-off triumph. The Violence entry frames the Apophis conflict as an 'ongoing battle' and says ritual slaying 'appears to have remained constant'. It also notes ongoing kinetic violence built into objects (door pivots, chariot wheels, bows) that re-enact defeat with every use. Complication: the rite also served immediate political ends against specific enemies and could involve real human victims, so it is not purely a cosmic housekeeping chore; and much of its frequency is inferred, not counted.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Execration Ritual: perishable materials are 'largely archaeologically invisible, making the known (mostly clay) remains of the ritual a vast under-representation of the frequency of the rite'; 'Private, small-scale rituals are far less archaeologically visible'; 'not every execration included all of the following components'. ### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> Enmarch: Chaos (nun, non-existence) came before the ordered cosmos and 'constantly threatened to overwhelm it again'. Maat (order) and isfet (disorder) map onto good and evil, an opposition already in the Pyramid Texts. Scholars disagree whether the cosmos began perfect and was spoiled, or whether chaotic imperfection was built in from creation; Egyptian sources support both. Cosmogonically, the threat of uncreation, personified by Apophis, is primordial (Apophis as the creator's umbilical cord). From the Middle Kingdom, the state's legitimacy rested on the king acting, sometimes violently, to curb humanity's chaotic tendencies; rebellions, the Hyksos and Amarna were later written up as outbreaks of chaos. Late prophetic texts defer cosmic justice to an unspecified future. Zinn: Egyptian cult expresses cyclical, endlessly repeated creation kept alive by continuous ceremonies. The king, as earthly representative of the sun god and 'Lord of the Rituals', was in theory the only one entitled to perform temple rites; in practice priests acted as his proxies. The divine cult was needed to maintain maat. Each morning (in some temples also midday and evening) the cult statue in the sanctuary was unsealed, unveiled, washed, anointed, clothed, censed and offered food, then resealed. In return the god was hoped to keep maat present so the king could rule. Ritual myth aimed at re-creating the past 'again and again' in cyclical time (neheh).

> Zinn: daily cult 'at least every morning (in some temples also at midday and evening)'; Edfu sanctuary reliefs describe it 'three times a day'. Cult statues 'only about 50 cm tall'; Munich crocodile statue ÄS 6080 is 22 cm long (perhaps 30 cm with tail). King titled 'Lord of the Rituals' (nb iret ikhet) from the 11th Dynasty. ### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> Enmarch names Apophis as a personification of 'the threat of the return to uncreation', strongly implied to be primordial, and notes the tradition that Apophis is the umbilical cord of the solar creator god (citing Quack 2015 and Ritner 2017; Kemboly 2017 disagrees). On final defeat, Enmarch says chaos 'constantly threatened to overwhelm' the cosmos, and that some scholars hold chaotic imperfection was 'an inherent feature of the cosmos from its creation'. Neither source describes a final victory over chaos within the present world. The closest thing to an end point is late prophetic literature that defers cosmic justice 'to an unspecified future date'. Zinn does not mention Apophis or the night journey, but frames the solar cycle 'sunset to sunrise to sunset ... endlessly repeated as an act of ongoing creation'.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> Daily cult image ritual (Zinn): at dawn the officiating priest purified himself in the 'House of the Morning', filled libation vessels, consecrated food, lit a torch 'to keep away enemies', recited texts (a version of Book of the Dead 125), broke the clay seals and opened the shrine with the Morning Hymn, unveiled the statue, prostrated, offered myrrh, washed, anointed and clothed the statue in coloured linen, censed it, closed the shrine, swept away footprints walking backwards, then presented food offerings that were afterwards redistributed among priests (the 'Reversion of Offerings'). Performed in the king's name; actually done by high-ranking priests (hem netjer) as his proxies, in the temple sanctuary. No drawing, wax figures, piercing or burning of enemies is described in either source; those belong to the separate anti-Apophis and execration rites.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Strong support. Enmarch: chaos predates creation and 'constantly threatened to overwhelm it again'; Egyptologists debate whether disorder was built into creation itself. Zinn: order was 'kept alive through continuous religious ceremonies', endlessly repeated, on the king's behalf, every morning, with myth enacted 'again and again'. Together these give the scholarly basis for maat as something maintained by routine rather than won once. Complication: Enmarch notes the king's maintenance of order could involve 'strong and sometimes violent action', and late prophetic texts imagine a future restoration, so the maintenance picture is not the only Egyptian frame. Zinn's daily ritual is care and feeding of the god, not combat; the link to fighting chaos is indirect (the torch 'to keep away enemies', and the god keeping maat present in return).

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Enmarch: 'To some degree, support for both viewpoints can be found in discrete Egyptian textual sources', and the Egyptians 'accepted differing explanations of a single phenomenon as equally valid' (Allen 2004). He also states that a closer link with later theodicy traditions 'is not currently possible to conclusively demonstrate'. Zinn: 'None of the sources describe the whole ritual'; the Edfu reliefs are 'unluckily incomplete, due to lack of space'; the king was 'theoretically' the only one able to perform the rites.
- **Source discrepancies:** 
> Frequency: Zinn gives 'at least every morning' generally, but the Edfu reliefs specify three times daily; other summaries (e.g. a secondary site citing the UEE) give 'about sixty' ceremonies at Thebes vs 'thirty-six' at Abydos, and Digital Karnak speaks of thirty-six actions; Zinn's own list has 12 steps (a grouping, not a count of episodes). Origin of disorder: Enmarch notes Apophis as primordial vs disorder arriving with Seth in the fourth generation of the Ennead. The often-quoted 'King as Sun-Priest' text (Re placed the king on earth to realise maat and drive out isfet) could not be checked against a primary edition or Assmann's translation; it was found only on an unreliable blog and is not used here.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Innovation is the dominant ideology of the era and is radically overvalued; what happens after innovation matters more.
- Maintenance, repair and infrastructure upkeep have more impact on daily life than most technological innovations.
- 'Innovation' rose in the late 1960s as a smaller, morally neutral substitute for faith in 'progress', then became a buzzword, a fetish and finally a slogan exposed as hucksterism.
- Three correctives: technology is not innovation (most technology in use is old, citing David Edgerton's Shock of the Old); recognise basic infrastructure; recognise that the central fact of industrial civilisation is labour, mostly outside innovation.
- Inventors and innovators are perhaps around one per cent of the workforce; most labour is upkeep (laundry, trash removal, janitorial work, food preparation).
- Maintenance means attending to 'the constant processes of entropy and un-doing' (Steven Jackson's 'broken world thinking') and 'the work we do to slow or halt them'. The framing is slowing decay, not ending it.
- Maintenance work is disproportionately done by women and by low-wage and minority workers; the innovation focus obscures it (Ruth Schwartz Cowan, More Work for Mother, 1983).
- Warning: swapping innovators for maintainers as the new heroes misses the point; hero-worship itself is part of the problem.
- Innovation is value-agnostic (their example: crack cocaine in the 1980s); emphasising maintenance moves the conversation from means to ends and asks who benefits.
- **Headline figures:** 
- 'perhaps somewhere around one per cent' : share of the workforce who are inventors and innovators. Offered as an estimate, no source given.
- 'more than 90 times' : uses of the word 'innovation' in Richard Florida's The Rise of the Creative Class (2002).
- 'more than 250 books with “innovation” in the title… published in the last three months' : Wall Street Journal count, 2012.
- '78 times' : appearances of 'infrastructure' on the programme of the American Anthropological Association annual meeting (2015).
- 'More than 40 scholars' : responses to The Maintainers conference call for papers.
- 'between 1870 and 1940' : Robert Gordon's period of unprecedented US growth.
- Dates: Kitchen Debate 1959; Cowan's More Work for Mother 1983; Christensen's The Innovator's Dilemma 1997; Bierut essay 2005; Economist 2006; Nussbaum 31 Dec 2008; San Francisco Google bus protests 2013; infrastructure bill approved December 2015.
- **Dating and context:** 
> Not applicable (a 2016 magazine essay, not archaeological material). Context: written to accompany The Maintainers, the conference and network Russell and Vinsel founded (the essay says they organised a conference; it does not give the venue or date in the text).

### Myth and ritual

- **What it says about apophis:** 
> Nothing. The essay does not mention Apophis, Egypt, Ra, myth or ritual. Its only conceptual bridge is the phrase 'the constant processes of entropy and un-doing ... and the work we do to slow or halt them', which parallels a cosmos where chaos is repelled nightly and never finally destroyed. Any link to Apophis is the essayist's own analogy, not Russell and Vinsel's.
- **Ritual practice:** 
> Not applicable. No ritual is discussed. The nearest equivalents are mundane upkeep tasks the authors list (laundry, trash removal, janitorial work, food preparation, guards on graveyard shifts, staff who repair fences and ID card-readers).
- **Prescribed vs attested frequency:** 
> Not applicable. The essay gives no frequencies for any maintenance work; it describes upkeep as 'ceaseless' (of domestic labour, via Cowan) and entropy as 'constant'.
- **Relevance to angle:** 
> Supports: the essay is the standard modern statement that keeping things going matters more than novelty, that decay is constant and work only slows it, that maintainers are mostly invisible, and that most of a workforce does upkeep, not invention. It fits 'chaos held off by routine work repeated' and the link to the heroic-developer essay. Complicates: (1) Russell and Vinsel explicitly warn against 'hero-worship that merely changes the cast of heroes', so the essay should not recast priests as heroic maintainers. (2) Where the analogy to Egyptian ritual breaks: their maintainers fix real physical decay (bridges, trains, fences) with measurable effects, whereas the Apophis ritual is symbolic action against a mythic threat whose efficacy cannot be shown; Egyptian ritual was elite temple work by literate priests, while Russell and Vinsel stress low-status, low-wage, often female or minority labour; the Egyptian ritual was performed to sustain an order (maat) regarded as given and good, while the essay's point is to ask 'who benefits, to what end?' of the systems being kept up; and their argument is a critique of capitalism's innovation ideology, which has no Egyptian counterpart. (3) The Egyptian case actually strengthens one point: the ritual of destroying Apophis is repeated precisely because the victory never sticks, matching 'slow or halt' rather than 'solve'. These breakpoints are the researcher's analysis, not claims in the source.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> The authors flag 'the danger of moving too triumphantly from innovation to maintenance' and that 'There is no point in keeping the practice of hero-worship that merely changes the cast of heroes'. They concede one argument 'Perhaps this point is cynical' (the crack cocaine example). They also criticise an over-correction toward object fetishism ('an equally superficial fascination with golf balls, refrigerators, and remote controls'). The one-per-cent figure is hedged ('perhaps somewhere around') and unsourced.
- **Source discrepancies:** 
> The page title is 'Hail the maintainers'; the URL slug and some citations use 'Innovation is overvalued. Maintenance often matters more'. Both refer to the same essay. The essay repeats the Wall Street Journal consultant quote twice (once as a pull quote). No conflicts with the Egyptological sources, since it makes no claims about Egypt.
