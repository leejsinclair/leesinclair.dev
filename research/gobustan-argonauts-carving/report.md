### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Boats are among the most recurrent themes at Gobustan and were engraved 'over a long span of time', changing style 'possibly according to new ideologies'. The paper calls its own basis 'preliminary studies'.
- Corpus studied: nine rocks with boats. Eight on Böyük Daş (upper terrace: Rocks 29, 30, 765A, 41, 42 and two small unnumbered rocks treated as one; lower terrace: Rock 8) and one on Kiçik Daş (Rock 19). Fieldwork July 2015. This is a sample, not a census of Gobustan boats: the author says he could not reach unpublished rocks.
- Boat typology. Long boats over 1.50 m on Rocks 29, 30, 765A and 41; shorter boats on Rocks 42, 8 and the unnumbered pair. Keels flat (Rock 29 north face east section, Rock 41) or curved (Rock 8). Crew shown as small vertical strokes (Rocks 29, 30, 765A, 41, 42) or as X-shaped elements (unnumbered rocks, Rock 8). Prows hooked; sun-like symbols at the end of the prow line or made by curving the prow and adding rays. Natural cracks in the rock are sometimes used as part of the boat (Rocks 29, 8, unnumbered).
- Rock 29 is on the upper terrace of Böyük Daş, forms the southern side of Ana Zaga cave (the cave is made by Rocks 29, 30 and 31 set against each other) and is 'probably in its original position'.
- Rock 29 south face: one boat, built on a natural curved crack that serves as the keel, prow pointing east towards the Caspian with a sun-shaped symbol. It overlaps a zoomorph and two male anthropomorphs, so on this panel the boat was cut after those figures.
- Rock 29 north face: a long boat about 1.60 m in the eastern section, and three boats in three rows in the western section, all using natural curved fractures. The long boat is overlain by several mainly anthropomorphic figures and is older than the aurochs that Anati assigned to phase II/B.
- The three western boats on the north face are overlain by a stylised male anthropomorph with a thin body and S-shaped legs that Anati put in a Holocene horizon (Phase III or IV), so the boats are older than that figure. They 'may appear slightly more recent than the one on southern surface'.
- Three rocks are singled out as palimpsests that can order the boats: Rock 29, Rock 8 and Rock 19.
- Rock 8 (lower terrace, current altitude 0 m asl): Djafarsade recorded 24 boats and eight female silhouettes. Boats are more curved, with sun symbols and hooked profiles, and recur beside aurochs. Female figures sit close to boats but never overlap them; male figures can lie under boats.
- Rock 19, Kiçik Daş (20 to 30 m asl; the 'Firuz' site): around 34 boats in different styles, the number uncertain because the palimpsest is hard to read. Three have a sun-shaped prow and one an X-shaped crew.
- Proposed relative chronology: the first boat images are those on the upper terraces of both hills, dated to the deglaciation of 16 to 14 ka 'or even before'. The boats on Rock 8 date to 14 to 12 ka cal. BP 'or to a later one'. New styles then developed and spread back up to the upper terraces, bringing the sun symbol and the X-shaped crew.
- Earlier chronologies are treated as unreliable. Anati's is used only as a starting point because his own tracing of Rock 29 contradicts his phase order. The published radiocarbon dates from the deposits are set aside because their context is unpublished.
- Holes in rocks on the terraces were 'probably used to dock the boats'. The terraces are 'possibly' ancient beaches.
- The paper says the stories told by the boat figures 'can never be deciphered'.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> The author's own July 2015 field recording of nine rocks: 3D photogrammetry on Böyük Daş, photographs plus existing and new tracings for Rock 19. Tracings were drawn on photographs. Unreached rocks are covered from published tracings (Djafarsade 1973, Rustamov 2006, Farajova 2009, 2011, 2012). The chronology rests on three things: differences in boat style, the altitude and terrace of each rock, and which figure lies over which. These are then matched to a Caspian sea-level curve taken from other authors (chiefly Arslanov et al. 2015, with Levchenko and Roslyakov 2010 for the roughly 50 m maximum). Counts for Rock 8 (24 boats, eight female figures) come from Djafarsade and for Rock 19 (34 boats) from Farajova 2011. No new dates, no excavation, no sampling. No total count of boats on Rock 29 or across Böyük Daş is given.

- **Dating method:** 
> Relative only. Style, superimposition on palimpsest panels, and altitude of the rock matched to Caspian shorelines dated by other researchers (14C and 230Th/234U on Khvalynian deposits, Arslanov et al. 2015). The logic for the lower terrace is a terminus post quem: a rock at 0 m asl cannot have been carved while under water. The paper takes no samples and dates no engraving directly. It rejects the two radiocarbon dates from the deposits as a basis for interpretation because the archaeological context is unpublished, and notes that it 'may be difficult to obtain samples' for any panel. It also admits the Caspian chronology itself 'is still ongoing' as a debate.
- **Panel details:** 
> Rock No. 29, Böyük Daş, upper terrace; forms the southern side of Ana Zaga cave, which is made by Rocks 29, 30 and 31 set together. Judged 'probably in its original position' on three grounds: all engravings share one orientation, figures run below the soil on both south and north sides, and the south face is partly covered by another boulder. Engraved on three surfaces: south, east and north. South face (Fig. 2): one boat using a natural curved crack as its keel, prow to the east towards the Caspian, sun-shaped symbol on the prow; it overlaps one zoomorph and two male anthropomorphs. The engraving is partly eroded by toothpaste that earlier researchers used to highlight it. North face, eastern section (Fig. 3): one long boat about 1.60 m, flat keel, crew as short vertical strokes; juxtaposed with, over and under other figures; overlain by several mainly anthropomorphic figures; older than an aurochs. North face, western section: three boats in three rows on natural curved fractures, possibly enhanced by blue clay in the rock; the central boat is overlain by a thin-bodied male figure with S-shaped legs; surface heavily eroded. That makes five boats described on Rock 29 (one south, four north). The paper does not say this is the full count. The east face is engraved but its content is not described. The paper does not mention wavy lines, water marks, armed figures, warriors or hunters on Rock 29.

> The Caspian Sea only. Gobustan is placed on the west shore of the Caspian, 60 km south of Baku, at the eastern end of the Greater Caucasus. At the Early Khvalynian high stand Böyük Daş and Kiçik Daş 'appeared as two islands', and their terraces are 'possibly' old beaches where the first inhabitants landed. ### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- Against a Bronze Age date for the Rock 29 boats: the only specialist study of this boat corpus puts the upper-terrace boats in the late Pleistocene (16 to 14 ka or earlier) and never mentions the Bronze Age.
- The scheme does leave a gap that a later date could use. The sun-prow type is given no absolute date, and the Rock 8 window is '14 to 12 ka cal. BP ... or to a later one' with no ceiling. The paper does not rule out a much later sun-prow boat; it simply offers no evidence for one.
- Against treating boat and human figures on the south face as one scene: the boat overlaps the two male figures and the zoomorph. It was cut across them afterwards. The paper describes the relation as superimposition, not as a composition.
- Against any narrative reading: the author's own conclusion is that the stories 'can never be deciphered'.
- Against firm dates of any kind: the chronology is called preliminary, is phrased as 'we can suggest', depends on a sea-level curve the paper says is still debated, and has no directly dated engraving behind it.
- Internal tension over the south-face boat. It carries the sun symbol, which the conclusion treats as a late introduction to the upper terrace, yet section 4 says it 'may belong to a previous phase' relative to the three western boats on the north face. The paper does not resolve this.
- Internal inconsistency on Rock 8: sun symbols are placed 'above the sterns' there, while the general typology and the Rock 29 description put the sun on the prow.
- The sample is small (nine rocks) and partly second-hand, built on older tracings whose reliability the paper itself questions in Anati's case.
- Disagreement with the 2025 team paper on one point: here the boulder covering part of the south face is evidence that Rock 29 has not moved; Sigari et al. 2025 describe Ana Zaga as 'displaced limestone boulders' and a slab fallen in a catastrophic event.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> The paper's own reading is environmental and local: the boats record a long dependence of Gobustan communities on the Caspian, carved on or above former beaches, with fishing implied by net-making tools in the Firuz burial and by pierced mooring stones. Style change is put down to 'new ideologies', left unspecified. The sun symbol is described as a decorative feature and given no meaning. Competing chronologies are reported: Djafarsade's six styles in two phases; Anati's reordering into phases I/A to II/C, which places the Rock 29 boat later than the aurochs that actually overlies it; Rustamov's separate stylistic sequences for anthropomorphs, zoomorphs and boats; Farajova's six phases with most ships in the Upper Palaeolithic to Mesolithic first phase. The paper offers no ritual, funerary or solar-boat interpretation and does not discuss Scandinavian parallels.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Counts: 'more than 7,000 petroglyphs on over 1,000 rocks' (abstract). - Chronological span of the rock art: Upper Palaeolithic to historical times (the latest inscriptions are in Cyrillic).
- Eleven thematic groups: aurochs, horse, boats, feminine silhouette, warrior-hunter, ibex, camel, tamgas, deer, rider, inscription.
- Rocks were reused over a very long period, producing palimpsests. Motifs were added to, reused or modified. Rocks 29 and 30 are named as the two main examples of dense palimpsest.

### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- Against a Bronze Age date for the Rock 29 boats: the specialists' own sequence puts simple boats on the upper terraces at 16,000-14,000 years ago and the sun-prow style from 14,000-12,000 years ago 'or later'. Nothing in the paper dates a Rock 29 boat to the Bronze Age.
- Against any firm date at all: the authors say the radiocarbon series has no established link to the engravings and that chronology must rest on typology. Their own boat dates are hedged ('would date', 'at first sight, it seems', 'or to a later one').
- Against reading boat, warriors and other marks as one scene: Rock 29 is described twice as a dense palimpsest built up over a long period. The paper says superimposed figures 'may be the result of diachronicity', and that only juxtaposition 'can express contemporaneity and possibly a narrative'. It never says the boat and the hunter-warriors on the south side were carved together.
- Hunter-warriors are grouped with the 'most ancient phases', not with the Bronze Age, which cuts against reading the armed figures as Bronze Age heroes.
- The Bronze Age repertoire at Gobustan is described as ibex, deer and anthropomorphs made by transhumant pastoral groups. Boats are not among the frequent Bronze Age themes.
- The existing stylistic chronologies contradict each other, and Rock 29 is the panel used to show that Anati's scheme fails.
- Internal inconsistency in the paper: section 2.1 gives the 14,000-12,000 year rises as reaching '0 and -12 metres', section 5 gives '0 and 12 metres usl'. Read together they mean 0 m and 12 m below sea level.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> The paper's own reading of the boats is local and maritime: they record a long relationship between Gobustan's settlers and the Caspian (fishing, net-weaving tools, mooring stones), carved on what were then beaches, with style changing over time 'possibly according to new ideologies'. Some rock art is tied to burial and afterlife ritual (the Firuz shelter, whose rock is engraved with boats, anthropomorphs and zoomorphs and which held a multiple burial). The sun symbol on the prow is noted as a recurrent decorative feature and is not interpreted further. Farajova is cited for a solar and fertility reading of ibex horns, not of boats. Other chronologies give different orderings: Rustamov has boats evolving by shape alongside anthropomorphs ordered from smallest to largest; Farajova has six phases with the Bronze Age as phase four. The Scandinavian comparison is reported as a proposal only; Heyerdahl is not named.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Dating at Gobustan is unsettled. The 2021 paper opens by calling the question still 'controversial' (Russian: 'diskussionnoy'), and the 2011 article says the data leave 'the question of their absolute dating open'.
- Radiocarbon series. More than 40 calibrated AMS dates (2021) from about 50 samples (2023) were obtained for cultural levels of the Gobustan shelters. Samples from Ana Zaga, Okuzler, Ovchular, Maral and Daire (Boyukdash) and Gaya Arasi and Firuz 2 (Kichikdash) went to a Ukrainian laboratory (January 2010), the University of Waikato (April 2010, February 2011) and Beta Analytic (September 2011, July 2014). Neither paper tabulates the full series; only a handful of dates are given.
- Oldest date: Gaya Arasi shelter, Kichikdash, level 350 cm, 'approximately 13,610 BP (calibrated)'. The lab report shown (Beta-305145) is 12200+/-50 BP on shell, reservoir-corrected, 2-sigma Cal BP 13660-13400. Farajova herself calls it 'the only' date from that level and says repeat analyses on all levels are needed before the age of the site can be stated.
- Ana Zaga dates as published: level 270 cm, Beta-305140, 9170+/-40 BP on bone collagen, 2-sigma Cal BP 10480-10460 and 10430-10240 (described in the abstract as 'approximately 10,600'); level 255-270 cm, 'test' date Cal BP 7500-7420 (2023 gives the uncalibrated value 6530+/-40 BP); level 185 cm, preliminary 'calibrated date of 9,029 BC' (2013 summary). The 2023 conclusion lists five clusters for Ana Zaga running from 9170+/-40 BP to 1560+/-55 BP and reads them as occupation 'from the Early Mesolithic to the Middle Ages'.
- Ana Zaga stratigraphy: the shelter is formed by near-vertical limestone blocks up to 15 m high and up to 5 m apart, on the upper terrace of Boyukdash at about 130 m a.s.l. Mesolithic, Neolithic and Chalcolithic cultural layers lie in the central and southern parts at the foot of stone 30. The lowest layers, at 5, 6 and 7 m depth, are 'sediments of Pleistocene marine basins'. Excavated in 1977 and again in 2009 to 400 cm. Faunal count (2011): of 10,000 bones, 40% wild horse and 40% gazelle.
- Method claim: loose carved stones and spalled wall fragments lie in the dated layers. Because a buried carved stone must be as old as or older than its layer, and because wall figures in the same style and technique are taken to be the same age, the layer dates are transferred to the wall panels.
- Applied to Stone 29: (a) a spalled fragment from Ana Zaga at 255-270 cm (inv. 2418) with a sunken-relief human figure is matched to hunters on the north-west side of stone 29 (figures 4, 5, 56, 57); (b) a loose stone with wavy lines (inv. 2453, found 1977 at 255-290 cm) is matched to the zigzag and wavy lines on the south side of stone 29, and the 270 cm date of Cal BP 10430-10240 is offered for them and for figures linked to them.
- Farajova's own period chart for the south side of Stone 29 (2021, Fig. 15) splits that single face across five periods: Mesolithic stage 2, Neolithic, Eneolithic, Bronze Age and one Iron Age mark. The long boat with the rayed prow is drawn in the Neolithic colour (9000-7800 BP). Only a few small human figures at the left edge and bottom are in the Bronze Age colour.
- Boats: 'the most ancient means of transportation for the inhabitants of Gobustan were boats depicted 7800 years ago, which could accommodate more than 50 people' (2023). Boat images are also reported on loose stones in the Gaya Arasi and Firuz 2 Mesolithic layers, and archaeologists are said to regard the Gaya Arasi boats as among the oldest boat images in the world.
- Sea level: the Khvalynian transgression is put at about 15-12 ka BP, ending 9-7 ka BP. At 14 ka BP the Caspian stood 22 m above ocean level; at 14-12 ka between 0 and -12 m; a transgression at 10 ka; the New Caspian regression from 8 ka; a rise to +25 m at 6 ka and +23 m at 4 ka; regression from 4 to 2 ka. The gap between the Gaya Arasi date (about 13.6 ka) and the first Ana Zaga date (about 10.3-10.5 ka) is attributed to the sea washing the mountains.
- Settlement sequence: late Upper Palaeolithic and Mesolithic occupation on the upper terraces; caves still the main habitation in the Neolithic and Eneolithic 'with the rise' of the sea; middle and lower terraces settled in the Bronze Age when the sea fell, with round settlements and kurgan burials at the foot of the mountains and a shift of rock art themes to bezoar goats with large curved horns, carved by cattle breeders.
- Periodisation (2021 conclusions): I, oldest, 12th millennium BC; II, Epipalaeolithic-Mesolithic, 12th-8th millennia BC; III, Neolithic, 7th-6th millennia BC; IV, Eneolithic, 5th-4th millennia BC; V, Bronze Age, end of 4th to 2nd millennium BC; VI, Early Iron Age, end of 2nd to early 1st millennium BC; VII, Middle Ages and Modern period.
- Stone 29 is described as the place where the succession of periods shows most clearly, on its north side at the entrance to Ana Zaga. Farajova says the north-side figures of aurochs, women and boats 'harmonize with each other and form unified plots'.
- None of the four sources mentions Jason, the Argonauts, Colchis, the Phasis or any Greek myth. The 2021 paper asks who the 'seafarers of Gobustan' were and how far they could travel, and leaves the question unanswered.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> Radiocarbon dates on bone, charcoal and shell from cultural layers in the shelters, with three lab calibration reports reproduced as figures (Beta-305140, Beta-305145, Wk-30004). Loose carved stones and wall spalls recovered from those layers in the 1970s excavations (inventory numbers 2418, 2453, 2454 from Ana Zaga; 1479, 1587 from Kyaniza; 791 from Firuz 2; stones 9A and 9B from Gaya Arasi), compared by eye with wall figures for style and technique. Superimposition order on panels. Extinct fauna in the layers and on the rocks. A Caspian sea-level chronology taken from Svitoch 2006, Arslanov et al. 2016, Mamedov 1997 and Yanko-Hombach et al. 2007. New 3D recording (Agisoft, 3D Studio Max) used to separate overlapping figures and colour them by period. No carving is dated directly. The papers give no excavation sections, no table of all dates, and no sample-by-sample context.

- **Dating method:** 
> Radiocarbon of cultural layers, then stratigraphic association, then style. Step one dates a layer (bone, charcoal or shell). Step two treats carved loose stones or wall spalls found in that layer as no younger than the layer. Step three extends the date to wall figures judged the same in style and technique, and then to other figures on the panel judged to belong to the same scene. Shoreline correlation is a supporting argument: the oldest art is said to sit on the ancient coast and coastal dates are said to give maximum ages. Superimposition gives relative order. Farajova states the limits herself: a tradition can persist for a millennium, figures in one style can differ by 1000 years (her tattooed-figure example), and single dates need repeating.
- **Geographic claims:** 
> Caspian only. The 2021 and 2023 papers describe the Khvalynian (palaeo-Caspian) sea washing Boyukdash, Kichikdash and Jingirdag, and place the earliest art on the ancient Caspian coast. Comparisons are drawn along the Caspian shore: Mesolithic sites of the North Caucasus (Chokh and others in Daghestan) and northern Iran (Ali Tappeh, Hotu, Kamarband). The 2011 article says that during the Khvalynian transgression 'the waters of the Mediterranean Sea reached the Caspian Sea through the Black Sea, i.e. via Manych Strait', a late Pleistocene geological statement citing Vereshchagin 1959 and Kovalevsky 1933 and used to date a dolphin carving. No river route, no Black Sea voyage and no Bronze Age sea contact is asserted. The question of how far Gobustan's 'seafarers' travelled is asked and left open.

### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- Against a Bronze Age boat on Stone 29: Farajova's own chart puts the sun-prow boat in the Neolithic (9000-7800 BP) and her text says boats were 'depicted 7800 years ago'. That is four to five thousand years before any Bronze Age date, and far earlier than any Greek tradition.
- Against one scene: her Fig. 15 divides the south face among five periods, so the boat, the large hunters, the wavy lines and the small Bronze Age figures are not contemporaries in her reading.
- Against water at the rock in the Bronze Age: in her account the sea had fallen by then and people had moved down to the middle and lower terraces. Her highest Holocene level is +25 m, about 100 m below Ana Zaga at 130 m.
- The link between dated layer and wall figure is by visual resemblance. No section drawings, sample contexts or full date list are published in these papers, so the association cannot be checked by a reader.
- The Ana Zaga dates do not form a simple depth sequence as published: 185 cm gives roughly 9000 BP or '9,029 BC', 255-270 cm gives 6530+/-40 BP, and 270 cm gives 9170+/-40 BP. The papers do not explain this. Different trenches or excavation datums (1977 and 2009) may account for it, but that is not stated.
- Reporting slips: the same lab report (Beta-305140) is captioned 270 cm in Fig. 7 and 270-290 cm in Fig. 11; the Ana Zaga date is called 'approximately 10,600' though the report's oldest 2-sigma bound is 10,480 cal BP; 7698+/-33 BP is called calibrated but is the conventional age; the abstract announces 9 trends and the conclusions list 7 periods; the wavy lines are tied to about 10,300 cal BP in the text but coloured 9700-9000 BP in the chart.
- The oldest date is on shell with a marine reservoir correction, from one sample, and Farajova says it needs repeating. She also reports that N. Museibli (2017) rejects Rustamov and Muradova's Upper Palaeolithic attribution of the Gaya Arasi carved stones and calls for new excavations.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> Farajova's reading of the same imagery is local and prehistoric. Boats are the transport of Mesolithic and Neolithic coastal hunter-fishers on a Caspian shoreline. Aurochs and female figures belong to totemic and fertility belief; she links the aurochs cult to the epic of the Oghuz Turkic tribes. The lines running from cup marks down through the hunters on the east side of Stone 29 are described alongside a figure in 'ritual clothing or in a state of trance' with a mushroom-shaped headdress compared to Mongolian, Altai and Tuvan rock art, which is a ritual reading and makes no reference to water. Wavy and zigzag lines are treated as a datable style element. Bronze Age art is the bezoar goats, deer drives, carts and riders of pastoralists. Jafarzade read a boat with two hunters on Stone 35 as 2nd millennium BC and the nearby objects as animal traps; Farajova re-reads part of that stone as a settlement plan.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the excavator-side account of the very rock the proposal uses, and it gives the dates the proposal would have to overturn. Farajova places the sun-prow boat on the south side of Stone 29 in the Neolithic, says boats were carved about 7800 years ago, splits that face across five periods, and has the sea falling away from the upper terrace by the Bronze Age. ### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Inscription: inscribed in 2007 at the 31st session of the World Heritage Committee (Christchurch) by Decision 31 COM 8B.49, under criterion (iii) only. Dossier number 1076rev. Serial property of three areas: Jinghirdagh Mountain-Yazylytepe hill, Boyukdash Mountain, Kichikdash Mountain.
- Size: property 537.22 ha, buffer zone 3,096.34 ha, inside a wider Gobustan Reservation of 'some 4,000 ha' (ICOMOS).
- Counts: UNESCO says 'more than 6,000 rock engravings'. ICOMOS says '1,000 known rocks with carvings' holding 'over 6,000 separate images'. The dossier adds engravings on 104 small stones found in archaeological levels, and that Jafarzade recorded more than 3,500 images on about 750 rocks from 1947.
- Time span: UNESCO's description says the engravings bear 'testimony to 40,000 years of rock art' and that use ran 'from the Upper Paleolithic to the Middle Ages'. The 40,000-year figure originates with the State Party; ICOMOS reports it as the State Party's justification and does not endorse a Palaeolithic date.
- Nomination history: first nomination deferred in 2004 (28 COM 14B.37) for lack of research. In 2007 ICOMOS recommended referral back to the State Party, and rejected criteria (ii) and (vi) as unproven. The Committee inscribed anyway under (iii) and suggested a later renomination under (vi).
- Boats, per ICOMOS (summarising the dossier): boats are among the images 'suggested' to be earliest; they 'are like canoes with people paddling', some for 2-4 people, others for 'around forty people' with a sun motif on the prow. Their prevalence is explained by a higher Caspian that made the three hills 'virtually islands'.
- Boats, per the State Party dossier: boats are evidence of regular long voyages when the Caspian was connected to the Black Sea; the oldest are linear boats for 2-4 people 'dated by Mesolithic period'; larger sun-bearing boats held up to 40; Gobustan boats are older than Egyptian and Saharan parallels and are forerunners of boats shown elsewhere.
- UNESCO's own Statement of OUV does not mention boats at all. It speaks of 'hunting and fishing' and a warmer, wetter climate. The Japanese-language description on the list page does list boats among the motifs.
- No official UNESCO or ICOMOS text read mentions Jason, the Argonauts, Colchis, a golden fleece or any Greek myth.
- Roman inscription: the ICOMOS evaluation and the UNESCO list page do not mention it. The dossier refers to 'the famous Roman inscription' or 'the Latin one, on the foothill of Boyukdash', near the prison gate, and cites Pakhomov 1949 on a first-century AD inscription of the 12th legion. It treats the inscription as marking the end of the rock art tradition.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> UNESCO and ICOMOS did no dating of their own; their descriptions restate the State Party dossier, usually behind 'it is suggested'. The dossier rests on: stylistic periodisation after Leroi-Gourhan (1965) and Jafarzade (1973); engraved stones found inside excavated cultural layers at shelters such as Ana-zaga, Kaniza, Okuzlar-2, Gaya-arasi and Firuz; stylistic match between those stones and wall images; Caspian transgression and regression levels; pollen from Ana-zaga hearths; stylistic comparison with Siberian, Mongolian, European and Saharan rock art. No radiocarbon dates are quoted on the pages read.
- **Date range estimates:**

- Neither UNESCO, ICOMOS nor the dossier pages read assign any boat image to the Bronze Age. In the dossier's Bronze Age group the motifs are deer, goats, carriages and riders.; site_and_layers: - UNESCO: '40,000 years of rock art'; use 'from the Upper Paleolithic to the Middle Ages'.
- ICOMOS: one cave excavation gave 'a 2 m stratigraphy covering 10,000 years'.
- Dossier: Upper Palaeolithic shelters sit at about 127 m above world ocean level; Bronze Age settlements (Daire-1, Daire-2) and burial mounds are on the lower terraces; last Caspian transgression about 1000 BC.
- Latest material: Persian inscription of the 13th-14th century (stone N 91, Yazili hill); medieval camel caravans and Islamic motifs.
- **Dating method:** 
> Relative and indirect only. (1) Stylistic periodisation into six groups. (2) Stratigraphic association: engraved stones found within dated cultural layers, cultural layers partly overlapping engraved panels, and one fallen engraved fragment giving a terminus ante quem for one anthropomorph. (3) Shoreline correlation: Caspian (Khvalynian) transgressions and regressions, with some images found below sea-sand level. (4) Comparative style and palaeoenvironment (fauna depicted, pollen). ICOMOS's verdict on the result: 'A well-analysed and dated sequence for images on the property is yet to be achieved'. No direct dating of any engraving is reported, and no radiocarbon dates appear in the UNESCO or ICOMOS texts.
- **Geographic claims:** 
> Caspian Sea only, as a real body of water at the foot of the site. ICOMOS: the property is 'approximately 65 km south of Baku and 6 km inland from the coast'; people 'fished in the greater Caspian Sea, then linked to the Aral and Black Seas'; a higher Caspian probably made the three hills 'virtually islands'. The dossier goes further: because the Caspian 'was connected with Black Sea, ancient people had more possibilities to make long voyages from different parts of Europe', and the Khvalynian sea reached Mingachevir, 300 km west of Baku. This sea link is placed in the Upper Pleistocene and early Holocene, the Palaeolithic and Mesolithic, not the Bronze Age: the dossier says later Holocene transgressions did not go 'outside from the modern frontiers'. No river route, no Phasis, Kura or Colchis, and no Greek voyage is asserted.

### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- The official record contains no Bronze Age boat dating. Where boats are dated at all, the State Party puts them among the earliest images (Mesolithic or older), thousands of years before any Bronze Age setting for the Argonaut story.
- ICOMOS says the sequence is not established: 'A well-analysed and dated sequence for images on the property is yet to be achieved', and it is 'not possible ... to say with certainty that Palaeolithic images exist'. Any confident date for one boat panel, Bronze Age or otherwise, goes beyond what the evaluators accepted.
- The Caspian to Black Sea water connection that the dossier invokes belongs to the Khvalynian high stand in the late Pleistocene. By the dossier's own account it was gone long before the Bronze Age, so it cannot supply a sailing route for a Bronze Age Argo.
- The dossier states that rocks carry superimposed drawings from different periods, so motifs on one rock need not be one scene.
- ICOMOS rejected criterion (ii), interchange of human values, because too little is known of the context. A claim of long-range contact with the Aegean has no support in the evaluation.
- UNESCO's headline figures are the State Party's. '40,000 years' is given in the English, French, Spanish and Dutch descriptions, while the Chinese and Russian descriptions on the same page give 4,000 years, so even the official page is not internally consistent.
- **Independent corroboration:** 
> For the Argonaut reading: none found in any UNESCO, ICOMOS or State Party document. For early boats and a higher Caspian: ICOMOS repeats these as the dossier's suggestions and corroborates neither. ICOMOS does independently accept that the engravings show hunting and fishing in a warmer, wetter period (criterion iii).
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> The institutional reading is local and environmental. Boats and fish are common because the sea once stood higher and the hills were nearly islands, and people there fished. UNESCO's criterion (iii) text frames the art as a record of 'hunting and fishing' and of a vanished way of life. The dossier adds ritual readings for other motifs (sun signs, ox and goat images at sanctuaries, sacrifice) and compares sun boats with Mesopotamian, Egyptian and Saharan imagery, and boat shapes with Norway and Sweden. No text reads any panel as a narrative of a particular voyage.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the baseline for what the authorities responsible for the site say, and it cuts against the proposal on dating. The official record never ties a boat to the Bronze Age or to any myth, puts boats among the earliest images, and admits through ICOMOS that no dated sequence exists. It also shows where the Caspian to Black Sea idea comes from: a late Pleistocene sea link in the State Party dossier, which a careless reader could carry forward into the Bronze Age. The Roman inscription is the one securely historical text at the site, and it is first century AD, written by a soldier with no reference to the carvings.
- **Open questions:** 
- What is carved on stone 29, and does the dossier's plate section or Jafarzade's 1973 catalogue describe it? Not established from text-searchable pages.
- The exact Latin text, standard epigraphic reference and discovery date of the Roman inscription need an epigraphic database (EDH or EDCS) or Pakhomov 1949.
- Whether the dossier's 'VII-V year B.C.' style dates mean millennia. Almost certainly yes, but the text never says so.
- ICOMOS writes sun motifs on the 'prow'; the dossier it summarises says 'stern'. Which end is meant is unresolved here.
- Whether Azerbaijan ever renominated under criterion (vi), and whether the documentation ICOMOS asked for has since produced a dated sequence. The 2014 and 2024 periodic reports were not read in full.
- Why the Chinese and Russian descriptions on the UNESCO page say 4,000 years where the others say 40,000.

- **Key claims:**

### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- The shape Heyerdahl relies on is generic. A curved hull line with vertical strokes for crew is about the simplest way to draw a manned boat, and he himself says he saw the same 'sickle-shaped ships' on Easter Island, in Egypt and in Mesopotamia (AI 8.2). A likeness found everywhere cannot single out one link.
- His dates do not meet. He has the Norwegian carvings 'millennia later' than Gobustan's, yet puts the migration in the 1st century AD. Specialists place Gobustan boats in the Neolithic or earlier (Farajova) or the late Pleistocene to early Holocene (Sigari), thousands of years before any 1st-century movement; Helskog's abstract puts Scandinavian boat carvings at 7,000 to 6,000 years old, also long before it.
- Helskog's abstract says boats 'most likely' had 'multiple points of origin rather than one', which cuts against deriving one region's boats from the other.
- The magazine that published Heyerdahl records that the idea 'came under heavy criticism in Norway' and 'has yet to be proven' (AI 14.4).
- For the Argonaut proposal: the 'unusual raised terminal' is not unusual at Gobustan. Hooked prows are 'characteristic', both ends can curve up, and the sun-prow recurs on Rocks 8, 19 and 29. A feature shared by many local boats cannot mark one carving as a particular ship.
- Boats with armed men around them are also a stock arrangement: Farajova describes boats in the centre with bow-and-arrow hunters around them as the pattern of a whole period.
- On Rock 29's south face the boat overlaps two male figures (Sigari), so boat and figures were not cut at one time.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> The resemblance between Gobustan and Scandinavian boats is read by specialists as convergence: two coastal peoples drawing the craft they depended on, in the most economical form. Helskog's abstract frames boats in both regions as tied to 'transportation, fishing and hunting' and to 'symbolism, beliefs and ritual communication', and looks at differences as well as similarities. At Gobustan the boats are read locally: a record of life beside a higher Caspian (Sigari), or a means of transport for Neolithic communities, shown with hunters in recurring scenes (Farajova). The sun on the prow is treated as a decorative or symbolic convention of a later boat style, not as the emblem of one vessel. Heyerdahl's 'reed boat' identification is his own; neither Sigari nor Farajova in the texts read says what the boats were made of.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> Two uses. First, the typology undercuts the proposal's 'unusual raised terminal': hooked and upcurved ends are standard on Gobustan boats and the Rock 29 south-face boat is an ordinary sun-prow type, shown with the armed figures that commonly accompany boats there. Second, Heyerdahl is the precedent. A famous outsider saw a likeness in the same boats, tied it to a much later written legend (Snorri's Odin) and announced an origin. The parallel with reading Rock 29 as the Argo is close, and the published record shows what happened next: criticism at home, a conference volume, and no specialist adoption.
- **Open questions:** 
- Whether anyone has counted how many Gobustan boats carry a sun-prow or an upcurved end. Sigari gives examples by rock, not a frequency; 'common' rests on his word 'characteristically' and on 'recurrent' in the 2025 paper.
- What Heyerdahl's second Gobustan boat type was.
- Which year Heyerdahl first saw Gobustan (1980, 1981 and 1989 all appear).
- What Farajova's chapter 'Boats in Rock Art of Gobustan' in the same volume says about types.
- Whether the 'raised terminal' in the Ragazas article is the sun-prow or something else; the readable part of that article does not say.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Odyssey 12.69-72 is the oldest surviving mention of the Argo. It names the ship, Jason, Hera and Aeetes, and says the Argo passed the Planctae 'on her voyage from Aeetes'. It names no country, no river and no sea. The word Aia does not occur in Homer at all in the passages opened; what occurs is Aiaie (Aeaea), Circe's island, and Circe is 'own sister to Aeetes' (10.137). Odyssey 12.3-4 puts that island where Dawn has her house and the sun rises, reached straight after leaving the river Oceanus.
- Hesiod, Theogony 992-1002, tells of Jason carrying Medea 'from Aeetes' to Iolcus after the labours set by Pelias. Iolcus is the only place named. Theogony 956-962 makes Aeetes the son of Helios and an Oceanid, with an Oceanid wife. Theogony 340 lists Phasis among the rivers born to Ocean and Tethys, with no location. Neither Homer nor the Theogony names Colchis.
- Mimnermus (later seventh century BC) is the first author opened who names Aia as the place the fleece came from. He puts 'the city of Aeetes' where the Sun's rays lie in a golden chamber 'beside the lips of Oceanus'. Strabo, following Demetrius of Scepsis, reads this as placing Aeetes 'in Oceanus, outside the inhabited world in the east'. No Colchis, no Phasis, no Black Sea in the lines as quoted.
- Eumelus: Pausanias 2.3.10 reports that Eumelus had Aeetes, given Ephyraea (Corinth) by Helios, leave 'for Colchis' (Greek: 'to the Colchians'). Scholars cite the verse itself as Eumelus fr. 3 Bernabe = fr. 17 West, and treat it as the first attestation of Colchis as Aeetes' country. Its date is disputed: Eumelus the man is put in the late eighth and early seventh century BC, but West dates the Korinthiaka no earlier than the mid sixth century, after Greeks had settled in Colchis, and Ivantchik agrees the fragment cannot date the identification.
- Pindar, Pythian 4 (462 BC), is the oldest surviving connected narrative (West's description) and the earliest securely dated text opened that puts the voyage at the Phasis among Colchians: Medea is 'queen of the Colchians' (line 11) and the Argonauts 'came to Phasis, where they clashed with the dark-faced Colchians in the realm of Aeetes himself' (211-213). Pindar does not use the name Aia in this ode (checked in the Greek). His return route still runs through Ocean and the 'Red Sea' (251) with a twelve-day portage 'from the Ocean' (25-27), so the river Phasis still opens onto the outer sea in his geography.
- Herodotus (later fifth century BC) is the first author opened to join the two names in one phrase: 'Aia the Colchian' (Aian ten Kolchida) at 1.2.2, 7.193.2 and 7.197.3, with the river Phasis. At 1.104 he treats the Phasis and the Colchians as an ordinary place thirty days' walk from the Sea of Azov, with Media beyond one intervening people.
- Apollonius Rhodius (third century BC) gives the fully mapped version: 'Colchian Aea lies at the edge of Pontus and of the world' (2.417-418); the Argo reaches the Phasis at 'the utmost bourne of the sea' and rows upriver with the Caucasus and 'the Cytaean city of Aea' on the left (2.1260-1270). At 4.131-135 he ties the Phasis to the Araxes through a river Lycus and has the joined stream flow into 'the Caucasian Sea', which in context is the Black Sea at the Phasis mouth. The word Caspian appears once in Seaton's translation of the whole poem, for the 'Caspian shell' in which Medea gathers the Promethean drug in Book 3. No voyage on the Caspian appears anywhere in the poem.
- So in the texts opened, the order is: Aeetes with no place (Odyssey, Theogony); Aia by the Ocean stream at the sunrise (Mimnermus); Colchians and Colchis (Eumelus if early, otherwise Pindar in 462 BC); 'Colchian Aia' on the Phasis (Herodotus); a charted Black Sea voyage (Apollonius). Strabo, writing around the turn of the era, already knew this was contested: he records Demetrius of Scepsis saying 'Homer knew absolutely nothing about the voyage of Jason to Phasis' and argues against him.
- Scholarship opened: West holds that the seventh-century Argonautic epic placed Aia in the north of the Black Sea (Crimea to the Sea of Azov, Phasis perhaps the Tanais/Don) and that 'the mythical Aia was not yet located at Colchis'. Ivantchik holds that Aia was at first a mythical land on the Ocean, a form of the land of the dead (after Wilamowitz and Lesky), that Greeks before the mid seventh century took the Black Sea to be part of Ocean, and that Aia was identified first with Qulha, a kingdom of Urartian inscriptions on the south-east Black Sea coast, and only later moved to the Phasis/Rioni basin. Gordeziani defends the traditional view that Aeetes' land was Colchis from the start, on the Lemnos and Hellespont references in Homer, and reports Drager's view that Aia was first thought of in Ethiopia.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> The texts themselves, read in the Greek where opened: what each names and what it leaves out. Strabo 1.2.38-40 preserves both the Mimnermus lines and an ancient argument (Demetrius of Scepsis against Neanthes, Strabo against Demetrius) over whether Homer knew a voyage to the Phasis. ### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- Against a Caspian origin: every ancient text opened that gives the Argo a real-world destination puts it on the Phasis and the Black Sea (Pindar, Herodotus, Apollonius, Strabo). None puts it on the Caspian. The one Caspian word found in Apollonius is an adjective for a shell.
- Against using Colchis as the fixed point at all: the two oldest sources name no place, and Mimnermus puts Aia on the Ocean at the sunrise. West and Ivantchik both state that the earliest Aia was not Colchis. If Colchis is a later identification, a theory that starts from Colchis and works east to the Caspian is arguing from the late form of the myth.
- Against any Bronze Age eyewitness source: Ivantchik argues that using the legend as direct testimony of precolonial contact is 'une utilisation plutôt naïve de la source', that there are no Greek imports around the Black Sea before the mid seventh century BC, and that Aia was a form of the land of the dead.
- Cutting the other way: an unlocated or sunrise Aia does not exclude the Caspian either. The early texts do not point to the Black Sea any more firmly than to anywhere else in the far east. And West himself allows that the legend's framing may go back to 1150-1000 BC, and Gordeziani argues for Bronze Age Aegean and Pontic navigation and an original Colchian setting. These views leave room for an old eastern origin, though none of them involves the Caspian.
- Apollonius 4.131-135 links the Phasis to the Araxes through the Lycus. The Araxes belongs to the Caspian drainage, so Hellenistic geography did imagine the Colchian rivers tied to waters further east. In Apollonius the joined stream still empties into 'the Caucasian Sea' at the Phasis mouth, and Herodotus 1.104 knows a land route from the Colchians to Media. This is the nearest thing in these texts to a Caspian connection and it is late.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> Three mainstream readings of the same texts. (1) Aia began as a mythical land of the sunrise on the Ocean stream, possibly a form of the otherworld, and was pinned to Colchis as Greek knowledge of the Black Sea grew (Wilamowitz and Lesky as reported by Ivantchik; Ivantchik; Haas as quoted by Gordeziani). (2) An early Argonautic epic already used Black Sea geography, but of the northern coast, and the Colchian setting came in the sixth century after Greek settlement there (West). (3) The traditional view that the destination was Colchis from the beginning (Gordeziani, citing Lordkipanidze), which Drager, as quoted by Gordeziani, dismisses as having 'nur lokalpatriotische, keine wissenschaftliche Begründungen'. Strabo's own reading is a fourth, ancient one: the voyage to the Phasis was historical, motivated by Colchian gold, and Homer built his myth on it.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the textual test of the proposal. The carving theory needs the myth to have a place of origin that can be traced back to the Caspian. The two oldest witnesses give the destination no place at all, the third puts it on the Ocean at the sunrise, and Colchis appears only once Greeks were sailing the Black Sea. So the 'route across the Caucasus from Colchis to the Caspian' starts from a late layer of the story. That weakens the Caspian reading and the confident Colchian one alike: an essay can say fairly that the early myth points nowhere on a map, and that a wordless carving cannot supply the name, the ship or the fleece that the texts themselves took centuries to locate.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Strabo 11.7.3: the Oxus is navigable, a statement that Aristobulus and Eratosthenes took from Patrocles; many Indian goods come down it to the Hyrcanian (Caspian) Sea, cross to Albania (roughly modern Azerbaijan), and go by the Cyrus (Kura) and 'the region that comes next' to the Euxine. The passage does not name the Phasis or Colchis.
- Strabo 2.1.15 gives the same report in a different form, with 'easily' and without 'many': Indian merchandise carried over the mountains to the Oxus is easily brought down to the Hyrcanian Sea and then by the rivers as far as the Pontus.
- Strabo 11.2.17 supplies the western link in his own voice: the Phasis is navigated up to the fortress of Sarapana, and from there a wagon road reaches the Cyrus in four days. Strabo 11.3.4 describes the same pass from Colchis into Iberia as narrow defiles with 120 bridges over the Phasis.
- Pliny 6.52: Varro reported that it was found out under Pompey's command that India to the Bactrian river feeding the Oxus is seven days, and that Indian goods taken across the Caspian and up the Cyrus can be brought to Phasis on the Pontus with a land journey of not more than five days. The Latin verb is 'posse devehi', 'can be carried down', a statement of possibility.
- Tarn 1901: the Oxus did not flow into the Caspian in historical times, so the supposed route needs rethinking. He allows 'some trade' but no 'important trade route', and sets Strabo's own remark that the Caspian was unnavigated, and Ptolemy's land road via Hyrcania and Merv, against Pliny.
- Tarn 1938 hardens this: there was only ever one independent statement, Patrocles' report to Antiochus I, which meant that a route could easily be made. Eratosthenes misread it as existing trade, and Varro's notice is Patrocles again with the known Cyrus to Phasis portage added. Tarn credits the explanation to Kiessling's 1914 Pauly-Wissowa article 'Hyrkania'.
- Against Tarn: Rtveladze 2010 argues the route was real and names it the 'Great Indian Road', citing settlement along the Uzboi channel, Caucasian pottery at Chorasmian sites, a pseudo-Scymnus line placing Bactrians and Indians at Phasis, and a few Graeco-Bactrian coins from Kabala and Tbilisi. Dundua 2019 (abstract) argues it worked in the 3rd and early 2nd centuries BC, was broken by 65 BC, and that Varro speaks only of a possibility.
- Greek and Roman writers did link Jason to the Caspian side, but as an overland extension from Colchis: Strabo 11.4.8 has him press on from Colchis to the Caspian; Strabo 11.13.10 reports Jasonian shrines in Media and a Mount Jasonium above the Caspian Gates; Pliny 6.38 says the Albani are said to descend from Jason; Tacitus Annals 6.34 has Iberians and Albanians claim Thessalian descent through Jason.
- **Evidence offered:** 
> For the route: two notices in Strabo that go back to one source (Patrocles), one notice in Pliny from Varro, and Strabo's separate description of the Phasis to Cyrus wagon road. Tarn's case rests on the geography of the Oxus (it reaches the Aral, not the Caspian), on the wording ('easily', and Pliny's 'posse'), on Strabo's own statement that the Caspian was not navigated, on the absence of any named Caspian port, and on Ptolemy describing the land road instead. Rtveladze and Dundua rely on coin finds, Uzboi settlement, Caucasian pottery in Chorasmia and pseudo-Scymnus. No source opened offers evidence older than the Hellenistic period for through traffic.
- **Date range estimates:** 
- No date for any Gobustan motif appears in these sources. They do not mention Gobustan.
- The tradition is Hellenistic. Tarn 1901 says Seleucus sent Patrocles to the Caspian; Tarn 1938 has the report made to Antiochus I. That puts the voyage in the 280s BC [uncertain: exact years not confirmed in a source opened here].
- Eratosthenes worked in the 3rd century BC [uncertain: dates from general knowledge, not opened].
- Varro's notice concerns Pompey's command in the Mithridatic war ('Pompeio iuxta res gerenti Mithridatico bello', Pliny 6.51). Dundua's abstract dates it to 65 BC.
- Strabo wrote in the early 1st century AD (Dundua's abstract says his work was 'published' in AD 19/20). Pliny's Natural History dates to the AD 70s (Dundua: 'the 70s of the 1st c. A.D.').
- Earliest date claimed by a defender of the route: Rtveladze says a Black Sea to Caspian route was in use 'already in the 5th century B.C.' on the strength of Herodotus 1.104, and floats the 8th to 7th centuries BC only 'if one gives credit to the legends about Jason's journey'. Nothing in these sources puts the corridor in the Bronze Age.
- **Dating method:** 
> Textual only. The tradition is dated by the lifetimes of the authors cited (Aristobulus, Patrocles, Eratosthenes, Varro, Strabo, Pliny) and by source criticism of who copied whom. The modern defenders add coin hoards and site occupation along the Uzboi. No radiocarbon, stratigraphic or shoreline dating bears on this item.
- **Panel details:** 
> Not applicable. None of the sources for this item mentions Rock 29, Boyuk Das, or any Gobustan carving.
- **Geographic claims:** 
> The route as the ancient texts give it: India over the mountains to Bactria; the river of Bactra (Bactrus in the LacusCurtius Latin, Icarus in Bostock and Riley, Iachrus in the Jahn text Tarn quotes) into the Oxus (Amu Darya); down the Oxus to the Hyrcanian or Caspian Sea; across the sea to Albania; up the Cyrus (Kura); overland four days (Strabo, Sarapana to the Cyrus) or not more than five days (Pliny) to the Phasis (Rioni); down the Phasis to the city of Phasis and the Euxine. Rtveladze identifies the land leg with the Surami Pass. The direction of travel in every text is east to west, from the Caspian to the Black Sea, for Indian goods. The sources describe a river and portage route with a mountain pass in the middle, not a through waterway: nobody sails from the Caspian to Colchis.

### Caveats

- **Counter evidence:** 
- The corridor is attested about a thousand years or more after the Bronze Age. It cannot by itself show Bronze Age traffic between the Caspian shore and Colchis.
- Tarn's reading is that the route was a proposal, not a practice: Patrocles' 'easily', Pliny's 'posse devehi', and the fact that Pompey had to ask.
- Strabo himself calls the Caspian unnavigated and idle (11.7.2, C509, as quoted by Tarn: 'aplous te ousa kai argos'). [uncertain: read only in Tarn's citation, the section itself was not opened.]
- No Caspian port is named by any ancient writer (Tarn 1938).
- The eastern half depends on the Oxus reaching the Caspian, which Tarn denies for historical times; the Uzboi question was unsettled when he wrote and Rtveladze treats the Uzboi as navigable.
- Strabo 11.5.8 describes a different arrangement for Indian goods: the Aorsi 'could import on camels the Indian and Babylonian merchandise, receiving it in their turn from the Armenians and the Medes', which is overland caravan trade.
- The middle of the corridor is a real barrier. Strabo 11.3.4 describes ravines and 120 bridges; Puturidze 2025 says the Likhi range stopped the westward spread of the Middle Bronze Age Trialeti culture, and that the Colchian culture of that period 'shows no apparent affinity with it'.
- The texts move goods westward from the Caspian. The Argonaut story moves a ship eastward to Colchis from the Aegean. The Caspian-side Jason traditions in Strabo, Pliny and Tacitus all start from Colchis and work outward.
- **Independent corroboration:** 
> For the existence of the route: partial and contested. Strabo's 11.2.17 wagon road independently confirms the Phasis to Cyrus land link, and Tarn accepts that portage as 'a well-known thing'. Rtveladze and Dundua (and, as cited by Rtveladze, Balakhvantsev and Lordkipanidze, not opened) support a working Hellenistic transit route; Tarn, following Kiessling, rejects it. For a Bronze Age Caspian to Colchis link relevant to Gobustan: none found.
- **Alternative explanations:** 
> The mainstream sceptical reading (Kiessling 1914 as reported by Tarn; Tarn 1938) is that all the notices descend from one feasibility report by Patrocles, written after he mistook the mouth of the Atrek for the Oxus. On that reading Strabo 11.7.3 is Eratosthenes' misunderstanding and Pliny 6.52 is a Roman staff report repeating it. A middle position, Tarn's own in 1901 and J. Kennedy's as Tarn quotes him, is that some goods passed this way but the trade was minor, fitful and through many hands. The Jason traditions on the Caspian side are best read as Greek aetiology: Strabo ties them to place names (Jasonia, Mount Jasonium, Media from Medus, Armenia from Armenus), which is how Greeks explained foreign names, not a memory of a voyage.

### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:** 
> This is the only textual bridge between the Caspian and Colchis, and it does not carry the weight a Gobustan origin needs. It is Hellenistic at the earliest, it runs the wrong way (Indian goods westward), it includes a four or five day mountain portage, and the leading sceptic argued it never operated at all. What the essay can fairly say: Greeks and Romans knew the two seas could be linked by the Kura and the Rioni, and by Strabo's day a tradition had Jason going on from Colchis to the Caspian. Both facts are late and both start from Colchis on the Black Sea.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
- Strabo 11.2.19: reports as hearsay ('It is said') that the Soanes, in the Caucasus heights above Dioscurias, recover gold from mountain torrents with perforated troughs and fleecy skins, and that this is the origin of the golden fleece myth. He then offers an alternative in the same sentence (the name 'Iberians' and gold mines). He does not say he saw it, and says nothing about a voyage.
- Strabo 1.2.39 and 11.2.18: the voyage to Phasis has 'an element of plausibility'; Colchian mineral wealth 'suggests a reasonable motive'; myths carry Jason 'as far even as Media', and sanctuaries of Jason are pointed out in Armenia and Media.
- Appian, Mith. 103: Caucasus streams carry invisible gold dust; inhabitants catch it on shaggy sheepskins; 'Perhaps the golden fleece of Aetes was of this kind.' A one-sentence aside in an account of Pompey's campaign of 66 BCE. Appian gives no view on why the Argonauts sailed.
- Okrostsvaridze et al. (Episodes 2014, open access): Svaneti is rich in bedrock and placer gold; locals still wash gold through holed wooden vessels onto sheepskins; therefore the 'gold sands' of the sources were 'a geological reality'; and the authors 'contend' the Argonaut voyage 'was a real event' whose purpose was gold and gold-mining technology.
- Okrostsvaridze et al. (Quaternary International, online 20 November 2014): by Science News's report, the same case, with the voyage placed 3,300 to 3,500 years ago and Svaneti identified as a province of ancient Colchis. Paper itself not read, and retracted in 2024.
- Tim Severin, 1984: a 54-foot oared replica galley can travel from Volos (Pagasae/Iolcus) through the Dardanelles and Bosphorus to Poti at the mouth of the Rioni (Phasis). Georgia Today adds that the crew visited Svaneti and saw sheepskin gold recovery. [uncertain: Severin's own conclusions were not read in his book]
- **Evidence offered:** 
> Strabo: report at second hand, no named informant. Appian: a narrative aside, hedged with 'perhaps'. Okrostsvaridze et al. (Episodes): 2007-2010 fieldwork in Svaneti using remote sensing; 'More than 1000 samples' assayed by ICP-MS at ACME Labs, Vancouver; a tentative estimate of 60-65 tons of gold in the Sakeni ore field (Science News reports 65 to 70 metric tons for the QI paper); collected wooden vessels and sheepskins in current use; then a reading of Strabo, Pliny and Appian. The geology supports 'there is gold in Svaneti streams and people there catch it on fleeces'. Nothing in it is evidence of a Bronze Age Greek voyage: no Mycenaean finds, no dated ancient workings. Severin: an experimental voyage, which tests seaworthiness and rowing endurance only.
- **Dating method:** 
> None for the myth's origin. The voyage date in Okrostsvaridze et al. is not measured: it is the conventional Mycenaean and pre-Trojan War bracket (the Episodes paper reasons from the end of Mycenaean civilisation, which it puts in the 11th century BC). No radiocarbon, stratigraphy or dated ancient mining feature is reported in the Episodes paper. The claim that conditions are 'the same now' as 3,300-3,500 years ago is an assertion of geological continuity reported by Science News.
- **Panel details:** 
> Not applicable. None of these sources concerns Gobustan, Rock 29 or any rock art. The only imagery mentioned is in Science News: bronze sculptures in Svaneti villages showing a ram's head on a bird's body, which Okrostsvaridze 'suspects' reflect the Golden Fleece legend.
- **Geographic claims:** 
> All of these sources put the fleece on the Black Sea side. Strabo's Soanes hold 'the heights of the Caucasus above Dioscurias' (a Black Sea port); the Phasis flows through Colchis (11.2.17); Aea 'is still shown on the Phasis' (1.2.39). Okrostsvaridze et al. place the gold in Svaneti, Greater Caucasus, western Georgia, and call the Black Sea coast Colchis. Severin's route: Volos, Dardanelles, Sea of Marmara, Bosphorus, Black Sea south coast, Poti (Georgia About gives arrival at Poti on 20 July 1984; Georgia Today says 'the Batumi coast'). ### Essay use

- **Relevance to essay:**

None of the three shows a voyage happened, and the one peer-reviewed paper that said so has been retracted. A wordless carving on the Caspian side has less than this: no text, no practice, no route. It also has to compete with sources that all point to the Black Sea.
