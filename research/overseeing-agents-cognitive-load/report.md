## BCG and HBR study: When Using AI Leads to Brain Fry (March 2026)

### Source

- **Citation:** 
> Julie Bedard, Matthew Kropp, Megan Hsu, Olivia T. Karaman, Jason Hawes, Gabriella Rosen Kellerman. 'When Using AI Leads to "Brain Fry"'. Harvard Business Review, Generative AI section, 5 March 2026. https://hbr.org/2026/03/when-using-ai-leads-to-brain-fry ; BCG summary page https://www.bcg.com/news/5march2026-when-using-ai-leads-brain-fry
- **Source tier:** 
> Consultancy research published as a practitioner article in a business magazine (not peer-reviewed; no methods appendix seen)
- **Access status:** 
> Printed copy of the HBR article, April 2026, via replyfabric.ai; live page paywalled. Re-opened 21 Oct 2026: HBR live page returned only the title, byline and truncated summary (no body). BCG page returned only a short summary ('Based on a BCG study of 1,488 US workers across large companies') with none of the percentages. No BCG Henderson Institute detail page was found or opened. All figures and quotes below come from the printed copy.
- **Sponsor and incentive:** 
> Authors are BCG staff: Bedard (Managing Director & Partner, BCG Henderson Institute Fellow), Kropp (Senior Partner, CTO of BCG X, BHI Fellow), Hsu (Project Leader, BHI Ambassador), Kellerman (Expert Partner and Director). Karaman and Hawes are UC Riverside PhD candidates who are also BCG research assistants. The article states no funder. BCG sells AI strategy and people-transformation advice, and the article recommends that 'A full 70% of AI transformation efforts should be devoted to people and processes', which is close to BCG's service line.
- **Exact quotes:** 
- 'AI brain fry, which we define as mental fatigue from excessive use or oversight of AI tools beyond one's cognitive capacity.' (para after sample description)
- 'we conducted a study of 1,488 full-time U.S.-based workers (48% male vs 51% female; 58% independent contributors vs 41% leaders) at large companies across industries, roles, and levels. We asked them about patterns and quantity of AI use, work experiences, and cognition and emotions.' (opening of methods paragraph)
- 'The workers in our study who reported that their AI work required high rather than low degrees of oversight expended 14% more mental effort on the job. A high degree of AI oversight also predicted 12% more mental fatigue for participants. Finally, more intensive AI oversight also predicted 19% greater information overload' (section 'When AI Use Predicts Mental Fatigue')
- 'As employees go from using one AI tool to two simultaneously, they experience a significant increase in productivity. As they incorporate a third tool, productivity again increases, but at a lower rate. After three tools, though, productivity scores dipped.' (same section; preceded by 'perceived productivity increases')
- 'Fourteen percent of participants in our study using AI for work endorsed the experience of AI brain fry.' (section 'Introducing AI Brain Fry'; asked at the end of the survey 'to avoid priming effects')
- 'Workers in our study who endorsed AI brain fry experience 33% more decision fatigue than those who did not.' (section 'The Business Cost')
- 'Among participants using AI at work, those experiencing brain fry reported making mistakes significantly more often— scoring 11% and 39% higher on the minor and major error frequency measures, respectively—than those who did not.'
- 'We defined minor errors as "small errors that are easy to catch or correct, such as coding or formatting errors" and major errors as "errors with more serious consequences, such as those that could affect safety, outcomes, or important decisions."'
- 'Among workers who did not report AI brain fry, 25% showed active intent to leave. Among those who did report AI brain fry, that rose to 34%. This represents a 39% increase in active worker intent to leave among top users of AI.'
- 'Quantitatively, we found strong correlations between AI brain fry and information overload, but a less straightforward relationship with items related to task switching.'
- 'Our research suggests adverse productivity gains after the use of three AI agents at the same time.' (Lessons for Leaders)
- 'AI oversight cannot simply be layered on top of human oversight; nor can AI agents be stacked on one user ad infinitum. Just as we have norms for spans of control for managing humans, so, too, limits need to be defined for human + agent oversight and for agents alone.'
- 'Acute mental fatigue, on the other hand, is caused by marshalling attention, working memory, and executive control beyond the limited capacity of these systems. This is exactly what intensive AI oversight requires.'
- 'our study suggests that use of AI tools may be causing the mental exhaustion itself.' (hedged causal language)
- 'Just because a worker can keep iterating with AI at a low marginal cost does not mean they should.'
- Engineer quote: 'I end each day exhausted—not from the work itself, but from the managing of the work.' (Francesco Bonacci, X post, cited as motivating anecdote)

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> Intensive oversight of AI tools produces acute mental fatigue ('AI brain fry'), distinct from burnout. Oversight was the most mentally taxing form of AI engagement. Brain fry is linked to decision fatigue, self-reported errors and intent to quit. Using AI to replace repetitive tasks is linked to lower burnout (but not lower mental fatigue). Manager, team and organisational practices (answering questions, organised integration, clear strategy, work-life balance signals) are linked to lower fatigue. Leaders should set limits on human-plus-agent oversight, shift metrics from activity to impact, and track cognitive load.
- **Headline figures:** 
- n = 1,488 full-time US workers at large companies; 48% male, 51% female; 58% independent contributors, 41% leaders.
- High vs low AI oversight: 14% more mental effort; 12% more mental fatigue; 19% greater information overload (all 'predicted', i.e. associations).
- 14% of participants using AI for work endorsed having experienced AI brain fry (single yes/no style item asked at the end of the survey).
- Brain fry vs no brain fry: 33% more decision fatigue; error frequency measures 11% higher (minor) and 39% higher (major); active intent to leave 25% vs 34%.
- Intent to quit arithmetic: 25% to 34% is a 9 percentage point rise, a relative rise of 36% (9/25). The article calls it '39% increase'. 39% is not reproduced by these rounded figures (possible unrounded values or a different calculation, not explained).
- Using AI to cut routine tasks: burnout scores 15% lower than non-users of AI in that way.
- Manager answers AI questions: 15% lower mental fatigue; manager expects staff to work it out alone: 5% higher ('AI orphan tax'); org expects more work due to AI: 12% higher mental fatigue; org values work-life balance: 28% lower mental fatigue.
- Tools simultaneously: productivity rises from 1 to 2 tools, rises more slowly with a third, dips after three. Measure is perceived/self-reported productivity across all AI tool types (search, research, data analysis, image, coding agents), no numbers given in text.
- **Observed vs stated:** 
> Entirely self-reported survey: AI use patterns, oversight level, mental effort, fatigue, information overload, decision fatigue, errors (self-reported frequency), intent to quit and perceived productivity. No observed behaviour, physiology, error audits or retention data. Brain fry itself is a self-endorsed single item, supplemented by open-text descriptions ('fog', 'buzzing', headaches).

### Oversight and load

- **What makes oversight tiring:** 
> Authors say acute fatigue comes from 'marshalling attention, working memory, and executive control beyond the limited capacity of these systems'. Oversight plus increased workload widens 'sphere of accountability', 'requiring them to pay attention to more outcomes for more tools in the same amount of time'. Qualitative accounts point to information overload and task switching; the data link strongly to information overload but only 'less straightforward' to task-switching items. The engineering manager quote: 'double-checking every little thing', 'working harder to manage the tools than to actually solve the problem'.
- **Consequence for quality:** 
> Self-reported: more decision fatigue (33%), more minor and major errors (11%, 39% higher frequency scores), higher intent to quit (25% vs 34%). The authors also infer, without measuring it, that cultures prioritising cognitive thriving 'can expect to see better judgments, fewer errors, and higher retention'. Their $150M-per-year suboptimal-decision cost for a $5B firm is from a 2018 study they cite, applied loosely ('could increase that cost by millions').
- **What reduces the load:** 
> Limits on simultaneous agents per person (spans of control for human-plus-agent oversight; adverse perceived productivity after three); embed AI in team workflows as a collective capability; manager who answers AI questions (15% lower fatigue); clear AI strategy and training (directional only); avoid team pressure to use AI and large variation in use; clear workload expectations; work-life balance signals (28% lower); metrics based on impact not token or usage volume; do not immediately backfill automated work; upskill in problem framing, analysis planning and prioritisation; track cognitive load in people analytics; design tools needing less intense attention or working memory. Replacing routine tasks lowers burnout but, per the article, not mental fatigue scores.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> The printed copy states no formal limitations section. The authors use hedged language in places: 'predicted', 'directionally', 'seemed to', 'our study suggests that use of AI tools may be causing the mental exhaustion itself'. They concede the task-switching link is 'less straightforward' and that the organisational practices only 'seemed to help'. No mention of cross-sectional design, common-method bias, self-selection, or effect sizes / significance tests for most figures.

### Essay use

- **Usable claim:** 
> In a BCG survey of 1,488 US workers at large companies (self-reported), people who said their AI work needed high oversight reported 14% more mental effort and 12% more mental fatigue than those with low oversight. Fourteen percent of AI users said they had experienced 'AI brain fry', and those people reported more decision fatigue, more errors and higher intent to quit. The authors call for limits on how many agents one person oversees. Say 'reported' and 'associated with', and note the authors are BCG consultants.
- **Do not use:** 
> Do not say brain fry causes errors, quitting or lost productivity. Do not use '39% increase' in intent to quit (25% to 34% is 9 points, or 36% relative); say 'from 25% to 34%'. Do not describe 33% as the share of workers or as a rate of errors: it is higher decision-fatigue score. Do not say the 11% and 39% are shares of people making errors; they are higher error-frequency scores. Do not say productivity falls after three coding agents: it is perceived productivity across mixed AI tools. Do not use $150M figure as this study's finding. Do not use press paraphrases of the study. Do not say software engineers specifically were studied.

## HBR: AI Doesn't Reduce Work, It Intensifies It (February 2026)

### Source

- **Citation:** 
> Aruna Ranganathan and Xingqi Maggie Ye (UC Berkeley Haas). 'AI Doesn't Reduce Work—It Intensifies It'. Harvard Business Review, 9 February 2026. https://hbr.org/2026/02/ai-doesnt-reduce-work-it-intensifies-it ; Haas newsroom interview with Ye, 18 February 2026: https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/
- **Source tier:** 
> Practitioner article in a business magazine reporting initial findings of in-progress academic research (not peer-reviewed; no separate paper found)
- **Date and recency:** 
> Published 9 Feb 2026 (HBR); interview 18 Feb 2026. Data collection: eight months, April to December 2025 per Willison summary.

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> AI tools did not reduce work in the studied firm; employees worked faster, took on broader task scope and extended work into more hours, largely without being asked. Workers ran several threads at once (manual work while AI generates, parallel agents, reviving deferred tasks), producing constant task switching, frequent checking of AI outputs and a growing list of open tasks. Expectations reset so extra effort becomes standard. Authors propose an 'AI practice': intentional pauses, sequencing/batching, protected focus windows, and human check-ins and reflection.
- **Sample unit and population:** 
> One anonymised US technology company, around 200 employees across functional groups (not specifically software engineers; engineers were among those described coding with AI). Company did not mandate AI but offered enterprise subscriptions to commercial tools.

### Oversight and load

- **What makes oversight tiring:** 
> Per Willison's quote of HBR and the Haas interview: managing several active threads, continual attention switching, frequent checking of AI outputs, a growing backlog of open tasks, and reduced recovery time. Ye: constant switching and reduced recovery can impair judgment.
- **What reduces the load:** 
> An 'AI practice' of norms: intentional pauses before major decisions, batching non-urgent updates, protecting focus windows, and protected time for check-ins, shared reflection and dialogue. Recommendations are the authors' proposals, not tested interventions.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Described as in-progress research with initial findings, one company. No explicit limitations list found in the opened material (single-firm limit is the fetch tool's inference).
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Not searched in depth. Single firm, non-peer-reviewed, no control group or objective time data seen; worker enthusiasm and voluntary uptake means intensification may be self-chosen rather than imposed.

### Essay use

- **Usable claim:** 
> In an in-progress, eight-month study of about 200 employees at one US tech company, Ranganathan and Ye observed and interviewed staff and reported that AI use led people to work faster, take on more, and work longer hours, with several concurrent threads and constant checking of AI output adding cognitive load. Their proposed remedy is team norms such as pauses and protected focus time.
- **Do not use:** 
> The '62% unsustainable' figure; claims of measured burnout rates or error rates; generalisation to software engineers or all firms; any quote from the HBR body not seen directly; 'twice-weekly observation' and '40 interviews' as HBR facts without the Haas citation.

## Bainbridge, Ironies of Automation (1983)

### Source

- **Citation:** 
> Lisanne Bainbridge (Dept of Psychology, University College London). 'Ironies of Automation'. Automatica, Vol. 19, No. 6, pp. 775-779, 1983 (Brief Paper). Received 16 Dec 1982, revised 23 May 1983; first presented at IFAC/IFIP/IFORS/IEA conference, Baden-Baden, Sept 1982. Copy opened: https://gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/1983-bainbridge.pdf
- **Source tier:** Peer-reviewed (journal brief paper); conceptual/position paper, not an empirical study
- **Access status:** 
> Fully read (scanned reproduction with text layer, gwern.net PDF, all 5 pages; OCR has minor typos, e.g. 'main.tenance', which are corrected in quotes below only where noted)
- **Sponsor and incentive:** None stated in the paper. Academic psychologist; no product sold.
- **Exact quotes:** 
- Abstract: "This paper discusses the ways in which automation of industrial processes may expand rather than eliminate problems with the human operator."
- Section 1: "The second irony is that the designer who tries to eliminate the operator still leaves the operator to do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate."
- Section 1: "...the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be the contribution of the human operator."
- Section 1.1: "He may be expected to monitor that the automatic system is operating correctly, and if it is not he may be expected to call a more experienced operator or to take-over himself."
- Section 1.1.1: "Unfortunately, physical skills deteriorate when they are not used, particularly the refinements of gain and timing. This means that a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one."
- Section 1.1.1: "When manual take-over is needed there is likely to be something wrong with the process, so that unusual actions will be needed to control it, and one can argue that the operator needs to be more rather than less skilled, and less rather than more loaded, than average."
- Section 1.1.2: "There is some concern that the present generation of automated systems, which are monitored by former manual operators, are riding on their skills, which later generations of operators cannot be expected to have."
- Section 1.1.2: "Manual operators may come into the control room quarter to half an hour before they are due to take over control, so they can get this feel for what the process is doing."
- Section 1.1.3: "We know from many 'vigilance' studies (Mackworth, 1950) that it is impossible for even a highly motivated human being to maintain effective visual attention towards a source of information on which very little happens, for more than about half an hour."
- Section 1.1.3: "A classic method of enforcing operator attention to a steady-state system is to require him to make a log. Unfortunately people can write down numbers without noticing what they are."
- Section 1.1.3: "A more serious irony is that the automatic control system has been put in because it can do the job better than the operator, but yet the operator is being asked to monitor that it is working effectively."
- Section 1.1.3: "There is therefore no way in which the human operator can check in real-time that the computer is following its rules correctly... The human monitor has been given an impossible task."
- Section 1.2: "...the job is one of the worst types, it is very boring but very responsible, yet there is no opportunity to aquire or maintain the qualities required to handle the responsibility." (spelling as printed)
- Section 2.1: "Unfortunately automatic control can 'camouflage' system failure by controlling against the variable changes, so that trends do not become apparent until they are beyond control."
- Section 2.1: "...automatic systems should fail obviously."
- Section 2.1: "Ephrath (1980) has reported a study in which system performance was worse with computer aiding, because the operator made the decisions anyway, and checking the computer added to his workload."
- Section 2.3: "One possibility is to allow the operator to use hands-on control for a short period in each shift. If this suggestion is laughable then simulator practice must be provided."
- Section 2.3: "...it is ironic to train operators in following instructions and then put them in the system to provide intelligence."
- Section 2.3: "Perhaps the final irony is that it is the most successful automated systems, with rare need for manual intervention, which may need the greatest investment in human operator training."

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> (1) Automation does not remove the human; it leaves the operator the tasks designers could not automate. (2) Two residual tasks: monitor, and take over on failure. (3) Take-over needs manual and cognitive skills that decay with disuse, and occurs exactly when the process is abnormal, so the operator needs to be more skilled and less loaded than average. (4) Humans cannot sustain vigilance on rarely changing sources beyond about half an hour. (5) The operator cannot check in real time a computer that decides faster and on more dimensions, so monitoring is at best a meta-level acceptability judgement. (6) Operators lose status and satisfaction when deskilled. (7) Remedies: alarms, displays, hands-on time, high-fidelity simulator practice, systems that fail obviously and make decisions at a human-followable pace.
- **Headline figures:** 
> Only one: 'more than about half an hour' as the limit of effective visual attention on a source where very little happens (citing Mackworth, 1950). Also 'quarter to half an hour' of pre-shift familiarisation for manual operators taking over. No original quantitative data in the paper.
- **Sample unit and population:** 
> No sample. Argument drawn from process-industry control rooms and flight decks, citing prior studies (Edwards and Lees 1974; Ekkers et al. 1979; Ephrath 1980; Mackworth 1950). Not software engineers.
- **Observed vs stated:** 
> Mainly conceptual argument and synthesis of earlier lab and field studies; author asserts rather than reports new observation. One anecdote of an automated plant where operators switched to manual on night shift unless management was present.

### Oversight and load

- **What makes oversight tiring:** 
> Paper's mechanism is less fatigue than impossibility and disuse: vigilance collapses on rarely-changing sources (about half an hour); the monitor cannot verify a faster, higher-dimensional computer in real time; skills and long-term knowledge decay without use; working-storage knowledge of the process state is not built because the operator is not in on-line control. Checking the computer also adds workload (Ephrath 1980). The job is described as 'very boring but very responsible'.
- **Consequence for quality:** 
> Take-over by a degraded operator may set the process oscillating, slows diagnosis, and happens in abnormal conditions with high load. Automatic control can camouflage failure until trends are beyond control. Operators with too little to do may defeat automation (switching to manual). Stress is linked to errors and poor health to absenteeism (citing Ekkers 1979, Mobley 1979).
- **What reduces the load:** 
> Alarms (even 'alarms on alarms') with alarm analysis, though too many flashing lights confuse; displays showing target values; direct-wired displays for main process information; automatics that monitor unusual variable movement and fail obviously; computers making decisions by methods and at a rate the operator can follow; hands-on control for a short period each shift; high-fidelity simulator practice and training in general strategies; fully automatic response for very fast failures; heavy training investment for rarely-failing systems.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> "Solving these problems involves very multi-dimensional decision making: suggestions for discussion will be made here." Recommendations depend on process size, rate of change, failure frequency, shut-down cost and operator quality. Author notes designer-error data is hard to interpret and unpublished. "Brief paper": no new empirical evidence.

### Essay use

- **Usable claim:** 
> Bainbridge (1983) argued that when you automate a process, the human is left with the tasks the designer could not automate: watching the system and stepping in when it fails. Skills decay with disuse, so the person called in at failure may be least practised, at the moment the job is hardest. She also argued a person cannot check in real time a computer that decides faster and on more dimensions, so monitoring is at best a judgement of whether outputs look acceptable. She cites vigilance research that attention on a source where very little happens cannot be sustained for more than about half an hour.
- **Do not use:** 
> Do not say Bainbridge studied software or AI agents, or present the half-hour figure as her own finding or as precise for code review (it is Mackworth 1950, via Bainbridge). Do not claim she measured cognitive load or reported error rates. Do not quote 'ironies' as proof of present-day agent behaviour; frame as a prediction/analogy. Do not attribute the phrase 'ironies of AI' to her.

## Vigilance and mental workload in monitoring tasks

### Source

- **Citation:** 
> (1) Warm, J.S., Parasuraman, R., & Matthews, G. (2008). Vigilance requires hard mental work and is stressful. Human Factors, 50(3), 433-441. PMID 18689050; DOI 10.1177/0018720808320710. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18689050/ (2) Wohleber, R.W., Matthews, G., Lin, J., Szalma, J.L., Calhoun, G.L., Funke, G.J., Chiu, C.-Y.P., & Ruff, H.A. (2019). Vigilance and Automation Dependence in Operation of Multiple Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS): A Simulation Study. Human Factors, 61(3), 488-505. DOI 10.1177/0018720818799468. https://sciences.ucf.edu/psychology/perl/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2019/08/Wohleber-et-al.-Vigilance-and-Automation.pdf
- **Source tier:** 
> Peer-reviewed (both, Human Factors). Warm et al. is a narrative review; Wohleber et al. is an experimental simulation study.
- **Access status:** 
> Warm, Parasuraman & Matthews 2008: ABSTRACT ONLY (structured abstract retrieved from PubMed via NCBI efetch; the SAGE page returned 403 and full text was not opened). Wohleber et al. 2019: FULLY READ (full article PDF, author-hosted at UCF, text extracted and read). Note: Wohleber et al. was a 2018 online / May 2019 print article, Human Factors 61(3), a special issue in remembrance of Joel Warm.
- **Exact quotes:** 
> Warm et al. 2008 (PubMed abstract, BACKGROUND): "Vigilance tasks have typically been viewed as undemanding assignments requiring little mental effort. The vigilance decrement function has also been considered to result from a decline in arousal brought about by understimulation." | (RESULTS): "Subjective reports also show that the workload of vigilance is high and sensitive to factors that increase processing demands." | (RESULTS): "physiological and subjective reports confirm that vigilance tasks reduce task engagement and increase distress and that these changes rise with increased task difficulty." | (CONCLUSIONS): "Converging evidence using behavioral, neural, and subjective measures shows that vigilance requires hard mental work and is stressful." | (APPLICATION): "This research applies to most human-machine systems that require human monitoring, particularly those involving automated subsystems." || Wohleber et al. 2019 (abstract): "Performance accuracy on the more demanding surveillance task showed a vigilance decrement, especially when automation reliability was low. Dependence on automation on this task declined over time. State but not trait factors predicted performance. High distress was associated with poorer performance in more demanding task conditions." | (Intro, p.490): "automation that relegates the operator to a passive monitoring role could exacerbate vigilance decrement (Warm, Parasuraman, & Matthews, 2008)." | (Discussion): "However, the NASA-TLX workload of 35.8 was well below the scale midpoint of 50" | (Discussion): "The stability of distress across the trial was also unexpected" | (Discussion): "passive fatigue may be an operational issue even when operators are required to perform routine task activities, given sufficient task duration." | (Discussion): "over shorter durations, UAS surveillance tasks may be less vulnerable to decrement than laboratory studies would suggest." | (Conclusion): "The Warm et al. (1996) resource theory does not fully explain the effects observed"

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> Warm et al. 2008 (review, per abstract): vigilance tasks were long assumed undemanding and the decrement attributed to understimulation/low arousal; converging behavioural, neural (transcranial Doppler) and subjective evidence instead supports an attentional resource theory in which vigilance is hard mental work and is stressful. Applies to human-machine systems requiring monitoring, particularly with automated subsystems. Wohleber et al. 2019: in a 2 hr multi-UAS simulation with low cognitive demands, accuracy on the more demanding surveillance task (WR) showed a vigilance decrement, worse with low automation reliability; reliance on automation fell over time; large loss of task engagement; distress (state, not personality traits) predicted poorer performance under demanding conditions. Authors say Warm's resource theory does not fully explain the pattern (workload was modest, distress did not rise).
- **Headline figures:** 
> Wohleber: N = 131 participants; 2 hr task (within a 4 hr session); performance scored per 15-min interval; NASA-TLX workload 35.8 (scale midpoint 50), i.e. low, not high; low-reliability condition WR accuracy "fairly stable for the first 45 min, declined progressively for the next hour, and recovered sharply in the final 15 min" (end effect); typical laboratory decrement happens in first 20-30 min. Comparison cited from another study (Matthews et al. 2014): distress rose by nearly 1 SD during a 1 hr map-display vigil. Warm et al. 2008: no numbers in the abstract.
- **Sample unit and population:** 
> Wohleber: 131 University of Central Florida undergraduate psychology students (50 women, 81 men, mean age 19.86, range 18-31), civilians, untrained, not software engineers; automation reliability manipulated between groups. Warm et al.: literature review of vigilance research, mostly laboratory participants; not software engineers.
- **Observed vs stated:** 
> Mixed. Warm et al. synthesises behavioural performance, neural blood-flow (TCD) measures and subjective workload/stress reports. Wohleber: observed accuracy and automation-dependence behaviour plus self-reported state (Dundee Stress State Questionnaire, NASA-TLX).

### Oversight and load

- **What makes oversight tiring:** 
> Warm et al. (abstract): sustained attention draws on limited attentional resources, so keeping watch is effortful; workload ratings are high and rise with processing demands; vigilance reduces task engagement and raises distress, more so as difficulty rises. Wohleber: automation that makes the operator a passive monitor may worsen the decrement; they also report passive fatigue (big engagement loss) even where workload was low.
- **Consequence for quality:** 
> Wohleber: lower detection/discrimination accuracy on the harder task over time, stronger with unreliable automation; higher distress tied to worse accuracy in demanding conditions; operators increasingly under-used automation over time. Warm et al.: the performance decrement is linked to resource depletion (per abstract).
- **What reduces the load:** 
> Wohleber (practical implications): workload mitigation, supporting operator motivation on long missions, interface design that raises the salience of automation recommendations plus training, possible diagnostic monitoring of stress and fatigue to drive adaptive automation. Authors note task variety/active involvement can counter the decrement (Parsons et al. 2007 eliminated it in a detection-action scenario; this is cited, not tested here). ### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Wohleber: "An obvious limitation is the use of untrained civilian participants rather than Air Force personnel"; a simulation "cannot fully capture operational conditions"; automation "limited in nature" with no test of transparency; variability in event frequency and reliability between blocks added noise; dependence metric does not show how participants reached decisions; operational stressors such as sleep and long hours not captured. Warm et al.: limitations not visible from the abstract.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Wohleber itself cuts against a simple reading: workload was low (NASA-TLX 35.8), distress did not increase, decrement onset was delayed (not the usual 20-30 min), the easier task showed no sustained decrement, and the authors say real UAS tasks "may be less vulnerable to decrement than laboratory studies would suggest". ### Essay use

- **Usable claim:** 
> A 2008 Human Factors review (Warm, Parasuraman and Matthews) concluded that converging behavioural, neural and subjective evidence shows vigilance 'requires hard mental work and is stressful', and that it applies to monitoring of automated systems. A 2019 Human Factors simulation (Wohleber et al., 131 students, 2 hr, multiple drone surveillance with automation) found accuracy on the harder task fell over time, more so with unreliable automation, and people relied on the automation less as time went on. > Do not claim Wohleber found high workload or rising stress (workload was low, distress flat). ### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> Leroy 2009 (from the RePEc abstract summary): across two experiments, people switching between tasks have trouble fully shifting attention. Leaving the earlier task unfinished makes the shift hard and hurts performance on the next task. Finishing a task is not enough by itself; time pressure on the prior task helped people disengage and improved next-task performance. Mark et al. 2008: interruptions, whether of the same or a different topic as the main task, made no difference to disruption cost; interrupted people finished the email task faster with no loss in quality but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. Murty et al. 2022 (snippet only): app and website toggling is frequent and the authors argue the cost comes from how work and tools are designed. NOTE: all three are about knowledge work in general (email, project tasks, office apps), not about AI agents or agent sessions. Any link to juggling several agent sessions is the essay's inference, not something these sources test.
- **Headline figures:** 
- Mark et al.: 48 participants; 3 conditions, 12 emails per condition; interruptions every 2 minutes. Mean time to perform task (minutes, SD): baseline 22.77 (7.60), same-context interruption 20.31 (5.94), different-context 20.60 (4.93); B vs S and B vs D p<.05. Errors per email set: 1.94 vs 1.93 vs 1.84, not significant.

> Mark et al.: task time, errors and email length were observed; stress, frustration, effort and workload were self-reported on NASA-TLX scales. Leroy: experimental performance measures plus attention measures (details not seen). HBR: logged app/website switching (observed by software, per snippet) with the time cost estimated by the authors, not measured per person.

### Oversight and load

- **What makes oversight tiring:** 
> Not about oversight. Leroy's mechanism is attention residue: thinking about the unfinished earlier task persists and competes with the new one, mainly when the earlier task is left incomplete. Mark et al.'s mechanism is compensation: people speed up to make up for lost time, which raises felt stress, frustration, pressure and effort. HBR authors blame fragmented tool and workflow design (snippet).
- **Consequence for quality:** 
> Leroy: worse performance on the next task after switching away from an unfinished one (abstract-level). Mark et al.: no measurable drop in quality (errors, politeness unchanged) over a 12-email block; the cost showed up as stress and effort instead. The authors say they cannot tell whether people cope or the strain grows over longer periods. HBR: time lost (about 9% of time) per snippet, not quality.

### Caveats

- **Limitations stated:** 
> Mark et al.: lab setting ("While laboratory studies are always subject to criticism of ecological validity"), mostly student sample, short 20-minute exposure, and "We cannot say whether people would cope over time or if these measures would only increase." They also note a possibility that interruptions do lengthen task time, but only right after the interruption, which they did not measure. Leroy and HBR limits not seen.
- **Counter evidence:** 
> Mark et al. itself cuts against a simple 'switching hurts output' story: interrupted work was faster with no quality loss; the cost was felt strain. Their interruptions were imposed by a supervisor and were not self-chosen switches, so they differ from a person deliberately cycling between agent sessions. Leroy's residue effect depends on leaving a task unfinished, so a clean hand-off point may avoid it. The HBR per-switch cost (about two seconds) is tiny and counts app changes, not deep task changes; it should not be equated with attention residue. None of the three studies involve AI agents.

### Essay use

- **Usable claim:** 
> Lab studies of knowledge work (not agents) find that moving on from an unfinished task leaves attention on it and hurts performance on the next task (Leroy 2009, abstract-level), and that being interrupted every couple of minutes made people work faster at the price of more stress, frustration and effort, with no quality drop over 12 emails (Mark et al. 2008, 48 students, lab). Applying this to several agent sessions is an inference.
- **Do not use:** 
> The HBR numbers (1,200 switches a day, 9%, four hours a week, five weeks a year) until the article or the underlying Soroco data is opened. Any '23 minutes to refocus' style figure (not in these sources). The 588 billion dollars figure. Any claim that these studies show harm from running multiple agents, or that Leroy's work has been tested on software engineers. Any claim of a quality drop from Mark et al. (they found none).

### Claims

- **Key claims:** 
> No source found sets an evidence-based limit on agents per person. Gallup: span of control is not universal and depends on context; it gives no single optimal number. ### Caveats
