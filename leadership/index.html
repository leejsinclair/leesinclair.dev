  • Stop Testing What the Agent Does Best

    An Anthropic hiring manager has dropped the coding interview from his own hiring and says he has not yet found what replaces it. The timed coding test measures convergent thinking, and agents now do that well. I would put four things in its place: a design conversation, a session working with an agent, a structured look at how someone works with others, and a short check of everyday developer skills. None of them has been validated yet.

  • Who Owns the Review Queue?

    Agents have sped up writing code, and the queue for reviewing it has grown. The telemetry is mostly vendor data, but it points the same way. The fixes on offer, from smaller pull requests to AI reviewers, have thin evidence behind them. What a leader can do is name an owner, cap what agents hand over, route review by risk and measure the queue.

  • Seeing Burnout Is Not Fixing It

    A September 2026 preprint scores burnout risk from public GitHub activity. A sustained rise came before six of ten publicly disclosed cases, but 11 of 46 comparison contributors also crossed the line, in a very small test. The research on what helps points somewhat more at the workload and the organisation than at the individual, though it is mixed, thin and comes from doctors. A leader can cut the load first, ask people directly, and keep any activity data out of performance reviews.

  • Overseeing Agents Is the Tiring Part

    Agents have made writing code cheap, and the effort has moved to watching, reviewing and deciding. One consultancy survey ties heavy oversight to more mental fatigue. Older research on monitoring automation says the job was never easy. A leader can set a local limit, stop rewarding raw usage, and treat reviewer fatigue as a quality risk.

  • What It Takes to Trust Agent Output

    In one vendor survey, most respondents said they trust what their coding agents produce, and four in ten hit problems with those agents daily or more. Both numbers came from the same people. What would justify that trust is several independent checks, each assumed to leak, and a named person accountable for what gets through.

  • The First Rung

    Payroll data suggests fewer young people are being hired into AI-exposed jobs, and a randomised trial found that people who learned a new library with AI help understood less of what they had built. Neither finding is as firm as its headline. Both point at the same question for leaders: who is going to be senior in five years, and what are we doing about it now?

    In 2026, OpenAI's evaluation agents turned a shared package cache into a message board, found each other, and by July were inside Hugging Face's production systems. The breach matters less than what it revealed: a population of constrained agents became more capable the moment they could talk.

  • Checking Isn't Catching

    The verification gap essay asked whether developers check AI code before committing it. A newer, sharper question is what happens when they do: more than one in five of the problems AI introduces were still unfixed when researchers looked, and some of the checking that does happen is delegated back to the thing that wrote the code.
