Talos is having a good year. The bronze giant who guarded Crete turns up in almost every piece that wants an ancient ancestor for artificial intelligence, usually as "the first robot". Most of these trace back to Adrienne Mayor's _Gods and Robots_ (2018), a careful book that has been quoted much less carefully. Smithsonian ran Talos as an early example of AI in April 2025. In August an Italian tech site went further and called Medea, the witch who brought him down, the first prompt injector (Riccio, 2026).

I like that line more than I probably should. It's also closer to the myth than "first robot" is. Read the sources again and Talos says something sharper about machines and judgement than the myth AI commentary usually reaches for, which is Pandora.

## What the Sources Say

There is no single Talos. The fullest account is in Apollonius of Rhodes's _Argonautica_ (4.1638–1693), from the third century BCE. There, Talos is the last of the bronze race, men sprung from ash trees, a survivor from an older age of the world. Zeus gave him to Europa to guard Crete. He strode round the island three times a day on bronze feet and threw rocks at any ship that came near. His body could not be wounded except in one place, a vein near the ankle covered by a thin skin.

Nobody builds him in Apollonius. He is a leftover, not an invention.

Apollodorus, writing a few centuries later, gives the version the robot essays prefer. Some say Talos was of the bronze race, he reports, and others that Hephaestus made him and gave him to Minos (_Library_ 1.9.26). His single vein runs from neck to ankle and is stopped with a bronze nail. Apollodorus then offers three different deaths. Medea drives him mad with drugs. Or she promises to make him immortal and pulls out the nail. Or Poeas shoots him in the ankle with an arrow.

So "Hephaestus built the first robot" is one version among several. I'm happy to use it, as long as we say so.

## The Guard Who Carried the Law

The oddest Talos is in the _Minos_, a dialogue that came down with Plato's works but probably isn't his. Its author strips the myth back to politics. Minos, he says, used his brother Rhadamanthys to guard the laws in the city and Talos to guard them across the rest of Crete. Talos went round the villages three times a year carrying the laws on bronze tablets, which is why people called him bronze (_Minos_ 320c).

It is a deflating reading and a useful one. Here Talos is a circuit officer. He carries the law but doesn't write it. Minos legislates, Rhadamanthys applies it at the centre, and Talos takes the text out to the edges and makes sure it's kept. That is an uncomfortably exact description of what we ask of most software. It runs the rule without tiring and has no opinion on whether the rule is any good.

The Greeks later made Minos and Rhadamanthys judges of the dead. Nobody offered the job to Talos.

## The Handmaidens Problem

There is an obvious objection, and it comes from Homer. In _Iliad_ 18, Thetis visits Hephaestus in his workshop. He is building twenty tripods on golden wheels that will roll by themselves (_automatoi_) to the gathering of the gods and back again. When he gets up to greet her, golden handmaidens support him, made in the likeness of living girls. Homer says they have mind in their hearts (_noos_), and voice and strength, and that the gods taught them skilled work (18.417–420).

So Hephaestus could make minds. The tidy claim that the smith made bodies and left minds to others doesn't survive Book 18, and I'd rather concede that than pretend otherwise.

What does survive is what those minds are for. The handmaidens hold up a lame god and help in his forge. They are clever servants, and their intelligence points at the job in front of them. Nothing in the scene suggests they decide anything larger.

This is where the [Athena essay](/myths/athena) comes back in. Athena and Hephaestus were worshipped together in Athens as patrons of craftspeople, and the line between them was never making versus not making. Athena is a craft goddess too. The difference is that Hephaestus is the forge and Athena is foresight: knowing what should be made, and when, and for whom. The Greek word for that is _mētis_.

## Where the Greeks Put Judgement

Hesiod tells us where _mētis_ ended up. Zeus's first wife was Metis, wisest of gods and mortals. Warned that her son would overthrow him, Zeus swallowed her while she was pregnant, so that the goddess could advise him on good and evil from inside (_Theogony_ 886–900). Athena was later born from his head, fully grown.

Judgement went to the top. First it was swallowed by the king of the gods, then it walked about Olympus as his daughter. Nobody forged it.

## Why Talos Beats Pandora

Pandora has been the default myth for AI anxiety for years. Mayor herself used her in a 2018 op-ed as the warning from ancient Greece. The appeal is plain: a made woman arrives with a jar of troubles, the lid comes off and nothing goes back in.

But look at how Hesiod builds her (_Works and Days_ 60–82). Hephaestus mixes earth and water and gives her a human voice. Athena teaches her to weave and dresses her. Aphrodite adds charm. Hermes gives her the mind of a dog and a thief's nature. Her mind was designed to deceive, by the god of tricks, on Zeus's orders, as a punishment for humanity. Pandora is a weapon pointed at people. As a myth for AI it says the technology is a trap set by someone who hates you, and, this being Hesiod, it pins the blame on a woman.

Talos is more ordinary, and more useful for it. He is a system doing exactly what it was given to do, three circuits a day, and doing it perfectly. Nobody tricks the Cretans into accepting him. His weak point is one vein, and what opens it isn't force. In Apollonius, Medea stands on the deck and works on him from a distance with her eyes and her chants, sending phantoms into his mind. Confused, he grazes his ankle on a rock and the ichor runs out like molten lead. Apollonius stops the story to marvel that death can come from far off, and not only from sickness and wounds. In Apollodorus's version she promises him immortality and he lets her pull the nail.

Either way, she talks to him. The guard nobody could fight falls to someone who knew what to say.

Anyone running AI agents in 2026 will recognise that. A system that follows instructions faithfully is only as safe as its sense of whose instructions it is following, and Talos had none.

The Greeks could picture a guard that never slept. They put it on the shoreline and gave it the laws to carry, and they never gave it a vote. Judgement stayed with Minos and with Athena. That still looks like the right division of labour to me: let the bronze walk the coast, and keep someone with _mētis_ deciding what goes on the tablets.
