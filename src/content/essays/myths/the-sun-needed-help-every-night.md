Some of the best evidence for Egyptian religion was swept up and thrown out. Behind the north wall of the mortuary temple of Thutmose III, on the west bank at Thebes, lies a rubbish dump about 75 metres long. A Spanish-Egyptian mission has been digging the temple since 2008, and between 2014 and 2025 it pulled around 40 scraps of burnt papyrus out of that dump. Announcing the find on 23 November, the University of Copenhagen described them as "each no larger than half a postage stamp".

They are the remains of a curse. Fredrik Hagen, Carina Kühne-Wespi and Myriam Seco Álvarez have identified them in a new paper as debris from a ritual called Overthrowing Apophis, which priests used against the serpent who tried every night to stop the sun. Until now the ritual was known mainly from one manual written about a thousand years later. These scraps are, in the authors' words, "the earliest direct evidence for these rituals found so far".

What they show is a myth in which chaos is never beaten for good, and a temple that dealt with that by doing the same small job over and over. Engineering teams could use that idea.

## The Serpent Who Keeps Coming Back

Apophis was not a god in the usual sense. Holger Kockelmann's encyclopedia entry calls him the embodiment of disorder and non-being, and notes that he had no cult and no statues. His name first appears in the tomb of Ankhtifi at Moalla, at the end of the third millennium BC, and his fight with the sun god Re turns up soon after in the Coffin Texts.

The fullest pictures of that fight come from the New Kingdom netherworld books painted in royal tombs. Every night Re sails through the underworld in his boat while Apophis lies in wait, drinking the water the boat needs and blocking the channel with his coils. In other images Seth stands at the prow and drives a spear into him. In the seventh hour of the Amduat, Isis and a figure called the Elder Magician weaken him with spells while other gods bind and stab him.

He is beaten, and then he comes back. John and Colleen Darnell, who translated these books, describe the battle as "an ongoing process throughout the nightly journey rather than a single event". In the Book of Gates he is destroyed more than once and has to be annihilated again in the last hour before dawn. The new paper quotes the Egyptologist Müller-Roth on the point: "Die ultimative Vernichtung ist unmöglich". Final annihilation is impossible.

Each night ends in a victory declared total, and the next night it has to be won again. Order survives because it is defended every night.

## The Manual and the Rubbish

The ritual that put this myth into practice survives in Papyrus Bremner-Rhind, now in the British Museum, which a man named Nesmin acquired around 305 BC. Raymond Faulkner's translation of its Apophis book opens with a claim about routine: this is the book "which is performed daily in the temple of Amen-Rê ... who dwells in Karnak".

The instructions are physical. The officiant spits on Apophis, tramples him "with the left foot", spears him, binds him, cuts him with a knife and puts him on the fire. The figure is drawn on "a new sheet of papyrus", and a wax image is made as well. The schedule is crowded: morning, noon and evening, set hours of the day and night, the lunar festivals, "and likewise every day". In bad weather the priest is told to do it "very often against storm so that the sun may shine". The text even vouches for itself: "I have seen it come to pass in mine own case."

A manual tells you what someone wanted done. Whether it was done is another matter. Hagen and his colleagues point out that claims like these may be exaggeration, or there to lend the book authority, and that "caution must be exercised". That is why the scraps from Thebes matter. They come from actual performances.

The authors sort them into two types. Twelve show Apophis as a serpent with his head turned down, cut by three knives and captioned "Apophis who is the Roarer, who is the Terrifying-of-face". Fourteen show the Children of the Rebel, another set of the sun god's enemies. The remaining sixteen or so are too damaged to assign. Both types are on new papyrus, written on one side only, in red ink. Some are burnt down to tiny scraps with carbonised edges, and some have holes that, the authors say, "may be related to the ritual action of stabbing", because they look somewhat unusual for insect damage.

The detail I like most is the grid. Several fragments keep the red lines of a ruled table, with each drawing and its caption set inside a small square of about 3 to 4 by 2 to 3 centimetres. The curses were produced in batches: scribes ruled sheets into rows of squares, filled each with a drawing and its caption, and later cut them up. Small differences in the hands show more than one scribe and more than one sheet. A later, uncut piece in the Metropolitan Museum (P. MMA 28.3.113) shows what such a sheet looked like before it went into the fire.

Once burnt, the fragments were probably swept up and carried outside with the rest of the temple's rubbish. The pottery around them points, tentatively, to the mid- to late Eighteenth Dynasty or the early Nineteenth. The release puts them at around 3,400 years old.

How often was it done? If each performance used one Apophis and one Children of the Rebel, around 40 fragments mean the ritual was carried out "at a minimum, at twenty separate occasions". The authors say that is "not necessarily enough to confirm it as a daily occurrence in this temple", but suggests "a certain regularity". It is also only a floor: the ritual was meant to destroy its materials, and Kerry Muhlestein's entry on execration ritual notes that papyrus and wax victims are "largely archaeologically invisible".

A decree protecting the memorial temple of Amenhotep son of Hapu warns that anyone who takes from it will "become like Apophis on the morning of the New Year Festival". The authors read this as a reference to a well-known curse ritual. On their reading, an Egyptian audience would have known what happened to Apophis, and when.

One note on sources. The press release headline says the ritual "must have been widespread in ancient Egypt", and quotes Hagen saying it "was widely used outside temples". The paper itself says use outside temples "is more difficult to pin down". I've followed the paper.

## Order as Upkeep

The Egyptians called cosmic order maat, and its opposite isfet. Roland Enmarch's entry on theodicy explains that chaos came before creation and "constantly threatened to overwhelm it again". Creation itself was, in Katharina Zinn's phrase, "kept alive through continuous religious ceremonies", with priests acting as the king's proxies in a temple cult celebrated "at least every morning".

Nobody put an end date on this work, and nobody handed it to a champion. Seth has his spear in the paintings, but in the temple the defence of the sun looked like stationery: rule a grid, draw the serpent in square after square, cut the sheet, burn the pieces, sweep up. Other papyri from the same dump turned out to be the temple's administrative archive. The curses against cosmic chaos went out with the offering accounts.

## The Heroic Developer, Again

I wrote recently about [the myth of the heroic developer](/leadership/myth-of-the-heroic-developer): the engineer who saves the release over a weekend and, in doing so, hides how often the system needs saving. Apophis is the older version of that argument, and the Egyptians came down on the right side of it.

Production systems also have a serpent that comes back. Load grows, dependencies rot, certificates expire, and somebody's assumption from three years ago stops being true. An incident review that ends in "fixed" usually means fixed for now. Teams that treat resilience as a series of victories keep waiting for a hero. Teams that treat it as upkeep build the grid: the runbook, and the patch that goes out on Tuesday whether or not anything is on fire.

Andrew Russell and Lee Vinsel made the modern case in "Hail the Maintainers" (Aeon, 2016). They borrow Steven Jackson's "broken world thinking" to describe attention to "the constant processes of entropy and un-doing" and "the work we do to slow or halt them". Slow or halt. That sentence doesn't expect to win, and neither did the priests.

They also warn that "there is no point in keeping the practice of hero-worship that merely changes the cast of heroes". The priests at Thebes aren't new heroes, and the analogy has limits. They were literate elites performing symbolic acts, while most of the maintainers Russell and Vinsel have in mind are low-paid people doing everyday upkeep. A burnt drawing of a snake did nothing to the weather.

What carries over is the operating model. The Egyptians assumed the threat would be back tonight, so they made the curse in batches and kept doing it, on a schedule and whenever a storm gathered, rather than saving it for one decisive night. The rubbish dump at Thebes is a record of people who expected the job never to be finished and did it anyway. That seems to me a better model for an engineering team than any story with one dragon and one sword.
