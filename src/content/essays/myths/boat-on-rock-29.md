At the end of September a piece on Ancient Origins asked whether a carving in Azerbaijan shows the voyage behind Jason and the Argonauts. The carving is on Rock 29 at Gobustan, a field of limestone boulders above the Caspian. The author, Constantinos Ragazas, lists what he sees there: "a large oared vessel, water-like markings, armed figures moving across rough ground, and an unusual raised terminal at one end of the boat". His question is whether "the original expedition" was "carved into stone thousands of years before it became Greece's most famous adventure" (Ragazas, 2026).

I should say what I have and haven't read. The article sits behind a paywall after its opening paragraphs, and the opening is all I can test. It promises evidence leading "from a cave behind the engraving to a transformed Caspian shoreline and a possible route across the Caucasus". That chain is in the part I couldn't see. Ragazas's author page describes him as a retired mathematics educator and independent researcher. I found no journal paper behind the piece and no response from anyone who works on the site.

So this is a test of the idea as stated. It is a good one to test, because it asks what it would take for a picture with no words to be the origin of a story.

## One Rock, Many Hands

Gobustan has more than 7,000 engravings on over 1,000 rocks, made from the Upper Palaeolithic to historical times (Sigari et al., 2025). Rock 29 stands on the upper terrace of the hill called Böyükdaş. With Rocks 30 and 31 it forms a shelter known as Ana Zaga, the Mother Cave.

The proposal reads the boat, the water marks and the armed figures as a composition. Ragazas cites the rock art specialist Dario Sigari for where the rock sits. Sigari's paper, from fieldwork in 2015, also describes how the figures sit on each other. The boat on the south side uses a natural crack in the stone as its keel, its prow points east towards the Caspian, and it carries a sun symbol. He also notes that "the boat overlaps both a zoomorph and two male anthropomorphs" (Sigari, 2017).

The boat was cut over two of the men. Whoever carved the ship found those figures already there.

That is ordinary at Gobustan. The 2025 paper, by the team that excavated at Ana Zaga from 2021 to 2024, calls Rocks 29 and 30 a "dense palimpsest" produced by "the long reuse of the rock surfaces". The Azerbaijani archaeologist Malahat Farajova, who has dated the site's deposits, spreads the figures on that south face over five periods in her chart, though she puts some of the hunters in the same period as the boat (Farajova, 2021). So parts of the face may belong together. But a scene needs all its parts to be made together, and nothing shows that the boat, the water marks and the armed figures were.

## How Old Is the Boat?

Nobody has dated it directly, or any other carving at Gobustan. The radiocarbon dates come from the floors of the shelters. Sigari and his colleagues write that "the real link with the parietal engravings is missing". Farajova thinks the layers can date the walls. When ICOMOS assessed the site for UNESCO in 2007 it wrote that "a well-analysed and dated sequence for images on the property is yet to be achieved".

What exists are estimates, and they disagree. Sigari ties the boats to old Caspian shorelines and puts the first ones on the upper terraces between 16,000 and 14,000 years ago, with the sun-prow style arriving later and undated. Farajova works from carved stones found in dated layers and writes of "boats depicted 7800 years ago" (Farajova, 2023). An older estimate by Jafarzade, who put a boat on another rock in the second millennium BC, is one she reports without adopting. By the Bronze Age, on Farajova's account, the sea had dropped and people had moved down to the lower terraces, where they carved goats with large curved horns.

The proposal says "thousands of years" and it is right about that. Take the younger estimate. The earliest securely dated text I found that puts the voyage on the Phasis among the Colchians is Pindar's fourth Pythian ode, from 462 BC. Between Farajova's boat and Pindar there are more than five thousand years and a mountain range, with nothing in the gap.

## The Unusual Prow

The proposal calls the raised terminal unusual. The part I could read doesn't say what it is. If it means the upturned end of the hull, that is the usual form in Sigari's typology: "The prows are characteristically engraved in a hooked shape." Sun symbols at the prow recur across the site.

The armed figures are no discovery either. Farajova already describes the south side of Rock 29 as "the plot with boats, and hunters armed with bows and arrows". The wavy lines on that face, which may be the proposal's water marks, she matches to a carved stone from a layer she dates to roughly 10,000 years ago, older than her date for the boat. On the east side of the rock, lines run from hollows at the top down through the hunter figures to the ground. She describes them alongside a figure she takes to be in ritual dress, and doesn't mention water.

## A Different Sea

Then there is the geography. Once the Greek versions name a real place, it is on the Black Sea. Apollonius has the Argo row into the Phasis with the Caucasus on the left, and says that "Colchian Aea lies at the edge of Pontus and of the world".

The early sources are vaguer, which might look like room to move the voyage. The _Odyssey_ says only that the Argo sailed "from Aeetes" (12.70). M. L. West concluded that in the earliest poetry "the mythical Aia was not yet located at Colchis", and placed it on the northern Black Sea instead (West, 2007). Mimnermus, in the seventh century BC, puts the city of Aeetes where the sun's rays lie in a golden chamber "beside the lips of Oceanus". Neither reading points to the Caspian. Mimnermus points to the edge of the world, where the sun comes up, and that is not somewhere a rowing boat can reach.

Ancient writers did carry Jason to the Caspian. Strabo reports a tradition that he "pressed on from there as far as the Caspian Sea" (11.4.8). But he gets there from Colchis. The Caspian is an extra leg added to a story already fixed on the Black Sea.

As for a route across the Caucasus, Strabo and Pliny describe one. Goods came over the Caspian, up the river Cyrus, then overland to the Phasis: four days by wagon road in Strabo (11.2.17), five days at most in Pliny (6.52). It is a road over a watershed, and the oldest report behind it comes from Patrocles, who explored the Caspian for the Seleucid kings. W. W. Tarn doubted the trade ever ran at all: "there is no evidence that it ever existed" (Tarn, 1938). Whatever one makes of Tarn, nothing here is older than Alexander's successors.

## We Have Been Here Before

Gobustan's boats have been given a foreign story once already. In 1995 Thor Heyerdahl wrote that its ship petroglyphs were "identical to those of the ancestors of the Vikings" and tied them to Snorri's account of Odin and the land of the Aser. The magazine that published him later recorded that the idea "came under heavy criticism in Norway" and "has yet to be proven".

The method is the same both times. Take a boat, find a later written legend with a boat in it, and propose the first as the source of the second. People who live beside water draw boats, and people everywhere tell stories about voyages, so a match between the two tells you very little.

The Greeks did this themselves. Strabo passes on a report that mountain people above Colchis caught river gold in troughs "lined with fleeces; and hence the fable of the golden fleece" (11.2.19, in Hamilton and Falconer's translation). He marks it as hearsay.

## What It Would Take

I don't think the question is foolish. A myth can keep a memory of a real place. But a carving can only be the origin of a story if three things hold. Its parts have to be one picture, and it has to date from a time that could feed the story. Something also has to carry it from the rock to the poet.

On the published evidence Rock 29 has none of the three. The stone itself cuts against the first, the second is unknown, and nothing I could read fills the third. I wrote in the [Gilgamesh essay](/history/machine-at-the-edge-of-gilgamesh) that coherence is not evidence, and this is the same trap without a machine in it.

What the rock does record is plainer and, to me, better. People lived beside a sea that rose and fell, and drew boats on the same stone for thousands of years, each generation cutting across the last. Sigari concludes that the stories told on these rocks "can never be deciphered". Medea and the bronze guard she brought down, the subject of the [Talos essay](/myths/talos), belong to the Greeks. The boat on Rock 29 belongs to whoever stood there and looked east at the water.
