The Epic of Gilgamesh—preserved most fully in the Standard Babylonian recension attributed to Sîn-lēqi-unninni (c. 1200 BCE) and rooted in earlier Sumerian poems whose oral roots reach back to the Third Dynasty of Ur (c. 2100 BCE) but whose earliest surviving manuscripts date to the Old Babylonian period (c. 19th–18th century BCE)—traces the transformation of a king who begins as a semi-divine tyrant and ends as a fully human ruler (Tigay, 1982).

## Divine Power and Human Limits

The epic introduces Gilgamesh as two-thirds divine and one-third human, an unusually pronounced ratio for a Mesopotamian king (George, 2003). His mother, Ninsun, is divine; his father, Lugalbanda, is a former king of Uruk whose status is itself unstable across traditions. The Akkadian epic sometimes speaks of him in divine terms, while the older Sumerian King List names an entirely different father for Gilgamesh, a "phantom lord of Kulaba" (Jacobsen, 1976; George, 2003). The two-thirds/one-third formula, in other words, is less a settled genealogical fact than a theological claim the epic asserts over a genuinely tangled inheritance.

Gilgamesh's early rule is marked by excess: he exhausts the young men of Uruk and violates social norms by taking any woman he desires. This is not a formal "right," but a depiction of unchecked royal power. The gods respond by creating Enkidu—a wild man formed from clay by Aruru—to counterbalance Gilgamesh's destructive force.

## Enkidu: The Necessary Counterpart

Enkidu embodies the natural world Gilgamesh has abandoned. Their initial clash in Uruk ends in mutual recognition, forming one of the earliest recorded literary friendships. In many interpretations, Enkidu "completes" Gilgamesh: he tempers the king's arrogance and anchors him emotionally. This reading is not stated explicitly in the text but emerges from the narrative structure, and it is closely argued by Abusch (2001), who traces how Gilgamesh's identity as "man, hero, king, god" is only resolved through his relationship with Enkidu and, later, through Enkidu's death.

## Heroic Exploits and the Illusion of Invincibility

Together they undertake the Cedar Forest expedition, slaying Humbaba (Huwawa), the guardian appointed by Enlil. Gilgamesh seeks šumu—a name that will outlast death. Yet the killing of a divinely sanctioned guardian foreshadows the cost of transgression. Even beings empowered by gods can die, hinting at the fragility of Gilgamesh's imagined invulnerability.

Their next act—killing the Bull of Heaven sent by Ishtar—pushes the gods to decree Enkidu's death. This moment is the epic's emotional and philosophical fulcrum.

## Enkidu's Death: The Shattering of Hubris

Enkidu's slow, agonizing death forces Gilgamesh to confront mortality directly. He sees in Enkidu's body his own inevitable fate. Many scholars argue that "part of Gilgamesh dies with Enkidu"—a metaphorical death of his divine arrogance and youthful certainty. Abusch (2001) makes a version of this argument directly, contending that Gilgamesh, as both man and god, must ultimately "learn to die" precisely because his bond with Enkidu forces the human half of his nature into view. This interpretation aligns with the epic's thematic arc, though it is not a literal textual claim.

Gilgamesh abandons his royal garments and wanders the wilderness, mirroring Enkidu's earlier movement from wildness to civilization. His quest shifts from fame to survival: how can a man escape death?

## The Futility of Immortality

Gilgamesh's journey to Utnapishtim, the flood survivor granted eternal life, exposes the limits of human striving. He fails the test of sleeplessness—succumbing instantly to exhaustion—and learns that immortality is not a reward for heroism but a singular divine exception.

Utnapishtim reveals a plant of rejuvenation, "The Old Man Becomes Young Again" (Foster, 2001). Gilgamesh retrieves it, intending to share it with Uruk's elders—a sign of his emerging civic responsibility. But a serpent steals the plant, shedding its skin as it departs, symbolizing renewal denied to humans.

## Return to Uruk: Human Achievement as Immortality

Empty-handed but transformed, Gilgamesh returns to Uruk. The epic closes with him inviting Urshanabi to behold the city's walls—its brickwork, its foundations, its temples. This ring composition mirrors the opening lines but with a changed consciousness: Gilgamesh now understands that human immortality lies not in escaping death but in building enduring institutions, communities, and cultural memory.

In accepting his mortality, Gilgamesh becomes fully human—and fully king.
