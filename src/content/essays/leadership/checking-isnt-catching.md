The verification gap essay asked a simple question: do developers check AI-generated code before they commit it? The answer was a 48-point gap between stated distrust and stated checking, and it held up across surveys, telemetry and non-software research alike. That's still true. But it leaves a second, harder question sitting underneath it, one the original framing couldn't reach because it was asking about an action, not an outcome: when checking happens, does it catch anything?

A 2026 study of AI-authored commits across more than six thousand GitHub repositories gives the first clean answer, and it isn't reassuring. Researchers ran static analysis before and after every commit from five major coding assistants, Copilot, Claude, Cursor, Gemini and Devin, to see what each one introduced. More than 15% of commits from every single assistant introduced at least one new issue. The part that matters more: of the issues introduced, more than one in five were still sitting in the codebase's latest revision at the time of the study, meaning nobody had fixed them since. Security issues were worse than the average, with assistants introducing new vulnerabilities at about one and a half times the rate they fixed existing ones. This is a study of whether looking actually worked, not just whether anyone looked.

**When correction happens, it often doesn't look like correction.**

A separate six-month study tracked five hundred and eight AI-generated files against an equal number of human-written ones, logging every follow-up commit for half a year after creation. The headline finding sounds almost reassuring: 83% of the maintenance work on AI-generated files was done by humans, not by another AI pass. But the detail underneath it is the interesting part. AI-generated files received noticeably fewer commits explicitly labelled as bug fixes than human-written files did, and correspondingly more commits labelled as feature work or refactoring. The researchers' own reading is that problems in AI-generated code aren't getting caught and logged as bugs so much as absorbed into later feature and refactor work, fixed, perhaps, but never named as a fix. A defect that gets quietly folded into the next feature commit doesn't show up in any bug count, any incident report, or any retrospective about AI code quality. It just disappears into the normal churn of the codebase, correction without an audit trail.

**Some of the checking is delegated to the thing being checked.**

A behavioural study published this year coded seventy-five thousand real developer messages across more than eleven thousand AI chat sessions in Cursor and Copilot, the actual conversations developers have with AI tools while coding, not a survey about them. Developers explicitly corrected AI output that had drifted from what they wanted in 7.21% of messages. That's the bigger number. The smaller, more unsettling one sits right next to it: in 3.99% of messages, validation work like code review or runtime checking was handed straight to the AI itself rather than performed independently by the developer. It's a minority of sessions, but it describes something the correction rate doesn't, work that looks like checking but only checks the model against itself. The paper's own warning is the sharpest sentence to come out of this entire research pass: when diagnosis, comprehension and validation are all delegated to the same tool that wrote the code, "the assistant may become the only judge of both what the code does and whether it is correct, with no independent check on its own errors." That's not a trust problem or a checking-rate problem. It's a structural one: the verification gap essay assumed a human was on the other end of the check. In a small but real share of sessions, the other end of the check is the same model.

**Confidence that you could catch a problem and actually catching one are different things, and the gap shows up at the organisation level too, not just the individual one.**

GitLab's 2026 accountability survey found 87% of organisations confident they could trace an AI-generated code's role in a production incident within 24 hours. Among organisations that had actually had such an incident, only 34% could do it. That's a fifty-three point gap between stated confidence and demonstrated capability, and it's a direct echo of the original verification gap's belief-versus-action split, except this version sits one level up. The overestimation here isn't about individual diligence. It's about an organisation's confidence in its own tooling and audit trail, confidence that only gets tested when an incident actually happens.

That same pattern of self-assessment running ahead of reality shows up again in a disagreement between two of the field's most-cited 2026 reports. DORA's research argues that organisations with strong engineering foundations are protected from AI's downsides, that good platforms and good practices act as a buffer. Faros AI's telemetry, pulled from twenty-two thousand developers' actual commit, review and incident history rather than from survey responses, finds no such protection: high-performing teams show the same downstream quality deterioration as everyone else once AI adoption rises. DORA's finding is built from what organisations say about themselves. Faros' is built from what their systems recorded. When a report built on self-report and a report built on telemetry disagree this directly, on this specific question, that disagreement is itself evidence for the thesis: the people closest to a system are consistently the worst-positioned to judge it dispassionately, and that includes people judging their own employer's resilience, not just their own code.

Even the research designed specifically to correct this kind of self-assessment bias isn't immune to it. METR's widely cited 2025 trial found developers were measurably 19% slower with AI assistance while believing, before and after the study, that they'd been faster. Its 2026 follow-up, intended to see whether that gap had narrowed, hit a problem baked into its own design: the developers most convinced AI was helping them increasingly declined to take part, or withheld the specific tasks most likely to show AI's benefit, rather than risk having that belief tested against a no-AI condition. Between thirty and fifty percent of surveyed developers admitted doing exactly this. METR now describes its own newer data as "only very weak evidence." That's not a flaw unique to METR's method. It's what happens whenever you try to measure how well people check their own work: the people most confident in the answer are the ones most likely to route around the question.

None of this says checking is pointless, or that the original verification gap doesn't matter. It says the gap has a second layer underneath it that stated-distrust and stated-checking surveys can't see. A developer who dutifully reviews every pull request is still operating inside a system where more than one in five of the problems that get through never get fixed, where correction often gets logged as something other than correction, and where the validation step is sometimes quietly handed to the tool that needs validating. Closing the first gap, getting people to check more, doesn't automatically close the second one. What would: tracking not just whether code gets reviewed but whether review finds anything, treating a bug smoothed into a refactor commit as a near-miss worth logging rather than invisible work, and building the kind of traceability that a real incident tests rather than the kind an org chart assumes exists. The verification gap was about whether anyone looks. This one is about whether looking, as currently practised, actually works.
