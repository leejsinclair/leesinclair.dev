---
title: The no-AI control group is getting harder to keep
date: 2026-10-12
---

The best-known trial of AI coding tools with experienced engineers is METR's from 2025. Sixteen open-source developers worked through 246 tasks, each one randomly assigned to allow AI or forbid it. Tasks took 19% longer when AI was allowed, and the developers came away believing it had cut their time by about 20%. The design was clean. The same people did both kinds of work, and the answer came from time on the task, not from opinion.

In February METR said it was changing that design. The follow-up started in August 2025 with 57 developers and more than 800 tasks, and the main reason it gave was not one I expected: more developers were choosing not to take part because they did not want to work without AI. METR gives no count. Among those who did take part, 30% to 50% said when surveyed that they were holding back some tasks for the same reason. One put it this way: "I avoid issues like AI can finish things in just 2 hours, but I have to spend 20 hours."

It's easy to turn this into "developers won't work without AI", and some of the commentary did. METR's claim is smaller. People withheld some tasks, and it says the selection seems to affect "a minority share of developers and of tasks". It had also cut the pay from $150 an hour to $50, which it says "also likely contributed". The new numbers lean towards a speed-up (tasks took an estimated 18% less time for the ten returning developers, and 4% less for the new ones), but both ranges include zero. METR calls the data "only very weak evidence" for how much things have improved since early 2025.

What stays with me is which tasks went missing. They were the ones where developers expected AI to help most, so the study lost the cases with the largest expected effect before any task was assigned.

Control groups have slipped before, in other ways. A Copilot field experiment at Microsoft ended in spring 2023, in its authors' words, "as growing awareness of AI-assisted coding tools led the control group participants to seek access to Copilot". Anthropic's trial, the one I wrote about in [The First Rung](/leadership/the-first-rung), had 35% non-compliance with its no-AI rule in the first two pilots. It held in the main study only after a change of platform and a check of screen recordings.

I can't tell from any of this whether it's dependence or plain good sense. Nobody wants to spend 20 hours on what they believe is a 2-hour job, and I found no survey that asks developers whether they'd work without the tools.

For anyone running a team the practical point is the same either way. Of the trials I looked at, the ones that held on to a control group were short sessions on fixed tasks, a few hours at most, and even one of those had to police it. That is a long way from real work. For work that runs over months the options are weaker: a staggered rollout, where the comparison lasts only weeks, self-report, which the 2025 trial showed can point the wrong way, or a comparison between people who took up the tool and people who didn't. If you want a baseline for your own team, it is likely to be easier to get now than in a year.
