Heroic Greatness and the Corruption of the Soul: Beowulf and Gilgamesh

Beowulf begins as an admirable warrior and Gilgamesh as an oppressive king, yet both epics arrive at the same question: what happens when a society's highest virtues become insufficient for the world that society must preserve.

TL;DR

Beowulf's heroic virtue tips into the pride (oferhygd) that leaves his kingdom exposed, while Gilgamesh's tyranny softens into humane, civic greatness: two opposite routes to the same question about a society's highest virtues.

  • Hrothgar's sermon warns the young Beowulf against pride decades before the aging king's fatal confrontation with the dragon repeats exactly that pattern.
  • The dragon's hoard, and Beowulf's retainers fleeing the final battle, expose how fragile a community built around one exceptional warrior really is.
  • Gilgamesh runs the opposite arc: a failed, oppressive king is humanised by Enkidu's death and ends by building lasting civic institutions instead.
  • Sutton Hoo and the Staffordshire Hoard confirm the poem's anxiety about treasure divorced from social use reflects real Anglo-Saxon practice, not just poetic imagination.

Beowulf and The Epic of Gilgamesh both centre on men whose strength, courage and authority set them apart. Neither poem is content to celebrate that power. Both ask what greatness is worth in the face of death, and how personal virtue squares with public responsibility.

Their heroes travel in opposite directions. Gilgamesh starts as a ruler whose power has turned oppressive, and his experiences slowly make him more human. Beowulf starts as an admirable warrior, but his later life raises a harder question: can the virtues that make a hero great turn destructive when they are bound too tightly to reputation and honour?

This essay works through that comparison using the surviving texts and the scholarship on both epics. It also asks why stories like these last. The answer offered here is that they preserve a culture’s ideals while exposing the cracks in them.

A Necessary Distinction: Corruption, Tragedy, and the Heroic Ideal

First, it helps to separate what the poem actually shows from what a modern reader can infer.

Beowulf does not straightforwardly portray its protagonist as a morally corrupt man. He is repeatedly presented as courageous, generous, and committed to his people. The poem’s tragedy is more subtle: it places admirable heroic virtues within a social system that can produce destructive outcomes.

The strongest version of the argument, therefore, is not that Beowulf becomes evil. It is that the heroic ideal may contain a dangerous vulnerability: a man can become so committed to honour and the preservation of his reputation that he cannot adequately distinguish personal courage from the responsibilities of leadership.

This reading sits between two long-standing positions in Beowulf criticism. Margaret Goldsmith’s The Mode and Meaning of Beowulf argued much more forcefully that the poem condemns its hero. She read the final battle through an Augustinian lens, where attachment to worldly treasure is a kind of avarice and Beowulf’s death its consequence. Edward Irving’s A Reading of Beowulf pushed back against importing doctrine like this, insisting the poem be read on its own heroic terms rather than judged against a theology it never states. The reading here stops short of Goldsmith’s avarice and damnation. It shares her sense that something in Beowulf’s final choice falls short of his situation, a question Irving’s approach sets aside.

This interpretation is supported by the poem’s treatment of the dragon, the contrast between Beowulf and Hrothgar, and the final scenes involving Wiglaf and the Geats.

Beowulf and the Moral Ambiguity of Heroic Culture

The poem’s opening establishes a world in which kingship (cyning-dōm) and heroic reputation are closely connected. The Danish king Scyld Scefing is praised for his ability to acquire power and secure the loyalty of his people. Later, Hrothgar’s great hall, Heorot, embodies the social order that a good king is expected to maintain.

The hall is more than a building. It is the centre of communal life, where the king distributes treasure, warriors receive honour, and bonds of loyalty are renewed. Grendel’s attacks threaten this entire social system.

Beowulf’s arrival therefore has a public significance. He does not merely seek a personal contest; he offers his strength to restore a damaged community. His victories over Grendel and Grendel’s mother establish him as an exemplary warrior.

Yet the poem also introduces a warning through Hrothgar’s speech after Beowulf’s victory. Hrothgar tells the young hero that a powerful man can become a victim of pride (oferhygd) and that worldly success is temporary. The speech is frequently read as a central reflection on the dangers of pride, wealth, and the instability of earthly achievement.

The warning is important because it is not delivered by a weak or failed man. Hrothgar is a successful king who has experienced both greatness and loss. He understands that heroic success does not guarantee lasting security.

The poem thus establishes two related ideals:

  • The warrior must be courageous and capable of extraordinary action.
  • The ruler must possess wisdom and recognise the limits of worldly power.

The tragedy of Beowulf’s later life emerges from the tension between these ideals.

The Young Warrior and the Older King

Beowulf’s transformation from warrior to king is one of the poem’s most significant structural features.

As a young man, he can measure success through direct action. He travels to Denmark, confronts Grendel, and defeats his enemies through exceptional physical strength. His reputation grows because his deeds are visible and their benefits are immediate.

As king, however, his responsibilities change. He must preserve the kingdom, maintain alliances, protect his people, and ensure continuity after his death. The qualities that made him an exceptional warrior are no longer sufficient by themselves.

This distinction has been explored in scholarship on the poem’s heroic and kingly values. The relationship between the lord and his retainers (comitatus) depends upon reciprocal obligations: the king protects and rewards his followers, while the warriors provide loyalty and military service. The heroic system is therefore not simply individualistic. It is a social structure built around obligations and reputation.

The older Beowulf remains a generous ruler. His problem is not that he has abandoned duty. It is that the poem asks whether his understanding of duty has become too closely tied to the heroic model of personal confrontation.

The dragon presents a threat to the kingdom, but Beowulf chooses to face it largely through the same mode of action that defined his youth. He will confront the monster personally, despite his age and despite the fact that the kingdom’s future depends upon him.

His courage hasn’t gone anywhere. The question is whether it still fits the role he now holds.

The Dragon and the Corruption of Heroic Values

The dragon reveals more than any other figure in the poem, because it mirrors values at the heart of the heroic world.

It guards a hoard built up over generations: treasure tied to the past, to dead peoples and the remains of a vanished society. The hoard has value, but it also shows what wealth becomes when it is cut off from living relationships.

The dragon’s attack begins when someone steals a cup from the hoard. Its revenge is out of all proportion. It burns the land, destroys homes and threatens the whole social order of the Geats.

Beowulf decides to face it himself. Partly this is duty: he must defend his people. But before the fight he dwells on his past victories and says he wants to win glory in this last contest. That places the battle inside the danger Hrothgar warned him about decades earlier, the oferhygd that lies in wait for worldly success. Some scholars compare it with ofermod, the related word the Battle of Maldon poet uses for Byrhtnoth’s fatal decision to let the Vikings cross the causeway unopposed. It is a different poem, but the diagnosis is similar: heroic excess overriding practical judgement.

This is where the idea of corruption earns its place. The poem does not require us to believe that Beowulf has become greedy or malicious. Instead, it asks whether the pursuit of glory has become so deeply embedded in his identity that it shapes his response to danger.

The dragon is therefore not simply an external enemy. It can be read as a symbolic counterpart to the heroic culture itself: a force associated with treasure, the past, destructive power, and the inability of worldly achievement to preserve what matters.

Tolkien’s influential reading of Beowulf emphasised the poem’s literary power and its elegiac quality, particularly its concentration on the hero’s final fate. This approach encourages us to read the dragon episode not merely as an adventure but as the culmination of the poem’s meditation on mortality and heroic greatness.

The Failure of the Heroic Community

Beowulf’s death is not the worst outcome of his final decision. The worst outcome is what it reveals about the society he spent his life protecting.

During the fight with the dragon, his retainers flee. Only Wiglaf stays to help. The comitatus bond that holds the heroic world together, loyalty in return for protection, breaks at the moment it matters most.

A good king should inspire loyalty, and a good warrior should stand beside his lord. Yet the final battle reveals that the community’s loyalty is fragile. Beowulf has been a great protector, but his people may not be capable of sustaining the world he has created.

Wiglaf’s speech after the battle condemns the warriors who abandoned their king. He argues that their cowardice will have consequences: the Geats will lose their protection and face the hostility of neighbouring peoples.

The poem’s conclusion is therefore deeply pessimistic. Beowulf achieves the heroic victory he sought, but his people inherit insecurity and the possibility of destruction.

This is one of the reasons the poem can be interpreted as a critique of heroic culture. A society that depends upon extraordinary individuals may become vulnerable when those individuals die. The hero’s greatness can conceal the weakness of the institutions and relationships around him.

The argument should not be overstated: the poem does not present Beowulf as solely responsible for the collapse of his people. The Geats face external political threats, and the retainers’ failure is their own moral failure. Nevertheless, the final battle exposes the limits of a social order built around the exceptional warrior.

Gilgamesh: A Different Journey Towards Humanity

The Epic of Gilgamesh sharpens the argument by running it in reverse.

Gilgamesh begins with power but lacks the qualities required for good kingship. The people of Uruk suffer under his rule, and the gods create Enkidu as a counterpart to him. Their friendship becomes the central force of Gilgamesh’s transformation.

The early adventures of Gilgamesh and Enkidu are driven by the pursuit of fame. They defeat Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven, but these victories also provoke divine punishment. Enkidu’s death becomes the turning point in Gilgamesh’s life.

The epic then changes its emphasis. The warrior who once sought glory becomes a man terrified of death. His journey towards Utnapishtim is an attempt to escape the human condition itself.

The important point is that Gilgamesh’s transformation is not simply a movement from bad to good. It is a movement from an incomplete understanding of greatness towards a recognition of human limitation.

Scholarship on the development and meaning of the epic has emphasised the role of Enkidu’s death in this transformation. The experience of suffering forces Gilgamesh to confront mortality and eventually accept his place within the human community.

The contrast with Beowulf is revealing:

  • Gilgamesh’s journey begins with a failure of kingship and moves towards a more human understanding of responsibility.
  • Beowulf’s journey begins with heroic excellence and ends by exposing the limits of heroic excellence as a foundation for collective survival.

Both stories explore the relationship between personal greatness and the human condition, but they do so through different narrative trajectories.

Stories as Evolving Reflections of Cultural Values

Both epics were reshaped over time by the people who told, copied and compiled them. That history helps explain what they emphasise.

Beowulf survives in a single manuscript copied around the year 1000, but its story belongs to an older Germanic heroic tradition. A Christian poet is describing a pagan heroic society, and the result pulls two ways. It admires courage, loyalty, generosity and reputation, and it broods on pride, mortality and how little worldly power lasts.

The poem’s interest in treasure and hoarding is more than a literary device. The ship burial at Sutton Hoo in Suffolk belongs roughly to the world the poem imagines, if not to the date of the manuscript. It shows an East Anglian elite burying exactly the kind of wrought gold, weapons and imported luxuries the poem describes being handed out, mourned and buried. The Staffordshire Hoard, a seventh-century deposit of dismantled sword fittings and other war-gear discovered in 2009, offers a further and more unsettling parallel: a mass of martial wealth removed from circulation for reasons still debated by its excavators. Neither find tells us what the poem means, but both confirm that its anxiety about treasure separated from living social use reflects real practices, not only poetic imagination.

Gilgamesh, meanwhile, developed through a long history of Sumerian and Akkadian storytelling. Its surviving versions come from different periods and literary traditions. In the Standard Babylonian edition attributed to the scholar-priest Sîn-lēqi-unninni, the focus moves from heroic exploits to civic legacy. Gilgamesh doesn’t win immortality through eternal life or glory in battle. He wins it through Uruk’s walls and temples, which serve his people long after he is gone.

That reading of the ending is contested. The return to the walls can also be read as consolation rather than triumph: a smaller, sadder kind of permanence than the deathlessness Gilgamesh set out to find. Andrew George’s introduction to his translation treats the closing mood as far more ambivalent than a simple celebration of civic achievement.

The physical evidence for that long development is unusually direct, and more concrete than the literary-historical case for Beowulf can be. Gilgamesh material survives not only from Babylonia itself but from Hattusa in Anatolia, from Emar and Ugarit in Syria, and from Megiddo in the southern Levant, alongside a cluster of earlier Sumerian poems about the same hero (Bilgames and Akka, Bilgames and Huwawa, Bilgames and the Bull of Heaven) that circulated independently before being drawn into the Akkadian epic. The geographic and chronological spread of these finds charts the story’s movement across languages, scribal schools, and centuries in a way that the single surviving Beowulf manuscript cannot.

We should not assume that every feature of either epic was deliberately inserted to teach a particular moral. Nor can we simply equate a surviving poem with the beliefs of an entire society. But the stories’ continued transmission, and in Gilgamesh’s case their physical dispersal across the ancient Near East, suggests that their conflicts remained meaningful well beyond their place of origin.

Perhaps the audiences who preserved these stories recognised something uncomfortable about their own ideals. They admired the warrior’s courage, but feared the consequences of violence. They valued the king’s generosity, but understood the dangers of pride. They celebrated the hero’s greatness, but knew that every hero eventually dies.

The stories become powerful because they do not resolve these tensions neatly.

Conclusion

The journeys of Gilgamesh and Beowulf reveal two different ways in which heroic stories can examine the human condition.

Gilgamesh begins as a powerful ruler whose failure to understand his humanity produces suffering. Through Enkidu’s friendship and death, he learns that greatness cannot overcome mortality and that a meaningful life requires more than power. His ultimate victory is civic and institutional: accepting his limits and building a lasting world for his people, though the poem leaves open how completely that acceptance consoles him.

Beowulf begins as an admirable warrior whose courage and strength genuinely benefit others. Yet his final battle exposes the limitations of a heroic culture that depends upon individual greatness. His death is not proof that he has become evil, but it reveals how the pursuit of honour, reputation, and personal confrontation (the oferhygd Hrothgar warned against) can become destructive when separated from the wider responsibilities of kingship (cyning-dōm).

So the deeper question is what happens when a society’s highest virtues are no longer enough for the world that society has to preserve.

A hero can be brave, generous and loyal and still fail to leave behind a community that can survive without him. Another can begin as an oppressive ruler and slowly learn that his humanity depends on his friendships, his city and his acceptance that he will die.

Each generation that retold these stories got to test its own ideals against them. The poems admire greatness and count its costs in the same breath, and they have never settled which matters more.
