Klumpe-Dumpe and the Tragedy of Grantræet

Andersen gave Denmark's own Humpty Dumpty a happy ending borrowed from the untitled fragment that would become his Klods-Hans tale eleven years later, and The Fir Tree dies of mistaking that borrowed plot for how the world actually works.

TL;DR

Andersen's Fir Tree dies of a category error: it mistakes the borrowed fairy-tale plot of Klumpe-Dumpe, a fall that ends in a throne, for how nature itself actually works.

  • The Klumpe-Dumpe fragment inside Grantræet's manuscript is an early, untitled sketch of the tale Andersen would publish eleven years later as Klods-Hans.
  • Klumpe-Dumpe is Denmark's own Humpty Dumpty figure, but Andersen gives him a happy ending the English rhyme never has.
  • The Fir Tree applies that borrowed fall-then-triumph logic to its own life, which turns out to be linear rather than redemptive.
  • It only recognises its happiest moments after they're gone, spent waiting for a triumph that was never coming.

Hans Christian Andersen’s Grantræet (“The Fir Tree”) was first published on 21 December 1844, alongside “The Snow Queen,” in Nye Eventyr. Første Bind. Anden Samling from the Copenhagen publisher C. A. Reitzel. The tale is usually read as a study in deferred happiness: a fir tree that can never inhabit the present, always straining towards a future that never arrives as promised. What drives that reading home is a miniature story told beneath its own branches: the tale of Klumpe-Dumpe, a figure who falls down a staircase and rises, against all odds, to a throne and a princess.

English translations render Klumpe-Dumpe as “Humpty Dumpty”; H. P. Paull’s widely circulated version calls him “Klumpy-Dumpy.” That choice is exact rather than loose. “Klumpe-Dumpe,” alongside the near-rhyming “Lille Trille,” is the standard Danish name for the Humpty Dumpty figure: the name Danish translations already used for the English nursery rhyme and for Lewis Carroll’s Humpty Dumpty in Through the Looking-Glass (Wikipedia). What Andersen changes is not the name but the plot he grafts onto it: a fall that ends in a throne, not in irreparable ruin.

The Danish Text

In Andersen’s original, the little fat man beneath the tree asks the children which story they want:

“…den om Klumpe-Dumpe, som faldt ned ad Trapperne og kom dog i Høisædet og fik Prindsessen?”

(“…the one about Klumpe-Dumpe, who fell down the stairs and nevertheless reached the high seat and got the princess.”)

That is a world away from the English rhyme, whose fall is catastrophic and final: no king’s horses or king’s men can put Humpty together again. Andersen’s Klumpe-Dumpe, carrying the same name, falls and succeeds.

The word had long been Danish slang for a clumsy, accident-prone person. H.C. Andersen Information says it was used “since time immemorial” for a somewhat clumsy, unlucky figure, with no fixed plot attached. Andersen’s own later letters show him using it of himself, self-deprecatingly, after tripping over a trunk in a hotel room. So the name already meant mishap before he gave it a happy ending.

A Manuscript Shared with Klods-Hans

The connection to Klods-Hans is not only thematic. It is written into the manuscripts. Erik Dal’s critical edition of Andersen’s fairy tales records that the Grantræet manuscript contains a lacuna filled by an inserted narrative: the ending of the story that would become Klods-Hans, marked in the finished, published tale only by the title “Klumpe-Dumpe.” A retrospective on Klods-Hans from Museum Odense confirms the same point independently: a fragment of the tale survives from 1844, sitting in the middle of the poet’s manuscript for Grantræet, written the same year.

Andersen evidently wasn’t satisfied with this early draft. He set it aside for over a decade before reworking it and publishing it as Klods-Hans in 1855. In 1844 it had no title at all. Museum Odense notes that the eventual name came from a working title, “Klokke-Hans,” changed to “Klods-Hans” for its sound rather than its sense. The fragment is best read as an untitled sketch of the later tale: Andersen drafting two stories side by side, with one lending the other its story-within-a-story.

The Klods-Hans pattern, as it appears in the finished 1855 tale, is consistent: a clumsy outsider suffers an apparent humiliation, displays unexpected boldness, and wins the princess. The 1844 Klumpe-Dumpe episode compresses that pattern into a single sentence: fall, reversal, elevation, princess. It anticipates the later tale rather than reproducing it in full; the finished Klods-Hans has a resourceful, norm-defying wit that the brief fragment in Grantræet can only gesture towards. What the Fir Tree hears is the pattern in embryo: enough to recognise its shape, not enough to earn its outcome.

A Story Mistaken for a Law of Nature

The Christmas scene is the tale’s hinge. A little fat man is placed beneath the tree and offers the children a choice between Ivedy-Avedy and Klumpy-Dumpy. They choose the latter, and he tells the story of the fool who tumbles downstairs but nevertheless reaches the throne and marries the princess.

The Fir Tree believes the story is true because the man telling it was so good-looking. It reasons, in effect, that if Klumpy-Dumpy could fall and rise, it too might fall and win a princess. The tree is making a category error. The tree has learned a pattern (misfortune, reversal, triumph) and applies it to its own uncertain future, but nature does not obey fairy-tale logic. The very next morning the tree is dragged out of the drawing room and up into a dark attic. Its fall is not the beginning of glory; it is the beginning of decline.

The Tree’s Real Tragedy

The Fir Tree’s tragedy is its inability to live in the present. As a sapling it longs to grow. Grown, it longs to leave the forest. Indoors, it longs for what happens next. Decorated, it asks what happens after the candles burn out. Klumpe-Dumpe gives that restlessness the perfect story: proof, it seems, that disappointment is only the first stage of triumph. The tree reads its own humiliation as an early beat in a future success story. There is no future success story. There is only an attic.

Fairy-tale logic is a logic of reversal: the insignificant becomes important, the fool becomes wise, the fall becomes the rise. The natural world Grantræet actually depicts is linear: a tree grows, is cut down, is decorated, is discarded, withers, is chopped up, and burns. No reversal is built into it. The Fir Tree’s error isn’t vanity; it’s misreading which kind of story it’s in. Klumpe-Dumpe falls because the borrowed plot requires him to, and the fall carries meaning because the plot has already fixed a destination: the princess. The Fir Tree has no such guarantee. It has only a name, and a story it heard once from a good-looking man.

The Irony, and the Realisation That Comes Too Late

In the attic, the tree tells the Klumpe-Dumpe story to the mice, who love it, and more of them come the next night. Then two rats arrive, say it isn’t much of a story, and leave. The mice like it less after that, and eventually stop coming. The tale becomes a private mythology with no one left to hear it, and the tree goes on waiting for its own fairy-tale ending. Andersen’s irony runs the other way: perhaps a fall is simply a fall.

When spring comes, the tree is carried outside, branches yellowed and withered. A child tears off the golden star and calls it an ugly old Christmas tree. Only then, looking at the garden’s fresh flowers and remembering the forest, Christmas Eve, and the mice who listened to its one story, does the tree reach the realisation that its happiest time had already passed, unnoticed while it was happening. That is the inversion of the Klumpe-Dumpe pattern: his misfortune leads to triumph; the tree’s longing for triumph is exactly what stops it from recognising the happiness it already had.

As the tree is chopped into pieces and burned, it recalls three things: the forest, Christmas Eve, and the Klumpe-Dumpe story, the only tale it ever learned to tell. The golden star survives, worn now by a child; the tree does not. Every tale, Andersen closes, must end at last.

Conclusion

Klumpe-Dumpe in Grantræet is not a departure from the Danish Humpty Dumpty figure but a deliberate rewriting of him: the same name Danish readers already used for their fall-and-ruin nursery character, fitted with an ending borrowed from the Klods-Hans story Andersen happened to be drafting at the same time. The manuscript evidence bears this out directly: a fragment of that later tale is physically preserved inside the Grantræet manuscript, though it wouldn’t acquire the title “Klods-Hans,” or its full narrative shape, for another eleven years.

Klumpe-Dumpe embodies the seductive promise of fairy-tale logic: a fall can become an ascent, humiliation can become honour, the unlikely fool can win the princess. The Fir Tree believes this literally, and nature does not work like a fairy tale. A fall does not necessarily precede a rise; suffering does not necessarily prepare triumph; happiness does not always announce itself while it’s happening. By the time the tree understands that its happiest moments were already behind it, those moments are gone for good.

The fool falls and gets the princess. The tree falls and gets the fire.
